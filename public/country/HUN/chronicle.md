Founding: The Magyar conquest and the crowning of Saint Stephen — c. 895 / 1000. Hungary traces its origin to the Honfoglalás, the 'land-taking' of around 895-896, when seven Magyar tribes led by the chieftain Árpád crossed the Carpathian passes and settled the Carpathian Basin, founding the dynasty that would rule for four centuries. The Christian kingdom itself dates to the turn of the millennium: Árpád's descendant Stephen, baptised a Christian, was crowned the first king of Hungary around 1000-1001, traditionally with a crown sent by Pope Sylvester II. Stephen organised his realm on Western models, founded bishoprics and abbeys, and bound Hungary to Latin Christendom, for which he was later canonised as Saint Stephen. The kingdom he founded endured until it was destroyed at the Battle of Mohács in 1526.

Hungary's history begins with the Magyar conquest of the Carpathian Basin around 895-896, when seven nomadic tribes under the chieftain Árpád settled the Danube plain. A century later his great-great-grandson Stephen accepted Christianity and was crowned around the turn of the millennium, founding a Christian kingdom that endured for half a thousand years. That medieval kingdom survived the Mongol catastrophe of 1241 but was shattered at the Battle of Mohács in 1526, after which Hungary was split for nearly two centuries among the Ottomans, the Habsburgs and an autonomous Transylvania. Reunited under Habsburg rule, Hungary rose again in the revolution of 1848-49 and won near-parity in the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867. The First World War brought the Treaty of Trianon of 1920, the defining national trauma, which stripped Hungary of about two-thirds of its territory and population and left some three million ethnic Hungarians beyond the new borders. Drawn into alliance with Nazi Germany, occupied and host to the mass murder of its Jews in 1944, Hungary then spent four decades under communism, rose against Soviet rule in 1956, and finally helped pull down the Iron Curtain in 1989 before joining NATO in 1999 and the European Union in 2004.

## The Magyar Conquest and the Christian Kingdom — c. 895 – 1241

Seven nomadic tribes seized the Carpathian Basin, and within a century their leaders turned a steppe confederation into a Christian kingdom of Latin Europe.

- c. 895–896: **The Magyar conquest (Honfoglalás)** — Seven Magyar tribes under the chieftain Árpád crossed the Carpathians and seized the Carpathian Basin, founding the Árpád dynasty and the settlement that gave rise to the Hungarian state. [arpad-britannica, arpad-dynasty-britannica]
- 996: **Pannonhalma Abbey founded** — Benedictine monks settled at Pannonhalma, founding Hungary's first Christian monastery; it would help convert the Magyars, found the country's first school and, in 1055, produce the first document written in Hungarian. [pannonhalma-unesco]
- c. 1000–1001: **Coronation of Stephen I** — Stephen, of the Árpád line, was crowned the first king of Hungary, traditionally with a crown from Pope Sylvester II, founding a Christian kingdom organised on Western models. He was later canonised as Saint Stephen. [stephen-britannica, crown-museum]
- 11 Apr 1241: **Mongol invasion and the Battle of Mohi** — The Mongols under Batu Khan destroyed King Béla IV's army at the Sajó River and ravaged Hungary for a year, leaving much of the kingdom in ruins before withdrawing in 1242. [mongol-britannica, bela-iv-britannica]

Figures:

- Árpád (d. c. 907) — Chieftain of the Magyar tribes. Chosen to lead the seven Hungarian tribes westward, Árpád led the conquest of the Carpathian Basin around 895-896 and founded the Árpád dynasty, which ruled Hungary until 1301.
- Stephen I (Saint Stephen) (c. 975 – 1038) — First king of Hungary. Crowned around 1000-1001, Stephen converted his realm to Latin Christianity, founded its church and administration and won recognition for Hungary as a Christian kingdom. Canonised in 1083, he remains the country's patron and founding saint.

## Mohács and the Three-Part Kingdom — 1301 – 1699

A catastrophic defeat by the Ottomans in 1526 destroyed the medieval kingdom and split Hungary three ways for nearly two centuries.

- 1456: **Siege of Belgrade** — Hungarian forces under John Hunyadi broke an Ottoman siege of Belgrade, checking the Turkish advance into central Europe for decades and becoming a celebrated victory of the medieval kingdom. [mohacs-britannica]
- 29 Aug 1526: **Battle of Mohács** — Süleyman the Magnificent destroyed the Hungarian army and killed King Louis II, ending the medieval kingdom and opening Hungary to Ottoman and Habsburg domination. [mohacs-britannica]
- 1541: **Ottoman capture of Buda and the three-part division** — The Ottomans seized Buda and Hungary fractured into Royal Hungary under the Habsburgs, Ottoman Hungary ruled from Buda, and the autonomous Principality of Transylvania. [mohacs-britannica]
- 1686: **Reconquest of Buda** — A Habsburg-led Christian coalition retook Buda from the Ottomans, part of the campaign that drove the Turks from Hungary after more than a century. [mohacs-britannica]
- 1699: **Treaty of Karlowitz** — The Ottomans ceded almost all of Hungary to the Habsburgs, reuniting the country but binding it firmly within the Habsburg monarchy. [mohacs-britannica]

Figures:

- Louis II (1506 – 1526) — Last Jagiellonian king of Hungary. King of Hungary and Bohemia, Louis led the Christian army at Mohács and died in flight after its destruction at the age of twenty, his death marking the collapse of the independent medieval kingdom.
- Süleyman the Magnificent (1494 – 1566) — Ottoman sultan. The Ottoman sultan whose victory at Mohács in 1526 broke Hungary; his armies took Buda in 1541 and made central Hungary a province of the empire for nearly a century and a half.

## Habsburg Rule, Revolution and the Dual Monarchy — 1699 – 1914

Under the Habsburgs, Hungary's national awakening exploded in the revolution of 1848 and, after its defeat, won near-equality in the Compromise of 1867.

- 15 Mar 1848: **The Revolution of 1848** — A bloodless rising in Pest, with Sándor Petőfi among its voices, won the March Laws abolishing censorship and serfdom and establishing a responsible Hungarian government against Habsburg absolutism. [revolution-1848-britannica]
- Aug 1849: **Defeat of the war of independence** — After Hungary proclaimed independence under Kossuth, the revolution was crushed by a combined Austrian and Russian army; the surrender brought executions and Kossuth's exile. [revolution-1848-britannica]
- 1867: **Austro-Hungarian Compromise (Ausgleich)** — The Ausgleich transformed the empire into the Dual Monarchy, granting Hungary full internal autonomy with its own parliament while keeping defence and foreign affairs common to Austria-Hungary. [ausgleich-britannica, dual-monarchy-britannica]
- 1873: **The founding of Budapest** — Buda, Óbuda and Pest were unified into a single capital that grew into one of Europe's great cities; its historic core along the Danube is today a UNESCO World Heritage site. [budapest-unesco]

Figures:

- Lajos Kossuth (1802 – 1894) — Leader of the 1848-49 revolution. Journalist and orator who became the driving force of the 1848 revolution and, in 1849, leader of an independent Hungary. After its defeat he spent the rest of his long life in exile, denouncing Habsburg rule across Europe and America.
- Sándor Petőfi (1823 – 1849) — Poet of the revolution. Hungary's national poet, whose verse gave voice to the rising of 15 March 1848. He vanished in battle in 1849, becoming an enduring symbol of the revolution and of Hungarian liberty.

## Trianon and the Shrinking of Hungary — 1914 – 1939

Defeat in the First World War brought the Treaty of Trianon, which stripped Hungary of two-thirds of its land and left millions of Magyars beyond the new borders.

- 1918: **Collapse of Austria-Hungary** — Defeat in the First World War destroyed the Dual Monarchy; amid revolution Hungary passed through a democratic republic and a brief communist regime before the conservative order of Regent Horthy. [trianon-britannica, dual-monarchy-britannica]
- 4 Jun 1920: **The Treaty of Trianon** — Hungary lost about two-thirds of its territory and population, leaving roughly three million ethnic Hungarians outside the new borders in Romania, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and elsewhere; the trauma shaped Hungarian politics for a century. [trianon-britannica]
- 2 Nov 1938: **First Vienna Award** — Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy awarded Hungary parts of southern Slovakia from Czechoslovakia, the first of the revisionist gains that drew Hungary toward the Axis. [trianon-britannica]
- 30 Aug 1940: **Second Vienna Award** — Germany and Italy awarded Hungary northern Transylvania from Romania, deepening Hungary's dependence on the Axis powers in its quest to reverse Trianon. [trianon-britannica]

Figures:

- Miklós Horthy (1868 – 1957) — Regent of Hungary. Former admiral who became regent in 1920 and ruled interwar Hungary as a 'kingdom without a king'. His pursuit of treaty revision allied Hungary with Nazi Germany, a course that ended in occupation and catastrophe.
- Count István Bethlen (1874 – 1946) — Prime minister. Dominant statesman of the 1920s who stabilised Hungary after the upheavals of 1918-20 while making the revision of Trianon the guiding aim of national policy.

## The Second World War and the Holocaust in Hungary — 1939 – 1945

Allied to Germany in pursuit of the lost lands, Hungary was drawn into war, occupied in 1944, and made the scene of the mass murder of its Jews.

- 1941: **Hungary enters the war as an Axis ally** — Seeking to keep and extend the lands restored by the Vienna Awards, Hungary joined Germany's war and took part in the invasion of the Soviet Union. [holocaust-ushmm]
- 19 Mar 1944: **German occupation of Hungary** — German forces occupied Hungary to keep it in the war and to deport its Jews, then the largest surviving Jewish community in Europe, numbering some 760,000 to 780,000 people. [holocaust-ushmm]
- May–Jul 1944: **Deportation of the Hungarian Jews** — Between 15 May and 9 July 1944 about 437,000 Jews were deported, mostly to Auschwitz-Birkenau, where some three-quarters were murdered on arrival; Horthy halted the trains on 7 July. [holocaust-ushmm]
- Oct 1944: **The Arrow Cross seizure of power** — After Horthy's attempt to leave the war, Germany installed the fascist Arrow Cross, which murdered thousands more Jews in Budapest before the Soviet conquest of the city. [holocaust-ushmm]
- Feb 1945: **The siege of Budapest** — The months-long battle for the capital between German-Hungarian and Soviet forces destroyed much of historic Budapest, including the castle and the Danube bridges, as the Red Army completed its conquest of Hungary. [budapest-unesco, holocaust-ushmm]

Figures:

- Raoul Wallenberg (1912 – disappeared 1945) — Swedish diplomat and rescuer. A Swedish diplomat in Budapest who issued protective passes and sheltered tens of thousands of Jews during the Arrow Cross terror of 1944. Detained by Soviet forces in 1945, he vanished into the Soviet prison system.
- Ferenc Szálasi (1897 – 1946) — Leader of the Arrow Cross. Head of the fascist Arrow Cross movement installed in power by Germany in October 1944. His regime murdered thousands of Budapest's Jews; he was tried and executed for war crimes after the war.

## Communism, 1956 and the Return to Europe — 1945 – present

Four decades of communist rule, defied in the 1956 revolution and softened by 'goulash communism', ended in 1989 when Hungary helped open the Iron Curtain.

- 1949: **The Hungarian People's Republic** — Backed by the Soviet Union, the communists seized total power and proclaimed a one-party People's Republic, imposing Stalinist terror, show trials and forced collectivisation under Mátyás Rákosi. [kadar-britannica, revolution-1956-britannica]
- 23 Oct – 4 Nov 1956: **The Hungarian Revolution of 1956** — A nationwide rising under premier Imre Nagy declared Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact; the Soviet army crushed it on 4 November, killing some 2,500 Hungarians and driving about 200,000 into exile. [revolution-1956-britannica]
- 1968: **'Goulash communism' and the New Economic Mechanism** — János Kádár's reforms admitted limited market forces and private enterprise, giving Hungarians a relative prosperity and freedom unusual in the Soviet bloc. [kadar-britannica]
- 19 Aug 1989: **The Pan-European Picnic and the opening of the border** — A border gate opened near Sopron let some 700 East Germans flee west; Hungary's subsequent full opening of its frontier breached the Iron Curtain and helped bring down the Berlin Wall. [picnic-gov]
- 23 Oct 1989: **Proclamation of the republic** — On the anniversary of the 1956 rising Hungary proclaimed itself a free republic, ending four decades of communist rule and beginning its transition to democracy. [kadar-britannica, revolution-1956-britannica]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to NATO and the European Union** — Having joined NATO on 12 March 1999, Hungary acceded to the European Union on 1 May 2004 in the bloc's largest single enlargement, completing its return to the West. [nato-gov, eu-gov]

Figures:

- Imre Nagy (1896 – 1958) — Reform communist premier. Premier during the 1956 revolution, Nagy backed a multi-party government and declared Hungary's neutrality and exit from the Warsaw Pact. After the Soviet crushing of the rising he was captured and, in 1958, executed; he was reburied with honours in 1989.
- János Kádár (1912 – 1989) — Communist leader of Hungary. Installed by the Soviets in 1956 and leader until 1988, Kádár presided over the reprisals after the revolution but then built the relatively liberal 'goulash communism' that made Hungary the most relaxed state in the Soviet bloc.
