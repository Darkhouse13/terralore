# The Chronicle of Chad: From Toumaï and the Sahel empires to the heart of Africa

Founding: Independence from France — 11 August 1960. The Republic of Chad attained full independence from France on 11 August 1960, with the southern trade-union leader François (later Ngarta) Tombalbaye as its first president.

Chad straddles the Sahara, the Sahel and the wooded south, with Lake Chad as its ancient pivot. Its basin yielded Toumaï, a seven-million-year-old hominin, and nurtured the Sao culture and the great trans-Saharan empires of Kanem and Kanem-Bornu alongside the eastern sultanates of Baguirmi and Wadai. Conquered piecemeal by France, it became independent in 1960 and endured decades of civil war, conflict with Libya, and authoritarian rule under Hissène Habré and the Déby dynasty.

## Toumaï and the Sao — c. 7 million BP – 16th century CE

A hominin skull in the Djurab desert and a lost lake-basin culture set Chad's deepest record.

- 19 July 2001: **Discovery of Toumaï** — A team led by Michel Brunet recovered the cranium of Sahelanthropus tchadensis at the Toros-Menalla (TM 266) site in the Djurab desert; the species was announced in 2002 and dated to about seven million years. [nature-sahelanthropus, australian-museum-sahelanthropus, smithsonian-toumai]
- from c. 6th century BCE: **Rise of the Sao culture** — The Sao settled the lower Chari and southern Lake Chad, producing celebrated terracotta and bronze figures across more than 350 known sites before being absorbed by later states. [wiki-sao, ancient-origins-sao]

## Kanem and Kanem-Bornu — c. 700 – 1846

A thousand-year empire on the trans-Saharan trade routes, anchored by Lake Chad and the Sayfawa dynasty.

- c. 1075: **Hummay founds the Sayfawa dynasty** — Mai Hummay, the first Muslim ruler of Kanem, displaced the Duguwa and founded the Sayfawa (Sefuwa) dynasty, which would rule the empire for some seven centuries. [wiki-kanem-bornu, wiki-hummay]
- c. 1210–1248: **Zenith under Dunama Dibalemi** — Mai Dunama Dibalemi brought Kanem to its peak, projecting power from the Fezzan to the southern savanna and deepening trade and diplomatic ties with North Africa and Egypt. [wiki-kanem-bornu, ebsco-dunama]
- c. 1380s: **Sayfawa flee Kanem for Bornu** — Pressed by the Bulala, the Sayfawa abandoned Njimi and re-established the state in Bornu, west of Lake Chad, later founding the capital Ngazargamu. [wiki-kanem-bornu]
- late 16th c.: **Golden age under Idris Alooma** — Mai Idris Alooma modernised the army with Ottoman firearms and advisers and promoted Islamic law, leading Kanem-Bornu to its second peak. [wiki-kanem-bornu, lumen-bornu]
- 1846: **End of the Sayfawa line** — The traditional office of mai was abolished in 1846 after the al-Kanemi shehus, who had risen during the Fulani jihad, took full control of Bornu. [wiki-kanem-bornu]

Figures:

- Idris Alooma (reigned c. 1564–1596) — Mai (king) of Kanem-Bornu. Reforming warrior-king who reorganised the army with imported firearms and Ottoman military advisers and enforced Islamic law, presiding over Kanem-Bornu's late-16th-century golden age.

## Baguirmi, Wadai and the Saharan trade — c. 1500 – 1900

East of Lake Chad, rival Muslim sultanates grew rich on caravans of salt, kola and captives.

- c. 1635: **Founding of the Wadai Sultanate** — Abd al-Karim led an Islamic revolt that overthrew the Tunjur and established the Wadai (Ouaddaï) sultanate east of Lake Chad, ruled from Abéché. [wiki-wadai, britannica-wadai]
- c. 1804–1815: **Wadai's commercial expansion** — Under Muhammad Sabun, Wadai expanded and prospered by controlling caravan routes from Abéché north to Benghazi and the east-west trade between Darfur and Bornu. [britannica-wadai, wiki-wadai]
- 16th–19th c.: **Baguirmi and the slave trade** — The sultanate of Baguirmi, Islamic from the sixteenth century, supplied large numbers of captives to the trans-Saharan slave trade alongside skins, ivory and cotton. [wiki-bagirmi]

## Rabih's empire and French conquest — 1879 – 1960

A Sudanese warlord overran the basin, then fell to France, which folded Chad into Equatorial Africa.

- 1892–1894: **Rabih conquers Bornu** — Rabih az-Zubayr overran the Kanem-Bornu lands with one of the best-equipped armies in sub-Saharan Africa, ruling the basin as a military dictatorship. [wiki-rabih]
- 22 April 1900: **Battle of Kousséri** — Converging French columns defeated and killed Rabih near the Chari River; the French commander Major Lamy also fell, ending Rabih's empire and opening the basin to France. [wiki-kousseri, wiki-rabih]
- 1910: **Chad joins French Equatorial Africa** — France organised the conquered territory and incorporated Chad into the federation of French Equatorial Africa, governing the south far more closely than the Muslim north. [britannica-chad-independence, wiki-french-chad]
- Nov 1958: **Autonomous republic proclaimed** — Chad became an autonomous republic within the French Community, a step toward the full independence achieved in 1960. [britannica-chad-independence]

Figures:

- Rabih az-Zubayr (c. 1842–1900) — Sudanese warlord and conqueror of Bornu. Built a formidable private army and conquered the Lake Chad basin in the 1890s before being defeated and killed by the French at Kousséri in 1900.

## Independence and civil war — 1960 – 1990

Sovereignty in 1960 gave way to north–south insurgency, coups, Libyan intervention and the rise of Habré.

- 11 August 1960: **Independence under Tombalbaye** — Chad gained full independence from France, with François Tombalbaye as first president; his southern-centred, authoritarian rule soon stoked northern grievances. [britannica-chad-independence, wiki-tombalbaye]
- 1966: **FROLINAT and the civil war** — The National Liberation Front of Chad (FROLINAT) launched an insurgency rooted in northern and Muslim discontent, beginning decades of civil war. [ebsco-civil-war, wiki-tombalbaye]
- 13 April 1975: **Coup kills Tombalbaye** — Tombalbaye was assassinated in a military coup; General Félix Malloum took power but could not end the conflict with FROLINAT's factions. [ebsco-civil-war, wiki-malloum]
- June 1982: **Habré seizes power** — Hissène Habré captured N'Djamena and made himself president, establishing a repressive regime amid continuing war and Libyan intervention. [britannica-chad-independence, factbook-chad]
- 1987: **The Toyota War with Libya** — Chadian forces under Hassan Djamous won a string of victories at Fada, Bir Kora and Ouadi Doum, driving the Libyan army back toward the disputed Aouzou Strip. [history-defined-toyota-war, icj-aouzou]

Figures:

- François (Ngarta) Tombalbaye (1918–1975) — First President of Chad. Southern trade-union leader who led Chad to independence in 1960; his authoritarian, southern-dominated rule provoked the northern insurgency, and he was killed in the 1975 coup.

## The Déby era and its legacy — 1990 – present

Three decades of Déby rule, an oil economy, a landmark war-crimes verdict, and a dynastic succession.

- Dec 1990: **Idriss Déby seizes power** — Leading the Patriotic Salvation Movement (MPS) from Sudan, Déby overthrew Habré and installed himself as president, beginning a thirty-year rule. [britannica-idriss-deby, wiki-mps]
- 3 February 1994: **ICJ awards Aouzou Strip to Chad** — The International Court of Justice ruled that the disputed Aouzou Strip belonged to Chad; Libyan forces withdrew by the end of May 1994. [icj-aouzou]
- 30 May 2016: **Habré convicted of crimes against humanity** — The Extraordinary African Chambers in Senegal sentenced Hissène Habré to life for crimes against humanity, war crimes and torture, a landmark of universal jurisdiction; the verdict was upheld on appeal in 2017. [hrw-habre, fidh-habre]
- April 2021: **Death of Idriss Déby; son takes power** — Déby died of wounds from fighting on the front line; a military council named his son, General Mahamat Idriss Déby, transitional president, widely seen as an unconstitutional succession. [france24-mahamat-deby, britannica-mahamat-deby]

Figures:

- Idriss Déby Itno (1952–2021) — President of Chad (1990–2021). Seized power in 1990 and ruled for three decades as a key Western military ally in the Sahel; killed in 2021 during a rebel offensive, after which his son succeeded him.
- Hissène Habré (1942–2021) — President of Chad (1982–1990). Wartime leader who defeated Libya in 1987 but ran a regime blamed for tens of thousands of deaths; convicted of crimes against humanity in Senegal in 2016.
