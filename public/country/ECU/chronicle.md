Founding: Republic of Ecuador separates from Gran Colombia — 13 May 1830. On 13 May 1830 an assembly of notables in Quito resolved that the Distrito del Sur, the southern district of Simon Bolivar's Gran Colombia, would separate and form an independent state, naming the Venezuelan-born general Juan Jose Flores supreme civil and military chief. A constituent assembly meeting at Riobamba from August 1830 drafted the first constitution of the Republic of the Equator (Republica del Ecuador) and confirmed Flores as the new nation's first president. The republic inherited the territory and identity of the colonial Real Audiencia of Quito, whose independence had been won at the Battle of Pichincha in 1822, and took its name from the Equator that crosses the country near its capital.

Ecuador's history reaches back more than five thousand years to the Valdivia culture of its Pacific coast, whose fired-clay figurines are among the oldest ceramics known in the Americas. In the highlands the Quitu and Cara peoples built the chiefdoms around Quito that the Inca, advancing from the south under Topa Inca Yupanqui and Huayna Capac, absorbed in the late fifteenth century, making Quito a northern seat of empire and the base of the last Inca ruler, Atahualpa. Spanish conquest followed in the 1530s, and the colonial Real Audiencia of Quito became famous for the Quito school of religious art before Quito's criollos raised the first cry of independence on 10 August 1809, earning the city the title Luz de America. Antonio Jose de Sucre secured that independence at the Battle of Pichincha on 24 May 1822; after eight years inside Simon Bolivar's Gran Colombia, Ecuador emerged as a separate republic in 1830, and through cacao, banana and oil booms, chronic political instability, the dollarisation of 2000 and a recent security crisis it has made its way to the present.

## Valdivia Potters and the Chiefdoms of the Andes — c. 3500 BCE - c. 1470 CE

For thousands of years before the Inca, the land that would become Ecuador held some of the Americas' oldest ceramic traditions on the coast and a patchwork of farming chiefdoms in the Andean valleys, among them the Quitu and Cara around the future site of Quito.

- c. 3500 BCE: **The Valdivia ceramic culture** — On the Pacific coast the Valdivia people make fired-clay vessels and figurines among the oldest pottery in the Americas, evidence of early settled, farming village life. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- early 2nd millennium CE: **Quitu, Cara and Cañari chiefdoms** — Highland peoples including the Quitu and Cara around Quito and the Cañari near Cuenca build farming chiefdoms linked by trade and rivalry. [britannica-ecuador-history]

## The Inca Conquest and the Last Emperor — c. 1470 - 1533

In the late fifteenth century the Inca pushed north from Peru and absorbed the highland chiefdoms after fierce resistance, making Quito a northern capital of their empire; it was from here that Atahualpa fought the civil war that left him master of the Inca world on the eve of the Spanish arrival.

- c. 1471-1493: **Inca advance into the northern Andes** — Topa Inca Yupanqui begins the conquest of the Ecuadorian highlands, met by fierce resistance from the Cañari and the peoples around Quito. [britannica-ecuador-history, britannica-atahualpa]
- c. 1493-1525: **Huayna Capac completes the conquest** — Huayna Capac extends Inca rule over all of highland Ecuador and makes Quito a northern capital of the empire. [britannica-ecuador-history, britannica-atahualpa]
- early 1532: **Atahualpa defeats Huascar** — Atahualpa's northern armies crush those of his half-brother Huascar near Cuzco, leaving him master of the Inca empire as the Spanish arrive. [britannica-atahualpa]
- 1533 CE: **Capture and execution of Atahualpa** — Seized by Pizarro at Cajamarca in November 1532 and ransomed, the last Inca emperor is tried and executed in 1533, shattering Inca authority. [britannica-atahualpa, britannica-benalcazar]

Figures:

- Atahualpa (c. 1502-1533) — Last Inca emperor. Son of Huayna Capac, associated with Quito and the northern armies, who won the Inca civil war against his half-brother Huascar only to be captured, ransomed and executed by Francisco Pizarro, ending the independent Inca state.

## Conquest, Colony and the Quito School — 1534 - 1808

Spaniards under Sebastian de Benalcazar founded a Spanish Quito in 1534; the Real Audiencia of Quito, established in 1563, governed the region for nearly three centuries and gave rise to the Quito school, one of the great workshops of religious art in colonial America.

- 6 Dec 1534: **Spanish founding of Quito** — Sebastian de Benalcazar founds San Francisco de Quito on the ruins of the Inca city burned by Rumiñahui, establishing Spanish rule over the highlands. [britannica-benalcazar]
- 1563 CE: **Real Audiencia of Quito created** — Philip II establishes the Audiencia of Quito, a royal court and administrative district that gives the region its lasting colonial identity. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- 17th-18th c.: **The Quito school of art** — Quito's convents produce the Escuela Quiteña, a mestizo Baroque tradition of painting and sculpture exported across colonial South America. [encyclopedia-quito-school, unesco-quito]
- 1792 CE: **Espejo founds Quito's first newspaper** — The physician and writer Eugenio Espejo, precursor of independence, launches Quito's first newspaper and presses Enlightenment ideas. [encyclopedia-espejo]

Figures:

- Sebastian de Benalcazar (c. 1480-1551) — Spanish conquistador. Lieutenant of Pizarro who conquered the Quito region and founded the Spanish city of Quito in 1534 before going on to campaign in the lands that became Colombia.
- Eugenio Espejo (1747-1795) — Physician, writer, precursor of independence. Quito's leading Enlightenment intellect, of Indigenous and mixed ancestry, who criticised colonial society, founded the city's first newspaper and inspired the patriots of 1809; honoured as the precursor of Ecuadorian independence and the father of its journalism.

## The Light of America: Independence and the Republic — 1809 - 1845

Quito raised the first cry of independence in 1809, earning the title Luz de America; after years of struggle Antonio Jose de Sucre won the decisive Battle of Pichincha in 1822, joined the region to Gran Colombia, and in 1830 Ecuador separated to become a republic of its own.

- 10 Aug 1809: **The first cry of independence** — Quito criollos depose the colonial authorities and form a governing junta, one of the earliest such acts in Spanish America, earning Quito the title Luz de America. [britannica-ecuador-history, encyclopedia-espejo]
- 24 May 1822: **Battle of Pichincha** — Antonio Jose de Sucre defeats the royalist army on the slopes of Pichincha above Quito, securing the independence of the lands of the old Audiencia of Quito. [britannica-pichincha]
- 1822-1830: **Union with Gran Colombia** — Liberated Quito joins Simon Bolivar's Republic of Gran Colombia alongside Venezuela and New Granada until the union breaks apart. [britannica-grancolombia, britannica-bolivar]
- 13 May 1830: **Republic of Ecuador founded** — An assembly in Quito separates the southern district from Gran Colombia and founds the Republic of Ecuador, with Juan Jose Flores as first president. [britannica-ecuador-history, britannica-grancolombia]

Figures:

- Antonio Jose de Sucre (1795-1830) — General, victor of Pichincha. Bolivar's most trusted lieutenant, who liberated Quito at the Battle of Pichincha in 1822 and went on to win independence for Peru and Bolivia; widely regarded as one of the noblest soldiers of the South American revolution.
- Juan Jose Flores (1800-1864) — First president of Ecuador. Venezuelan-born general and ally of Bolivar who led Ecuador's separation from Gran Colombia in 1830 and became the republic's first president, dominating its early politics.

## Cacao, Liberals and Bananas — 1845 - 1960

From the mid-nineteenth century Ecuador rode the export booms of cacao and then bananas, swung between the Catholic conservatism of Gabriel Garcia Moreno and the anticlerical Liberal Revolution of Eloy Alfaro in 1895, and saw Charles Darwin's Galapagos pass into legend and, later, protection.

- 1835 CE: **Darwin reaches the Galapagos** — The naturalist Charles Darwin visits the Galapagos aboard HMS Beagle, gathering observations that help shape his theory of evolution by natural selection. [galapagos-conservancy-history, darwin-foundation]
- c. 1880-1915: **The cacao boom** — Ecuador becomes the world's leading cacao exporter, enriching the coastal elite of Guayaquil and shifting wealth and power toward the coast. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- 1895 CE: **The Liberal Revolution of Eloy Alfaro** — Eloy Alfaro seizes power and launches liberal reforms separating church and state, founding secular schools and building the Guayaquil-Quito railway. [britannica-ecuador-history, loc-ecuador]
- 1950s: **The banana boom** — Ecuador becomes the world's largest banana exporter, the fruit replacing cacao as the leading crop and renewing coastal prosperity. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- 1959 CE: **Galapagos National Park** — Ecuador declares the Galapagos Islands a national park, the basis of their later inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage site. [galapagos-conservancy-history, unesco-galapagos]

Figures:

- Eloy Alfaro (1842-1912) — Liberal revolutionary and president. Coastal radical who led the Liberal Revolution of 1895, separated church and state, founded secular education and built the Guayaquil-Quito railway before being killed by a mob in Quito in 1912.
- Gabriel Garcia Moreno (1821-1875) — Conservative president. Dominant conservative leader of the 1860s and early 1870s who bound the Ecuadorian state closely to the Catholic Church and modernised the country before his assassination in 1875.

## Oil, Dollar and the Modern Crisis — 1960 - present

Amazonian oil from 1972 transformed the economy, but chronic political instability, financial collapse and the dollarisation of 2000 marked the decades that followed; after the long Correa years the country has confronted, since 2024, a grave crisis of organised crime and violence.

- 1972 CE: **The oil era begins** — Ecuador begins exporting Amazonian oil, transforming its economy and government and launching nearly a decade of rapid growth. [britannica-ecuador-history, worldbank-ecuador]
- 1979 CE: **Return to civilian rule** — After years of military government Ecuador returns to elected civilian democracy, opening an era of fragile and often interrupted constitutional rule. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- Jan 2000: **Dollarisation** — Amid financial collapse President Jamil Mahuad adopts the US dollar as Ecuador's currency, abandoning the sucre; he is ousted days later but the dollar stays. [britannica-ecuador-history, worldbank-ecuador]
- 2007-2017: **The Correa decade** — Rafael Correa governs for ten years of his Citizens' Revolution, using oil revenue to expand the state and rewrite the constitution. [britannica-ecuador-history]
- Jan 2024: **Internal armed conflict declared** — President Daniel Noboa declares an internal armed conflict and deploys the military against drug-trafficking gangs after a gang leader's prison escape and televised attacks. [usembassy-ecuador-emergency]

Figures:

- Rafael Correa (born 1963) — President, 2007-2017. Economist and populist leader whose decade-long Citizens' Revolution used oil wealth to expand the state and a new 2008 constitution to reshape Ecuadorian politics.
- Jamil Mahuad (born 1949) — President who dollarised the economy. President during the financial collapse of 1999 who in January 2000 replaced the sucre with the US dollar; he was driven from office within days, but dollarisation endured.
