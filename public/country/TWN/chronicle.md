# The Chronicle of Taiwan: An Austronesian island colonised in turn by Dutch, Spanish, Ming-loyalist, Qing and Japanese hands, and now the seat of a self-governing polity at the centre of an unresolved cross-strait dispute.

Founding: Relocation of the ROC central government to Taiwan — December 1949. The polity that governs Taiwan today, the Republic of China, originated on the mainland in 1912; its central government relocated to Taipei in December 1949 after the Nationalists (Kuomintang) were defeated by the Communists in the Chinese Civil War, the same year the People's Republic of China was proclaimed in Beijing. This relocation is the founding moment of the present self-governing polity on the island; it was not a declaration of independence, and the de jure status of Taiwan remains internationally contested and unresolved, with the PRC claiming the island as a province it has never administered.

Taiwan is a mountainous island off the southeastern coast of the Asian mainland, first home to Austronesian-speaking peoples whose language family is generally traced to the island itself. From the seventeenth century it passed through Dutch and Spanish trading outposts, the Ming-loyalist kingdom founded by Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), more than two centuries of Qing administration, and fifty years of Japanese colonial rule after the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki. Republic of China (ROC) forces took control in 1945, and the ROC central government relocated to the island in December 1949 after its defeat in the Chinese Civil War, since when it has governed Taiwan while the People's Republic of China (PRC), founded that same year, claims the island as a province but has never controlled it. After decades of martial law, an export-led economic miracle and democratisation produced Taiwan's first direct presidential election in 1996; its de jure international status remains contested and unresolved.

## Austronesian Island and the European Outposts — Prehistory - 1662

Long inhabited by Austronesian-speaking peoples, Taiwan entered the records of global trade in the seventeenth century as Dutch and Spanish companies planted fortified outposts on its coasts.

- 1624: **Dutch found Fort Zeelandia** — The Dutch East India Company establishes a fortified trading base, Fort Zeelandia, on the southwestern coast near present-day Tainan, beginning roughly four decades of Dutch commercial rule over parts of the island. [britannica-taiwan, loh-history]
- 1626: **Spanish settle the northern coast** — Spanish forces from the Philippines occupy northern Taiwan, building forts at Keelung and Tamsui in response to the Dutch presence in the south. [britannica-taiwan]
- 1642: **Dutch expel the Spanish** — A Dutch expedition drives the Spanish from their northern outposts, leaving the Dutch East India Company as the dominant European power on the island. [britannica-taiwan]
- February 1662: **Koxinga expels the Dutch** — After a siege begun in 1661, the Ming-loyalist commander Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) forces the surrender of Fort Zeelandia, ending Dutch rule and founding the Zheng (Tungning) regime on Taiwan. [britannica-koxinga]

Figures:

- Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) (1624-1662) — Ming-loyalist commander; founder of the Zheng regime on Taiwan. A loyal supporter of the last Ming claimant after the Manchu Qing conquest, he drove the Dutch East India Company from Taiwan in 1662 and established a Chinese-style government there as a base for the anti-Qing struggle.

## The Zheng Regime and Qing Rule — 1662 - 1895

Koxinga's heirs ran an independent Ming-loyalist kingdom from Tainan until 1683, when the Qing dynasty absorbed Taiwan and administered it for more than two centuries, eventually raising it to a province.

- 1683: **Qing conquest of the Zheng regime** — A Qing fleet defeats the Zheng (Tungning) forces, ending the Ming-loyalist kingdom; Taiwan is annexed the following year and attached to Fujian province as a prefecture. [britannica-koxinga, britannica-nanjing]
- 1885: **Taiwan made a Qing province** — The Qing government raises Taiwan to the status of a separate province, with Liu Mingchuan as reforming first governor promoting railways and modernisation. [britannica-taiwan]

## Fifty Years of Japanese Colonial Rule — 1895 - 1945

Ceded to Japan after the First Sino-Japanese War, Taiwan spent half a century as a Japanese colony that combined infrastructure-building and economic development with armed pacification and assimilation policies.

- 17 April 1895: **Treaty of Shimonoseki cedes Taiwan to Japan** — Ending the First Sino-Japanese War, the treaty obliges the Qing empire to cede Taiwan and the Pescadores to Japan, beginning fifty years of Japanese colonial rule. [britannica-shimonoseki, britannica-sino-japanese-war]
- 1895: **Japanese colonial administration** — Japan develops Taiwan with railways, ports, public health and expanded agriculture while suppressing resistance and, from the late 1930s, pursuing intensive assimilation campaigns. [britannica-taiwan]

## Handover, the February 28 Incident, and the ROC Relocation — 1945 - 1949

Republic of China forces took over Taiwan in 1945; harsh early administration sparked the February 28 Incident of 1947, and after defeat in the Chinese Civil War the ROC central government relocated to the island in December 1949.

- October 1945: **ROC takes over Taiwan** — After Japan's surrender, the Republic of China under the Nationalists (Kuomintang) takes over the administration of Taiwan from Japan. [britannica-nationalist-rule]
- 28 February 1947: **February 28 Incident** — Protests against Nationalist rule, triggered by a violent monopoly-enforcement episode in Taipei, are crushed by troops; thousands are killed, beginning the long repression known as the White Terror. [britannica-nationalist-rule, britannica-white-terror]
- 1 October 1949: **People's Republic of China proclaimed** — Having defeated the Nationalists in the Chinese Civil War, Mao Zedong proclaims the People's Republic of China on the mainland; Chiang and his forces withdraw toward Taiwan. [state-chinese-rev]
- December 1949: **ROC government relocates to Taiwan** — Chiang Kai-shek's Republic of China central government relocates to Taipei after defeat in the civil war, founding the present self-governing polity on the island; this was not a declaration of independence. [state-chinese-rev, britannica-chiang]

Figures:

- Chiang Kai-shek (1887-1975) — Leader of the Nationalists (Kuomintang); head of the ROC government on Taiwan. Head of the Nationalist government in China from 1928 to 1949, he led the Kuomintang to defeat in the Chinese Civil War and relocated the Republic of China government to Taiwan in 1949, ruling the island until his death in 1975.

## Cold War, the 'Two Chinas' Question, and the Economic Miracle — 1949 - 1987

Shielded by the United States, the ROC held China's UN seat until 1971 and Washington's recognition until 1979; meanwhile, under one-party martial law, Taiwan achieved a celebrated export-led economic miracle.

- October 1971: **UN seat transferred to the PRC** — The UN General Assembly votes to seat the People's Republic of China in China's place at the United Nations; the ROC's representatives lose the seat. The resolution does not address Taiwan's sovereignty. [state-rapprochement]
- 1972: **Shanghai Communiqué** — During President Nixon's visit to China, the United States and the PRC issue the Shanghai Communiqué; the U.S. acknowledges the position that there is but one China, beginning normalisation. [state-rapprochement]
- 1979: **U.S. switches recognition to the PRC** — The United States establishes formal diplomatic relations with the PRC as the sole legal government of China and ends recognition of the ROC; Congress passes the Taiwan Relations Act to sustain unofficial ties. [state-china-policy]
- 1965: **National Palace Museum opens in Taipei** — A permanent home opens in Taipei for a vast collection of Chinese imperial art and antiquities, much of it relocated from the mainland around 1948 to 1949. [npm]

## Democratisation and the Cross-Strait Dispute — 1987 - present

The lifting of martial law in 1987 began a transition to multiparty democracy, capped by the first direct presidential election in 1996; the cross-strait dispute over Taiwan's status remains unresolved into 2026.

- 1987: **Martial law lifted** — President Lee Teng-hui ends nearly four decades of martial law and the ban on travel to the mainland, opening the way to democratic reform. [britannica-lee, britannica-successors]
- 1996: **First direct presidential election** — Lee Teng-hui wins Taiwan's first direct, popular presidential election, becoming its first democratically elected president and completing the democratic transition. [britannica-lee, britannica-successors]
- to 2026: **Ongoing cross-strait dispute** — Into 2026 the dispute remains unresolved: the ROC governs Taiwan while the PRC claims it as a province without controlling it, and no peace treaty has settled the question. [britannica-taiwan, state-china-policy]

Figures:

- Lee Teng-hui (1923-2020) — President of the Republic of China (1988-2000). Succeeding to the presidency in 1988, he ended martial law, drove constitutional reform, and in 1996 won Taiwan's first direct presidential election, presiding over the island's transition to democracy.
