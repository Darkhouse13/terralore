# The Chronicle of Senegal: From Takrur to a poet's republic

History of Senegal: From Takrur to a poet's republic. 6 eras and 26 sourced events, every claim traceable to a named reference.

Canonical: https://terralore.co/country/SEN/chronicle
Updated: 2026-06-13

Founding: Independence from France — 1960. Senegal first gained independence on 4 April 1960 within the Mali Federation, a union with the Sudanese Republic. When the federation broke apart, Senegal proclaimed its separate independence on 20 August 1960, with Léopold Sédar Senghor as its first president.

On the westernmost edge of Africa, Senegal grew from the Islamic state of Takrur and the Wolof empire of Jolof into a mosaic of kingdoms, then into the cornerstone of French West Africa. Its coast — Saint-Louis, Gorée, Dakar — anchored centuries of Atlantic trade, including the slave trade. Independent since 1960 under the poet-president Léopold Sédar Senghor, Senegal has become one of Africa's steadiest democracies, even as a separatist conflict in the southern Casamance ran for four decades.

## Takrur and the Coming of Islam — c. 800 – 1300

On the Senegal River rose Takrur, among the first West African states to adopt Islam as official law.

- 9th–11th c.: **Islam reaches the Senegal River** — North African Muslim traders introduce Islam to the peoples of the Senegal valley well before any ruler adopts it officially. [blackpast-islam-wa, wiki-takrur, wiki-islam-senegal]
- c. 1035: **War Jabi adopts Islamic law in Takrur** — King War Jabi of Takrur is recorded as making Sharia official, by tradition the first Sahelian ruler to do so, anchoring Islam in the region. [wiki-takrur, blackpast-islam-wa]
- 1086: **Takruri forces and the Almoravids** — Takrur aligns with the Almoravid movement; black cavalry, possibly Takruri, are recorded among Almoravid forces in Iberia, reflecting Takrur's wider reach. [wiki-takrur]
- c. 1286: **Takrur drawn into Mali's orbit** — Political fragmentation and the rise of the Mali Empire bring Takrur under outside dynasties, ending its independent prominence. [wiki-takrur]

## The Jolof Empire and Its Successor Kingdoms — c. 1350 – 1700

A Wolof empire bound the western savanna together, then fractured into the kingdoms that defined precolonial Senegal.

- mid-14th c.: **Rise of the Jolof Empire** — A Wolof confederation unites provinces between the Senegal and Gambia rivers, drawing tribute and trade across the western savanna. [worldhistory-wolof, britannica-jolof]
- c. 1335: **Guelowar dynasty in Sine and Saloum** — The Gelwaar lineage establishes matrilineal monarchy over the Serer kingdoms of Sine and Saloum, ruling under the title Maad. [wiki-guelowar]
- 1444: **Portuguese reach the Senegambian coast** — Portuguese ships arrive off Senegal and the Gambia, opening Atlantic maritime trade — and the first European slave raids — along the coast. [openstax-african-trade, wiki-french-conquest]
- c. 1556: **Cayor breaks from Jolof** — The coastal province of Cayor rebels and wins independence, and Baol and Waalo follow, fracturing the empire into rival kingdoms. [worldhistory-wolof, wiki-cayor, wiki-baol]

## Atlantic Trade, Gorée and Saint-Louis — 1450 – 1850

The coast became a hub of Atlantic commerce, including the slave trade, and the seedbed of French settlement.

- 1659: **France founds Saint-Louis** — French traders establish Saint-Louis at the mouth of the Senegal River, the first permanent French settlement and future colonial capital. [wiki-french-conquest, britannica-saintlouis]
- 1677: **France takes Gorée** — France captures Gorée island from the Dutch during the Franco-Dutch War, holding the slaving and trading post for nearly three centuries. [wiki-french-conquest, unesco-goree]
- 1770s–1780s: **House of Slaves built on Gorée** — The Maison des Esclaves is constructed on Gorée; it later becomes a museum and a contested memorial to the Atlantic slave trade. [wiki-house-of-slaves, unesco-goree]
- 1848: **Citizenship granted to coastal towns** — The French Second Republic extends citizenship to inhabitants of the coastal communes, a status later restricted before being reaffirmed in 1916. [wiki-four-communes]

## French Conquest, the Four Communes and the Marabouts — 1850 – 1960

France subdued the interior kingdoms while a unique coastal citizenry and powerful Sufi brotherhoods reshaped Senegalese society.

- 1854–1855: **Faidherbe begins the conquest** — Governor Louis Faidherbe pushes up the Senegal River, conquering Waalo and building forts that subdue the interior kingdoms over decades. [wiki-french-conquest]
- c. 1883: **Amadou Bamba founds the Mourides** — Cheikh Amadou Bamba establishes the Mouride Sufi brotherhood, later founding the holy city of Touba in 1887 and preaching nonviolence and work. [wiki-amadou-bamba]
- 1895: **Bamba exiled; French West Africa formed** — France exiles Amadou Bamba to Gabon for his independent influence; the same year Senegal is incorporated into French West Africa. [wiki-amadou-bamba, wiki-french-conquest]
- 1914: **Blaise Diagne elected to the French parliament** — Diagne becomes the first African deputy in the French National Assembly, representing the Four Communes. [wiki-four-communes, blackpast-diagne]
- 1916: **The Diagne Law confirms citizenship** — Diagne secures a law affirming the originaires of the Four Communes as full French citizens who may retain Muslim personal status. [wiki-four-communes, blackpast-diagne]

Figures:

- Blaise Diagne (1872–1934) — First African deputy in the French parliament. Born on Gorée, Diagne won election in 1914 and championed the 1916 law confirming the citizenship of the Four Communes' originaires while preserving their Muslim personal status.

## Independence and the Senghor Republic — 1958 – 1980

Senegal won independence in 1960 and, under the poet Léopold Sédar Senghor, set a course of stable civilian rule.

- 1959: **Mali Federation formed** — Senegal and the Sudanese Republic unite in the Mali Federation, seeking a federal path out of French rule. [britannica-mali-fed, wiki-mali-fed]
- 4 April 1960: **Independence within the Mali Federation** — France grants the Mali Federation independence, transferring power on 4 April 1960; full independence follows on 20 June. [britannica-mali-fed, wiki-mali-fed]
- 20 August 1960: **Senegal proclaims separate independence** — After the federation collapses, Senegal secedes and proclaims its own independence; Senghor is elected first president. [wiki-mali-fed, asc-senghor]
- 31 December 1980: **Senghor resigns peacefully** — Senghor voluntarily hands power to Prime Minister Abdou Diouf, Senegal's first orderly transfer of power. [asc-senghor, usip-senegal]

Figures:

- Léopold Sédar Senghor (1906–2001) — First president of Senegal; poet and theorist of Négritude. Poet, philosopher and statesman, Senghor co-founded the Négritude movement and led Senegal from independence in 1960 until his voluntary resignation in 1980. In 1983 he became the first African member of the Académie française.

## A Democratic Republic and the Casamance Question — 1981 – present

Senegal built a record of peaceful elections while a long separatist conflict simmered in the southern Casamance.

- 26 December 1982: **Casamance insurgency begins** — Clashes in Ziguinchor over a Casamance flag mark the start of the MFDC separatist conflict, one of Africa's longest-running. [wiki-mfdc, newhumanitarian-casamance]
- 2000: **Wade defeats Diouf** — Abdoulaye Wade wins the presidency from Abdou Diouf — Senegal's first transfer of power across party lines. [usip-senegal, cdsafrica-senegal]
- 2012: **Macky Sall elected** — Sall defeats Wade at the polls, continuing Senegal's pattern of competitive elections and peaceful handovers. [wiki-macky-sall, cdsafrica-senegal]
- 2022: **Casamance peace agreement** — A peace deal between the government and MFDC factions proves more durable than earlier ceasefires; violence drops sharply. [wiki-mfdc, newhumanitarian-casamance]
- 2024: **Faye wins after democratic crisis** — After Macky Sall's attempt to delay the election is overturned, Bassirou Diomaye Faye wins; power passes peacefully again. [usip-senegal, wiki-faye]
