Founding: Independence from Australia — 16 September 1975. Papua New Guinea became an independent state on 16 September 1975, when Australia transferred sovereignty over the combined Territory of Papua and New Guinea to a new Melanesian nation led by Chief Minister Michael Somare, who became its first prime minister. The country had moved to internal self-government on 1 December 1973, and the independence ceremony in Port Moresby was attended by Prince Charles representing Queen Elizabeth II — who remained head of state — and by Australian leaders. The United States recognised the new nation the same day. This founding rests on a human history tens of thousands of years deep: New Guinea was first settled around 50,000 years ago, and its highland peoples were among the world's earliest farmers.

Papua New Guinea occupies the eastern half of New Guinea — one of the longest-inhabited regions on Earth, settled around 50,000 years ago — together with the Bismarck Archipelago, Bougainville, and hundreds of smaller islands. In the highland swamps of Kuk, its peoples invented agriculture independently some 7,000 to 10,000 years ago, draining wetlands to grow taro and bananas, and over millennia developed the greatest linguistic diversity of any country, with more than 800 languages. European traders, missionaries, and labour recruiters arrived in the nineteenth century; the island was partitioned into German New Guinea in the north and British (later Australian) Papua in the south, and the whole eastern half came under Australian administration after the First World War. The fierce Second World War campaigns along the Kokoda Track and on Bougainville gave way to decolonisation, and on 16 September 1975 Papua New Guinea became an independent nation under Michael Somare — a young, resource-rich, and extraordinarily diverse state still shaped by the Bougainville conflict and the question of that island's future.

## Deep past: settlement and the world's first farmers — c. 50,000 BCE – 1500 CE

People reached New Guinea around 50,000 years ago, and in the highland swamp of Kuk their descendants invented agriculture independently — one of only a handful of places on Earth where farming arose on its own.

- c. 50,000 years ago: **First settlement of New Guinea** — People crossed from Southeast Asia to the joined landmass of Sahul, settling New Guinea in some of the earliest sea voyages and beginning one of the longest continuous human occupations on Earth. [brit-png, brit-png-history]
- c. 7,000–10,000 years ago: **Independent invention of agriculture at Kuk** — In the highland swamp of Kuk, communities drained wetlands to cultivate taro and bananas, one of only a few places on Earth where farming arose independently — recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. [unesco-kuk, brit-kuk]
- c. 500 BCE: **Austronesian speakers reach the coasts** — A major migration of Austronesian-speaking peoples settles the coastal regions, layering a seafaring, trading world over the far older interior one. [bbc-png]
- before European contact: **More than 800 languages emerge** — Rugged terrain and long isolation gave rise to over 800 distinct languages and more than a thousand ethnic groups, making New Guinea the most linguistically diverse region in the world. [brit-png-languages, brit-png]

## European contact and colonial partition — 1500s – 1914

Traders, missionaries, and labour recruiters opened the coasts to the wider world in the nineteenth century, and in 1884 the island was carved between a German north and a British south.

- 1526: **The first European visitor** — The Portuguese sailor Jorge de Meneses reaches the islands and calls them the ilhas dos Papuas; twenty years later a Spaniard names the main island New Guinea for its resemblance to the African coast. [bbc-png]
- c. 1800: **The Kula ring and the trading seas** — Substantial exchange networks operate across New Guinea, the most extensive of them the Kula ring — a ceremonial circuit of shell valuables spanning hundreds of kilometres of open water. [bbc-png]
- 19th century: **Traders, missionaries, and labour recruiters arrive** — Whalers, plume hunters, and beche-de-mer traders worked the coasts, missionaries founded stations, and labour recruiters — including coercive 'blackbirders' — drew Melanesian workers to plantations in Queensland and Fiji. [slnsw-png, brit-png-history]
- November 1884: **The island is partitioned** — Germany proclaimed a protectorate over the north-east (German New Guinea) and Britain over the southern coast (British New Guinea) within days of each other, splitting eastern New Guinea between two empires. [brit-png-colonial, natarchives-uk-png]
- 1895: **Gold on the Mambare** — A strike on the Mambare River brings a gold rush and with it hostilities between miners and the people whose land they worked. [bbc-png]
- 1906: **Australia takes over Papua** — Following the Papua Act of 1905, British New Guinea became the Australian Territory of Papua, and formal Australian administration began in 1906. [brit-png-colonial, peo-papua]

## Australian rule and the highland 'first contact' — 1914 – 1942

Australia seized German New Guinea in 1914 and held it under a League of Nations mandate, and in the 1930s prospectors walked into the highlands to meet a million people the outside world had never known.

- September 1914: **Australia occupies German New Guinea** — At the outbreak of the First World War, Australian forces seized German New Guinea, ending German colonial rule in the region. [brit-png-colonial, natarchives-uk-png]
- 1921: **League of Nations mandate over New Guinea** — Australia received a Class C League of Nations mandate to administer the former German territory as the Territory of New Guinea, governed from Rabaul and kept separate from Papua. [brit-png-colonial, brit-mandate]
- 1920s: **The Bulolo gold rush** — A major find along the Bulolo river draws a second rush, pulling outsiders and aircraft into country that had barely been mapped. [bbc-png]
- early 1930s: **First contact in the highlands** — Australian prospectors and patrol officers walked into the central highlands and found densely populated farming valleys — perhaps a million people — entirely unknown to the outside world until then. [brit-png-history, brit-png]

## The Second World War: Kokoda and Bougainville — 1942 – 1945

Japan's drive toward Australia turned New Guinea into a vast battlefield; the Kokoda Track became the scene of some of the hardest fighting of the war, alongside Papuan soldiers and carriers.

- 21 July – 16 November 1942: **The Kokoda Track campaign** — Australian and Papuan troops halted the Japanese advance over the Owen Stanley Range toward Port Moresby and drove them back; over 600 Australians were killed in arguably Australia's most significant campaign of the war. [awm-kokoda, anzac-kokoda]
- 1942–1945: **Papuan carriers and soldiers** — Papuan carriers moved supplies and the wounded across the mountains, and soldiers of the Papuan Infantry Battalion fought alongside Australian forces throughout the New Guinea campaigns. [awm-kokoda, anzac-kokoda]
- November 1944 – August 1945: **The Bougainville campaign** — More than 30,000 Australians fought a long campaign of patrols and small actions on Bougainville, with over 500 killed, until the Japanese garrison surrendered in August 1945. [awm-bougainville-ww2, anzac-kokoda]

## Self-government and independence — 1945 – 1975

After the war Australia merged Papua and New Guinea and, under United Nations pressure to decolonise, guided the joined territory to self-government and, on 16 September 1975, to independence under Michael Somare.

- 1945: **Papua and New Guinea united** — Australia combined its administration of Papua and the former German mandate into a single Territory of Papua and New Guinea, with the north held as a United Nations trust territory. [brit-png-colonial, naa-png-independence]
- 1961: **The first elections with indigenous voters** — Papuans and New Guineans vote for the first time, sixteen years after the two territories were joined under one administration. [bbc-png]
- 1963: **West New Guinea transferred to Indonesia** — The United Nations hands control of the island's western half to Indonesia; the two governments agree the position of their shared border ten years later. [bbc-png]
- 1964: **An elected indigenous majority** — A 64-member House of Assembly replaces the Legislative Council, and for the first time indigenous representatives hold most of the seats. [bbc-png]
- 1971: **The country is named Papua New Guinea** — The two territories that two empires had drawn take a single name, four years before they take a flag. [bbc-png]
- 1 December 1973: **Internal self-government** — Papua New Guinea achieved internal self-government with Michael Somare as chief minister, a peaceful step toward full independence from Australia. [naa-png-independence, brit-png-history]
- 16 September 1975: **Independence** — Papua New Guinea became an independent nation under Prime Minister Michael Somare; Australia transferred sovereignty and the United States recognised the new state the same day. [naa-png-independence, statedept-png]

Figures:

- Michael Somare (1936–2021) — First Prime Minister of Papua New Guinea. Founder of the Pangu Pati and 'father of the nation', he led Papua New Guinea to self-government in 1973 and independence in 1975, becoming its first prime minister and a defining figure of its politics for decades.

## Bougainville and modern Papua New Guinea — 1975 – present

A decade-long secessionist war on Bougainville tested the young nation; a 2001 peace deal and a 2019 independence vote left its future open, even as resource wealth and deep diversity define modern Papua New Guinea.

- 1988–1998: **The Bougainville conflict** — A secessionist war over the Panguna mine, land, and environmental damage pitted Bougainvillean rebels against the PNG Defence Force; death-toll estimates range widely, commonly cited at roughly 10,000–20,000 and treated as estimates. [concil-bougainville, globalsec-bougainville]
- 1997: **Sandline, the mutiny and the Burnham Truce** — The government hires mercenaries to reinforce its troops on Bougainville, provoking a mutiny in its own army; the Burnham Truce later that year ends the decade of armed struggle. [bbc-png]
- 2001: **Bougainville Peace Agreement** — Following a 1998 ceasefire, a peace agreement created the Autonomous Region of Bougainville and promised a future referendum on the island's political status. [concil-bougainville, unimelb-bougainville]
- 23 November – 7 December 2019: **Bougainville independence referendum** — Bougainvilleans voted about 98 per cent for independence over greater autonomy; the non-binding result must be negotiated with PNG and ratified by its parliament. [unimelb-bougainville, cnn-bougainville]

Figures:

- Francis Ona (1953–2005) — Bougainville secessionist leader. A former mine surveyor and landowner whose 1988 sabotage of the Panguna copper mine over land and environmental grievances triggered the Bougainville conflict; he led the secessionist movement and never accepted the peace process.
