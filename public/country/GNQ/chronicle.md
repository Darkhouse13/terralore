Founding: Independence from Spain — 12 October 1968. The Republic of Equatorial Guinea became independent from Spain on 12 October 1968, ending nearly two centuries of Spanish claims that began with the 1778 Treaty of El Pardo. Spain, under United Nations and nationalist pressure, had moved the territory from colony to overseas "province" in 1959 and granted internal autonomy after a 1963 referendum; a constitutional referendum in 1968 preceded full sovereignty. Francisco Macías Nguema, who won the September 1968 presidential election, became the new state's first president. The country it inherited combined two very different lands: the island of Bioko (Fernando Pó), with its Bubi people and Spanish-built cocoa plantations, and the mainland enclave of Río Muni, home to the Fang and coastal Ndowe peoples — together with the small islands of Annobón and the islets of the Gulf of Guinea.

Equatorial Guinea is the only sovereign state in mainland Africa with Spanish as an official language, made up of the volcanic island of Bioko (long called Fernando Pó), the small island of Annobón, several islets, and the mainland enclave of Río Muni between Cameroon and Gabon. Bioko is the homeland of the Bantu-speaking Bubi; Río Muni is dominated by the Fang, with Ndowe and related "Playero" peoples on the coast, while the Annobonese descend from people brought by the Portuguese from Angola and São Tomé. Portuguese navigators sighted Bioko in 1472, naming it for the explorer Fernão do Pó, and held the islands until ceding them, with commercial rights on the adjacent coast, to Spain by the Treaty of El Pardo in 1778. Britain ran an anti-slave-trade base on the island from 1827 to 1843; Spain established effective administration only from 1858 and built a cocoa-plantation economy on Fernando Pó worked by contract labourers from Liberia and Nigeria. United from 1926 as the colony of Spanish Guinea, the territory became independent on 12 October 1968. Its first president, Francisco Macías Nguema, imposed an eleven-year reign of terror in which tens of thousands were killed and a large share of the population fled into exile, until his nephew Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo overthrew and executed him in 1979. Obiang has ruled ever since, his tenure transformed by the discovery of major offshore oil from the mid-1990s, which produced soaring wealth alongside deep inequality.

## Peoples of the island and the mainland — Before 1472

Bantu-speaking peoples settled the island of Bioko and the forests of Río Muni over many centuries: the Bubi on the island, the Fang and Ndowe on the mainland coast, with the Annobonese a later, Portuguese-introduced community.

- from c. 13th century: **Bubi settle Bioko** — Bantu-speaking peoples, ancestors of the Bubi, are held to have crossed from the mainland to the island later called Fernando Pó (Bioko) in successive waves, settling an island that earlier held Neolithic populations and developing a distinct island society. [britannica-history-eg, history-eg-wiki, nwe-eg]
- pre-colonial era: **Fang and Ndowe of Río Muni** — The Fang, a large Bantu people, settled the forests of the mainland enclave of Río Muni, while Ndowe and related coastal peoples — later grouped as Playeros — occupied the shoreline and mediated trade with the interior. [britannica-history-eg, encyclopedia-com-eg, state-eg-bgn]
- pre-colonial era: **Annobón peopled from Angola** — The remote island of Annobón, uninhabited before Atlantic navigation, was settled by Africans brought by the Portuguese largely from Angola via São Tomé; the Annobonese came to speak a Portuguese-based creole. [history-eg-wiki, encyclopedia-com-eg]

## Fernão do Pó, the Portuguese, and the cession to Spain — 1472 – 1827

The Portuguese navigator Fernão do Pó sighted Bioko in 1472 and named it Formosa; Portugal held the islands until ceding Fernando Pó, Annobón and rights on the adjacent coast to Spain by the Treaty of El Pardo in 1778.

- 1472: **Fernão do Pó sights the island** — The Portuguese navigator Fernão do Pó sighted the volcanic island in the Gulf of Guinea in 1472, reportedly naming it Formosa; it soon took his own name as Fernando Pó, the island later renamed Bioko. [britannica-history-eg, history-eg-wiki, nwe-eg]
- 11 March 1778: **Treaty of El Pardo cedes the islands to Spain** — By the Treaty of El Pardo, Portugal ceded Fernando Pó, Annobón and commercial rights on the adjacent African coast to Spain, in exchange for recognition of Portuguese claims in South America; Spain sought a base for the slave trade. [el-pardo-wiki, britannica-history-eg, history-eg-wiki, nwe-eg]
- 1778–1780: **The Argelejo expedition fails** — A Spanish expedition under the Conde de Argelejo arrived to take possession but was broken by disease and disorder; Spain effectively abandoned the islands for decades, administering them on paper from its American viceroyalties. [history-eg-wiki, el-pardo-wiki]

## The British base and the Spanish cocoa colony — 1827 – 1926

Britain ran an anti-slave-trade base on Fernando Pó from 1827 to 1843, founding Port Clarence; Spain established effective rule from 1858 and built a cocoa-plantation economy worked by contract labour from Liberia and beyond.

- 1827: **Britain founds an anti-slavery base at Port Clarence** — With Spanish acquiescence, the Royal Navy under William Fitzwilliam Owen established a base on Fernando Pó in 1827, founding Port Clarence (later Santa Isabel/Malabo) to support the squadron suppressing the Atlantic slave trade. [history-eg-wiki, nwe-eg, britannica-history-eg]
- 1843: **Britain withdraws to Sierra Leone** — The British base on Fernando Pó was withdrawn to Sierra Leone in 1843, Spain having declined to sell its rights; freed Africans, Sierra Leoneans and other settlers remained as the English-speaking Fernandino community. [history-eg-wiki, nwe-eg]
- 1858: **Spain installs effective rule under Carlos Chacón** — Spain sent the naval officer Carlos Chacón as governor in 1858 to organise a colonial administration on Fernando Pó; Baptist missionaries established under the British were pressed to withdraw in favour of Spanish Catholic missions. [history-eg-wiki, britannica-history-eg]
- 1900: **Treaty of Paris fixes Río Muni** — The 1900 Treaty of Paris with France delimited Spain's mainland enclave of Río Muni at roughly 26,000 km², far smaller than Spain had claimed, setting the borders with French Cameroon and Gabon. [history-eg-wiki, spanish-guinea-wiki]
- 1914: **Liberian labour treaty for the cocoa plantations** — A 1914 treaty brought contract labourers from Liberia to Fernando Pó's cocoa plantations; an ILO inquiry in 1930 found the recruitment had occurred under "criminal compulsion" akin to slave raiding, and the Liberian supply was cut off. [spanish-guinea-wiki, history-eg-wiki]

Figures:

- William Fitzwilliam Owen (1774–1857) — Royal Navy officer, founder of Port Clarence. British naval surveyor who in 1827 established the anti-slave-trade base on Fernando Pó and founded Port Clarence (later Santa Isabel, now Malabo), from which cruisers intercepted slave ships and landed liberated Africans.

## Spanish Guinea and the road to independence — 1926 – 1968

United from 1926 as the colony of Spanish Guinea, the island and mainland were governed as a cocoa-and-timber dependency until pressure for decolonisation carried the territory to independence on 12 October 1968.

- 1926: **Bioko and Río Muni united as Spanish Guinea** — From 1926 Spain governed Fernando Pó and the mainland enclave of Río Muni together as the colony of Spanish Guinea, its economy resting on the cocoa plantations of the island. [spanish-guinea-wiki, history-eg-wiki]
- 1959: **Made an overseas "province" of Spain** — In 1959 Spain reclassified the colony as the "Equatorial Region," divided into the provinces of Fernando Poo and Río Muni, with elected local representatives sent to the Spanish Cortes — a first step toward decolonisation. [spanish-guinea-wiki, history-eg-wiki]
- 1963: **Autonomy and the name Equatorial Guinea** — A 1963 referendum approved a regime of autonomy granting limited internal self-government; the territory took the name Equatorial Guinea ahead of full independence. [spanish-guinea-wiki, history-eg-wiki]
- 12 October 1968: **Independence from Spain** — After a 1968 constitutional referendum and September elections won by Francisco Macías Nguema, the Republic of Equatorial Guinea became independent from Spain on 12 October 1968, uniting Bioko, Río Muni and Annobón. [britannica-history-eg, history-eg-wiki, spanish-guinea-wiki, nwe-eg]

## The terror of Macías Nguema — 1968 – 1979

Independence gave way within months to the eleven-year dictatorship of Francisco Macías Nguema — a reign of killings, forced labour and flight in which a large share of the population died or fled, until his nephew overthrew and executed him in 1979.

- October 1968: **Macías Nguema becomes first president** — Francisco Macías Nguema, victor of the September 1968 elections, became Equatorial Guinea's first president at independence, soon concentrating power and beginning a slide into dictatorship. [britannica-history-eg, history-eg-wiki, globalsecurity-macias]
- 1972: **President for life** — After establishing a single-party state in 1970 and gutting the constitution, Macías had himself declared president for life in 1972, ruling through a violent personality cult that dismantled churches, schools and much of the state. [history-eg-wiki, globalsecurity-macias]
- 1972–1979: **Mass killing and exile** — Under Macías tens of thousands were killed — figures of roughly 35,000–50,000 deaths are commonly cited — and by 1979 around a quarter of the population had fled into exile; the Bubi and Nigerian workers were among those targeted. Figures remain estimates. [coup-1979-wiki, globalsecurity-macias, history-eg-wiki]
- mid-1970s: **Cocoa economy collapses as labourers flee** — Amid the terror, tens of thousands of Nigerian contract labourers were withdrawn from Fernando Pó's plantations in the mid-1970s, and cocoa production — the colony's economic mainstay — collapsed. [history-eg-wiki, spanish-guinea-wiki]
- 3 August 1979: **Obiang's coup overthrows Macías** — On 3 August 1979 Macías's nephew, army officer Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo, led a coup that overthrew the dictator after fighting that cost hundreds of lives. [coup-1979-wiki, globalsecurity-macias]
- 29 September 1979: **Macías executed** — Captured and tried, Francisco Macías Nguema was executed by firing squad on 29 September 1979 along with several associates, ending eleven years of dictatorship; Obiang assumed the presidency. [coup-1979-wiki, history-eg-wiki]

Figures:

- Francisco Macías Nguema (1924–1979) — First president and dictator. Equatorial Guinea's first president, from 1968, who built a personal tyranny — a single-party state, a president-for-life cult, and mass killings and exile that devastated a country of only a few hundred thousand people, until he was overthrown and executed in 1979.
- Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo (born 1942) — Army officer, later president. Senior army officer and nephew of Macías who had run the Black Beach prison; he led the August 1979 coup against his uncle, had him executed, and took the presidency — beginning a rule that became one of the world's longest.

## Obiang, oil, and inequality — 1979 – present

In power since 1979 and among the world's longest-serving leaders, Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo has presided over a country transformed by major offshore oil discovered from the mid-1990s — bringing sudden wealth and stark inequality.

- October 1979: **Obiang takes power** — Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo assumed the presidency after the 1979 coup and execution of Macías, and renamed the main islands Bioko and Annobón; he has remained in power ever since. [coup-1979-wiki, britannica-eg]
- March 1995: **Zafiro oil field discovered** — Mobil and partners discovered the major Zafiro oil field offshore Bioko Island in March 1995, the find that opened Equatorial Guinea's petroleum era. [zafiro-nsenergy, ogj-eg-oil]
- August 1996: **Oil production begins** — The Zafiro field came on-stream in August 1996, less than eighteen months after discovery, launching Equatorial Guinea as an oil producer; output later peaked in the hundreds of thousands of barrels a day. [zafiro-nsenergy, ogj-eg-oil]
- March 2004: **The "Wonga Coup" foiled** — An attempted coup by a group of foreign mercenaries — the so-called Wonga Coup, linked to Simon Mann — was foiled in 2004, underscoring the instability around oil-rich Equatorial Guinea. [history-eg-wiki, britannica-eg]
- 2000s onward: **Wealth amid deep inequality** — Oil gave Equatorial Guinea one of Africa's highest GDP-per-capita figures, yet much of the population remained poor; the government faced sustained criticism over corruption, human rights and the concentration of wealth in the ruling family. [britannica-eg, history-eg-wiki]
- 22 Feb 2008 – 16 Apr 2010: **Joined, then delisted, by the transparency initiative** — Equatorial Guinea joins the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative in February 2008 and is delisted in April 2010; a fresh application in October 2019 is refused by the EITI Board in February 2020. [eiti-gnq]
- 2016: **A capital in the rainforest** — The government moves its headquarters from Malabo on Bioko to Ciudad de la Paz — Djibloho — an unfinished purpose-built city deep in the mainland forest. [bbc-gnq]

Figures:

- Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo (born 1942) — President of Equatorial Guinea since 1979. Seized power from his uncle Macías in 1979 and has ruled ever since, among the world's longest-serving leaders; his tenure was transformed by offshore oil from the mid-1990s, bringing high GDP per capita alongside enduring poverty, corruption charges and human-rights criticism.
