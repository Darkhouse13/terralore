# The Chronicle of Costa Rica: From the chiefdoms and stone spheres of the Diquis to a disarmed democracy of coffee, peace, and rainforest.

Founding: Independence from Spain — 15 September 1821. On 15 September 1821, following the news that Mexico had broken with Spain, the Captaincy General of Guatemala proclaimed the independence of Central America, and Costa Rica with it. The remote province first joined the short-lived Mexican Empire, then in 1823 helped form the United Provinces of Central America. Disenchanted with the strife among the federation's other states, Costa Rica severed its ties in 1838, and in 1848 it declared itself a fully sovereign republic.

Costa Rica occupies a narrow isthmus that long served as a bridge between the civilisations of Mesoamerica and the Andes, where chiefdom societies left the enigmatic stone spheres of the Diquis. Reached by Columbus in 1502 and named the Rich Coast, it became one of Spain's poorest and most isolated colonies, a province of subsistence smallholders within the Captaincy General of Guatemala. Independence came on 15 September 1821 as part of Central America, and after a spell in a regional federation Costa Rica emerged as a separate republic, its society built on coffee and bananas. A brief civil war in 1948 led to the abolition of the army in 1949, since when the country has become renowned for stable democracy, social welfare, and the conservation of its extraordinary biodiversity.

## Chiefdoms and the Stone Spheres — c. 7000 BCE - 1500 CE

On the isthmus between Mesoamerica and the Andes, indigenous peoples built ranked chiefdoms and carved the perfect stone spheres of the Diquis, among the most enigmatic monuments of the ancient Americas.

- c. 500-1500 CE: **Diquis chiefdoms and the stone spheres** — Chiefdom societies in the Diquis delta carve more than 300 near-perfect stone spheres and build settlements of mounds and paved precincts. [unesco-diquis, met-sphere]
- 2014 CE: **UNESCO World Heritage inscription** — The Diquis settlements with their stone spheres become Costa Rica's first cultural World Heritage Site, recognised as unique evidence of pre-Columbian chiefdom societies. [unesco-diquis]

## The Rich Coast: A Poor Colony — 1502 - 1821

Named the Rich Coast by Columbus but yielding little gold, Costa Rica became one of Spain's poorest and most isolated provinces, a society of subsistence smallholders within the Captaincy General of Guatemala.

- 1502 CE: **Columbus reaches the Rich Coast** — On his fourth voyage Columbus lands on the Caribbean coast; gifts of gold inspire the name Costa Rica, the Rich Coast. [britannica-history-cr]
- 1563 CE: **Founding of Cartago** — The Spanish establish Cartago in the Central Valley as the colonial capital, which it remains until 1823. [britannica-history-cr, britannica-cartago]

Figures:

- Juan Vasquez de Coronado (c. 1523-1565) — Spanish conquistador and governor. Conquistador credited with the pacification and early settlement of Costa Rica, who founded the colony's first stable towns in the Central Valley and was named its governor before drowning at sea.

## Independence and a Coffee Republic — 1821 - 1889

Independence in 1821, membership of a fractious Central American federation, and the rise of coffee transformed an isolated province into a sovereign republic with a precocious tradition of elections.

- 15 Sept 1821: **Independence from Spain** — Central America, Costa Rica included, declares independence from Spain; the province soon joins the Mexican Empire and then a regional federation. [britannica-history-cr, britannica-independence-cr]
- 1808 CE: **Coffee arrives from Cuba** — Thirteen years before independence, coffee is introduced and becomes the principal crop of a province that had never had one — a dominance lasting into the 20th century. [bbc-costarica]
- 1824 CE: **Juan Mora Fernandez, first head of state** — Costa Rica's first elected head of state founds schools and courts and promotes coffee planting through free land grants. [encyclopedia-mora]
- 1838 CE: **Withdrawal from the federation** — Disenchanted with the strife of the United Provinces of Central America, Costa Rica severs its federal ties and charts an independent course. [britannica-history-cr, britannica-independence-cr]
- 30 Aug 1848: **Costa Rica declares itself sovereign** — A decade after leaving the federation, the republic formally declares independence and notifies Washington on 5 September. [statedept-costarica]
- 24 Apr 1849: **US recognition — on paper** — Washington recognises Costa Rican independence by empowering its charge d'affaires in Guatemala, who never visits the country and reaches no agreement by post. [statedept-costarica]
- 24 Mar 1851: **Diplomatic relations established** — Costa Rican minister Felipe Molina presents his credentials in Washington; a US consulate opens in San Jose the following year. [statedept-costarica]
- 1856-1857: **National War against William Walker** — Costa Rica joins Central America in defeating the American filibuster William Walker; the soldier Juan Santamaria becomes a national hero. [britannica-walker, britannica-santamaria]

Figures:

- Juan Mora Fernandez (1784-1854) — First head of state of Costa Rica. Teacher who served as Costa Rica's first elected head of state from 1824, founding schools and a judiciary and encouraging the coffee planting that would underwrite the nation's prosperity.
- Juan Santamaria (c. 1831-1856) — Soldier and national hero. Costa Rican soldier celebrated for his part in the defence against William Walker's filibusters in 1856. He is the country's foremost patriotic symbol, his name borne by its principal airport and a national museum.

## Coffee, Bananas, and a Maturing Democracy — 1870 - 1948

Banana enclaves and a deepening coffee trade bound Costa Rica into the world economy, while an expanding electorate and a tradition of orderly succession built one of Latin America's most stable polities, until the crisis of 1948.

- 1872 CE: **Minor Keith and the banana railways** — Minor C. Keith begins building railways and planting bananas, ventures that in 1899 merge into the United Fruit Company and reshape the Caribbean coast. [britannica-keith]
- 1890 CE: **First free election in Central America** — Costa Rica holds what is regarded as the first free and honest election in Central America, beginning a long democratic tradition. [britannica-history-cr]
- 1948 CE: **Disputed election sparks civil war** — The annulment of the 1948 election to bar president-elect Otilio Ulate triggers a brief but bloody civil war. [britannica-figueres, britannica-ulate]

Figures:

- Minor C. Keith (1848-1929) — American railway and banana entrepreneur. Builder of Costa Rica's railways and founder of its banana trade, whose enterprises coalesced into the United Fruit Company, the dominant commercial power of Central America for half a century.

## Civil War and the Abolition of the Army — 1948 - 1953

A two-month civil war led by Jose Figueres Ferrer ended in a new constitution that, in 1949, abolished the standing army, a defining choice that set the course of modern Costa Rica.

- 1948 CE: **Figueres leads the civil war** — Jose Figueres Ferrer leads an armed uprising in support of president-elect Ulate; the two-month war kills about 2,000 people before the government surrenders. [britannica-figueres]
- 1949 CE: **Constitution abolishes the army** — Figueres's junta promulgates a new constitution that bans a standing army, grants women the vote, and nationalises the banks, then hands power to Ulate. [britannica-figueres, britannica-ulate]

Figures:

- Jose Figueres Ferrer (1906-1990) — Revolutionary leader and president. Leader of the 1948 uprising and head of the junta that abolished the army in 1949. Founder of the National Liberation Party and three times president, he is the architect of Costa Rica's modern social democracy.
- Otilio Ulate Blanco (1891-1973) — President of Costa Rica. Newspaper publisher whose contested 1948 victory triggered the civil war fought on his behalf. He assumed the presidency in 1949 and oversaw the consolidation of the new constitutional order.

## Peace, Welfare, and the Green Republic — 1953 - present

Without an army, Costa Rica built one of Latin America's most stable democracies and generous welfare states, won a Nobel Peace Prize, and made the conservation of its biodiversity central to its identity and economy.

- 1970 CE: **National park system created** — Costa Rica establishes a national park system that grows to protect roughly a quarter of the country, anchoring its conservation and ecotourism economy. [britannica-21st-century]
- 1987 CE: **Oscar Arias wins the Nobel Peace Prize** — President Oscar Arias Sanchez is awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for the Central American peace plan signed by the region's leaders in August 1987. [britannica-arias]
- 1985 CE: **A country without an army raises a force** — After clashes with Sandinista troops across the San Juan, a US-trained anti-guerrilla force begins operating — the limits of demilitarisation in a region at war. [bbc-costarica]
- 2000 CE: **San Juan navigation dispute settled** — Costa Rica and Nicaragua resolve a long-standing quarrel over navigation on the river that forms their border. [bbc-costarica]
- 2007 CE: **Recognition switches from Taipei to Beijing** — Costa Rica transfers diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic of China in pursuit of investment. [bbc-costarica]
- 2010 CE: **Laura Chinchilla, first woman president** — Costa Rica elects its first female head of state. [bbc-costarica]
- 2014 CE: **Diquis spheres named World Heritage** — UNESCO inscribes the Diquis chiefdom settlements and their stone spheres as Costa Rica's first cultural World Heritage Site. [unesco-diquis]
- 2015 CE: **The Hague settles Isla Portillos** — The International Court of Justice rules that sovereignty over the disputed San Juan wetland belongs to Costa Rica — a demilitarised state settling its border in court. [bbc-costarica]

Figures:

- Oscar Arias Sanchez (born 1940) — President and Nobel laureate. President in 1986-90 and 2006-10 whose Central American peace plan earned him the 1987 Nobel Peace Prize, embodying Costa Rica's identity as a demilitarised broker of peace.
