Founding: Declaration of Independence — 4 July 1776. On 4 July 1776 the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, announcing the separation of thirteen British colonies from Great Britain. A durable framework of government followed: the Constitution was signed on 17 September 1787 and, after New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify on 21 June 1788, took effect when the new federal government convened on 4 March 1789, with George Washington inaugurated as first president on 30 April 1789.

The history of the United States spans the deep antiquity of Indigenous nations who peopled the continent for many thousands of years, the violent disruption of European colonisation, and the birth of a self-governing republic in the late eighteenth century. Founded on a Declaration that proclaimed human equality while tolerating slavery and the dispossession of Native peoples, the nation expanded across a continent, fractured in a civil war over human bondage, and reconstructed itself around abolition and citizenship. Industrialisation, mass immigration, and two world wars carried the United States to the centre of world affairs, where its mid-twentieth-century civil rights struggles and Cold War rivalry reshaped both its domestic order and the international system.

## Indigenous America before contact — c. 15,000 BCE – 1492 CE

For millennia before any European sail appeared, the land that would become the United States was home to hundreds of distinct nations, languages, and civilisations.

- c. 13,000 BCE: **Peopling of the Americas** — Humans were established across the Americas by at least 13,000–15,000 years ago, having entered via the Beringian land bridge and Pacific coast from Asia during the last Ice Age. [loc-overview]
- c. 1100 CE: **Cahokia at its height** — The Mississippian city of Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, reached its peak as one of the largest urban centres north of Mexico, anchored by monumental earthen mounds. [loc-overview]
- 1492 CE: **Columbus reaches the Caribbean** — Christopher Columbus's landfall opened sustained contact between Europe and the Americas, beginning an era of conquest, colonisation, and devastating disease for Indigenous peoples. [brit-jamestown]

## European colonisation and the Thirteen Colonies — 1492 – 1763

European empires planted rival colonies along the Atlantic seaboard, building societies on Indigenous land and, increasingly, on the labour of enslaved Africans.

- 1607: **Founding of Jamestown** — English settlers established Jamestown in Virginia, the first permanent English settlement in North America, financed by the Virginia Company of London. [brit-jamestown]
- August 1619: **First Africans landed in Virginia** — An English ship brought roughly twenty enslaved Africans to Point Comfort, Virginia, marking the beginning of African slavery in the English mainland colonies. [nps-1619]
- 1620: **Pilgrims found Plymouth** — Separatist Pilgrims aboard the Mayflower established Plymouth Colony, the first enduring European settlement in New England. [brit-plymouth, brit-mayflower]
- 1754–1763: **French and Indian War** — Britain's victory in the North American theatre of the Seven Years' War expelled France from the continent but left Britain heavily indebted, prompting new colonial taxes. [loc-overview]

Figures:

- Powhatan (Wahunsenacawh) (d. 1618) — Paramount chief of the Powhatan confederacy. Leader of the alliance of Algonquian-speaking peoples whose lands surrounded Jamestown, navigating trade, diplomacy, and conflict with the early English colonists.

## Revolution and the founding of the Republic — 1763 – 1815

Resistance to British taxation grew into a war of independence and the creation of a new kind of state: a large federal republic governed by a written constitution.

- 4 July 1776: **Declaration of Independence** — The Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, formally announcing the separation of the thirteen colonies from Great Britain. [nara-declaration]
- 1783: **Treaty of Paris** — Great Britain recognised the independence of the United States, ending the Revolutionary War and ceding territory east of the Mississippi. [loc-overview]
- 21 June 1788: **Constitution ratified** — With New Hampshire's ratification the Constitution took effect; the federal government began operating on 4 March 1789 and Washington was inaugurated that April. [nara-declaration]
- 1803: **Louisiana Purchase** — The United States bought France's Louisiana territory for $15 million, roughly doubling the nation's size and opening the trans-Mississippi West. [brit-lewis-clark]

Figures:

- George Washington (1732–1799) — Commander-in-chief and first U.S. president. Led the Continental Army to victory in the Revolution and, as the nation's first president (1789–1797), set enduring precedents for the office before relinquishing power voluntarily.
- Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826) — Principal author of the Declaration; third president. Drafted the Declaration of Independence and, as president, completed the Louisiana Purchase. A champion of liberty who nonetheless held people in slavery, he embodied the new republic's central contradiction.

## Expansion, slavery, and the Civil War — 1815 – 1865

Continental expansion, the displacement of Native nations, and an irrepressible conflict over slavery culminated in the bloodiest war in American history.

- 1838: **Trail of Tears** — Under the Indian Removal Act, the United States forcibly marched the Cherokee and other southeastern nations west of the Mississippi; thousands died en route. [loc-overview]
- 1861–1865: **American Civil War** — War between the Union and the secessionist Confederacy, ignited at Fort Sumter, became the deadliest conflict in U.S. history and decided the fate of slavery. [brit-civil-war]
- 1 January 1863: **Emancipation Proclamation** — President Lincoln declared enslaved people in the rebelling states 'forever free,' redefining the war's purpose and clearing the way for Black enlistment in the Union army. [nara-emancipation]
- 6 December 1865: **Thirteenth Amendment abolishes slavery** — Ratified after the Union victory, the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States. [nara-13th]

Figures:

- Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) — 16th president of the United States. Led the Union through the Civil War, issued the Emancipation Proclamation, and championed the Thirteenth Amendment before his assassination in April 1865.
- Frederick Douglass (c. 1818–1895) — Abolitionist, orator, and statesman. Escaped from slavery to become the era's foremost Black abolitionist, advancing emancipation and equal citizenship through his writing, speeches, and counsel to Lincoln.

## Reconstruction, industrial rise, and the World Wars — 1865 – 1945

Emancipation gave way to a contested Reconstruction, an industrial transformation fed by mass immigration, and two world wars that propelled the nation to global power.

- 1868: **Fourteenth Amendment** — Ratified during Reconstruction, it granted citizenship to all persons born in the United States and guaranteed equal protection of the laws. [loc-overview]
- 1869: **Transcontinental railroad completed** — The joining of the rails at Promontory, Utah, linked the Atlantic and Pacific coasts, accelerating settlement, commerce, and industrial growth. [loc-overview]
- 1929: **Great Depression** — The stock market crash of 1929 triggered a decade-long economic collapse, answered by Roosevelt's New Deal programmes of relief, recovery, and reform. [loc-overview]
- 7 December 1941: **Attack on Pearl Harbor** — Japan's surprise attack on the U.S. naval base in Hawaii brought the United States into the Second World War. [nara-pearl-harbor]

Figures:

- Franklin D. Roosevelt (1882–1945) — 32nd president of the United States. Led the nation through the Great Depression with the New Deal and through most of the Second World War, the only president elected to four terms.

## Superpower, civil rights, and the modern era — 1945 – present

A Cold War superpower wrestled with its unfinished promise of equality, reshaping itself through the civil rights movement and decades of social and technological change.

- 1954: **Brown v. Board of Education** — The Supreme Court ruled that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional, a landmark in the legal assault on Jim Crow. [loc-overview]
- 2 July 1964: **Civil Rights Act** — Signed by President Johnson, the Act outlawed discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex, or national origin in employment and public accommodations. [brit-civil-rights-act]
- 6 August 1965: **Voting Rights Act** — The Act banned racial discrimination in voting and authorised federal oversight of elections, dramatically expanding Black political participation. [brit-civil-rights-act]
- 1969: **Apollo 11 Moon landing** — American astronauts became the first humans to walk on the Moon, a defining achievement of the Cold War space race. [loc-overview]
- 1991: **End of the Cold War** — The dissolution of the Soviet Union left the United States as the world's sole superpower and reshaped the international order. [loc-overview]

Figures:

- Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968) — Civil rights leader. The most prominent leader of the nonviolent civil rights movement, whose campaigns helped secure the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts before his assassination in 1968.
