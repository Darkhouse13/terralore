# The Chronicle of Togo: From the Ewe exodus out of walled Notsé and the mud tower-houses of the Batammariba to the German 'model colony', a partitioned mandate, and a half-century ruled by one family.

Founding: Independence from France — 27 April 1960. On 27 April 1960 the United Nations Trust Territory of French Togoland became the independent Republic of Togo, with Sylvanus Olympio — leader of the nationalist Comité de l'Unité Togolaise and an economist trained at the London School of Economics — as its head of government and, from 1961, its first president. Independence here was the culmination of a long and tangled history: the migrations that brought the Ewe, Kabye, Mina and Guin into the land; the slaving ports of the coast; the German colony of Togoland proclaimed in 1884; and the partition of that colony after the First World War into British and French mandates, of which the French portion became modern Togo. The new state was small and ethnically divided between a southern, largely Ewe coast and a northern interior, a fault line that would shape its politics for decades. Within three years Olympio was dead, killed in a military coup, and the country entered an era of authoritarian and dynastic rule that has outlasted most of its post-colonial peers.

A narrow ribbon of land running inland from a short Atlantic coast, Togo was peopled long before its borders existed: the Ewe, who by tradition fled the walled town of Notsé to disperse across what is now Ghana, Togo and Benin; the Kabye and related farmers of the rugged north; and the Mina and Guin of the coast, refugees from the wars of the Gold Coast who founded trading towns such as Aného. That coast was the western 'Slave Coast', where Aného served as a slaving and commercial port in the Atlantic trade. In 1884 the German explorer Gustav Nachtigal secured a protectorate treaty on this shore, and at the Berlin Conference the powers recognised the German colony of Togoland, later promoted as a 'model colony' but in reality sustained by taxation and forced labour. At the outbreak of the First World War in August 1914 a swift Anglo-French invasion ended German rule, and the territory was partitioned into British and French mandates of the League of Nations. British Togoland voted in 1956 to join the soon-independent Ghana, while French Togoland became the independent Republic of Togo on 27 April 1960 under Sylvanus Olympio. Olympio's assassination in January 1963 — often called the first coup of post-independence Africa — opened the way to the long rule of Gnassingbé Eyadéma from 1967 to 2005, after which his son Faure Gnassingbé took power.

## Peoples, migrations and the land — c. 1200 – 1600

Before any state called Togo existed, the land was settled by the Ewe of the south, the Kabye and related farmers of the north, and — in the rugged Atakora hills — the Batammariba, whose mud tower-houses still rise from the Koutammakou landscape.

- by the 13th century: **Ewe ancestors present in the region** — By oral tradition and scholarly reconstruction, the ancestors of the Ewe had moved into the Togo–Ghana region from the east, settling around Tado and later the town of Notsé before their dispersal. [ewe-exodus-wiki, britannica-togo, togo-wiki]
- c. 15th–17th centuries: **The Ewe exodus from Notsé** — Ewe tradition tells of escape from the walled town of Notsé and the tyranny of King Agokoli, after which the people dispersed westward and southward — a foundational narrative of Ewe identity across Ghana, Togo and Benin. [ewe-exodus-wiki, britannica-togo]
- before c. 1600: **Northern peoples and the Batammariba** — The Kabye, Tem, Bassar and other groups settled the northern hills, while the Batammariba built the takienta mud tower-houses of the Koutammakou, living in largely decentralised, clan-based communities. [unesco-koutammakou, smarthistory-takienta, kabye-wiki, history-of-togo-wiki]

## The coast, the Guin and the Slave Coast — 1600 – 1884

Refugees from the wars of the Gold Coast founded the coastal town of Aného, which grew into a slaving and commercial port on the stretch of shore that Europeans called the Slave Coast.

- late 17th century: **Founding of Aného (Petit-Popo)** — Ane settlers fleeing conflict around Elmina in present-day Ghana founded Aného on the Togolese coast; it grew into the region's chief commercial and slaving port and would later briefly serve as a colonial capital. [aneho-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 17th–early 19th centuries: **The Slave Coast and the Atlantic trade** — The Togolese shore formed part of the 'Slave Coast', through whose ports including Aného enslaved Africans were trafficked to the Americas — a sustained system of violence and dispossession that reshaped coastal and interior societies. [aneho-wiki, britannica-togo]
- mid-19th century: **From slaving to 'legitimate' trade** — As the slave trade was suppressed, commerce on the coast turned to palm oil and other goods, and German and other European merchant houses established themselves at Aného and along the shore. [britannica-togo, togoland-wiki]

## German Togoland, the 'model colony' — 1884 – 1914

After Gustav Nachtigal secured a protectorate treaty on the coast in 1884, Germany built the colony of Togoland — promoted as a profitable 'model colony', but sustained by heavy taxation and forced labour.

- 5 July 1884: **Nachtigal proclaims a protectorate** — Gustav Nachtigal signed a protectorate treaty on the coast, associated with a ruler styled Mlapa, declaring German authority over the shore — the founding act of the colony of Togoland. [togoland-wiki, britannica-togo, nachtigal-wiki]
- 1884–85: **The Berlin Conference recognises Togoland** — At the Berlin Conference the European powers laid down rules for partitioning Africa and recognised the German protectorate that became the colony of Togoland. [togoland-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 1897: **Lomé becomes the colonial capital** — The seat of German administration moved to Lomé, which grew into the colony's main port and commercial centre, served by railways and roads built across Togoland. [aneho-wiki, togoland-wiki]
- c. 1884–1914: **A 'model colony' built on forced labour** — Germany promoted Togoland as a profitable Musterkolonie, but its plantations, railways and balanced budget depended on heavy taxation and the coercion of forced African labour. [togoland-wiki, germany-togo-wiki, britannica-togo]

## Conquest, partition and the mandates — 1914 – 1956

At the outbreak of the First World War a swift Anglo-French invasion ended German rule in 1914; the territory was then split into British and French mandates, and in 1956 British Togoland voted to join Ghana.

- August 1914: **Anglo-French conquest of Togoland** — At the start of the First World War, British and French forces invaded German Togoland; after destroying the Kamina wireless station, the German garrison surrendered on 26 August 1914. [togoland-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 1919–1922: **Partition into British and French mandates** — Togoland was divided between Britain and France and formalised by the League of Nations as two mandates — British Togoland in the west and French Togoland in the east — splitting the Ewe and other peoples. [togoland-wiki, britishtogoland-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 9 May 1956: **British Togoland votes to join Ghana** — In a UN-supervised plebiscite, about 58 per cent of British Togoland's voters chose union with the Gold Coast; the territory was integrated into Ghana, which became independent in 1957. [britishtogoland-plebiscite-wiki, britishtogoland-wiki, history-of-togo-wiki]

## Independence and the killing of Olympio — 1956 – 1967

French Togoland became the independent Republic of Togo on 27 April 1960 under Sylvanus Olympio — whose assassination in January 1963 is often called the first coup of post-independence Africa.

- 1958: **Olympio's CUT wins the elections** — Sylvanus Olympio's nationalist CUT defeated the pro-French parties in the 1958 elections, and Olympio became head of government of the Trust Territory of French Togoland. [olympio-wiki, frenchtogoland-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 27 April 1960: **Independence of the Republic of Togo** — French Togoland became the fully independent Republic of Togo, ending UN trusteeship; the United States recognised the new state the same day, and Olympio was elected its first president in 1961. [olympio-wiki, frenchtogoland-wiki, statedept-togo, aaregistry-togo, britannica-togo]
- 13 January 1963: **Assassination of Sylvanus Olympio** — Demobilised soldiers, among them Étienne Gnassingbé Eyadéma, killed President Olympio in Lomé — widely cited as the first military coup against an elected leader in independent sub-Saharan Africa. [olympio-wiki, eyadema-wiki]
- 1963: **Grunitzky installed as president** — After the coup, Nicolas Grunitzky, Olympio's brother-in-law and political rival, headed a civilian government, but his weak administration left the military as the country's decisive power. [britannica-togo, eyadema-wiki]

Figures:

- Sylvanus Olympio (1902–1963) — First President of Togo. London-educated economist and former Unilever manager who led the CUT to victory and brought Togo to independence in 1960; assassinated in January 1963 in what is often called the first coup of post-independence Africa.

## The Eyadéma era and the Gnassingbé dynasty — 1967 – present

Gnassingbé Eyadéma seized power in 1967 and ruled Togo for nearly four decades behind a one-party state; on his death in 2005 the army installed his son Faure Gnassingbé, who rules still.

- 13 January 1967: **Eyadéma seizes power** — Étienne Gnassingbé Eyadéma overthrew President Grunitzky in a bloodless coup and took the presidency, beginning a rule that would last nearly four decades. [eyadema-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 1969: **Founding of the RPT one-party state** — Eyadéma created the Rassemblement du Peuple Togolais as Togo's sole legal party, the institutional core of his personal autocracy and cult of personality. [eyadema-wiki, britannica-togo]
- 1990–1992: **Pro-democracy pressure and a new constitution** — A wave of unrest forced Eyadéma to accept a multiparty constitution in 1992, but he retained power through disputed elections and the loyalty of the army. [britannica-togo, eyadema-wiki]
- 5 February 2005: **Death of Eyadéma and dynastic succession** — Eyadéma died after nearly 38 years in power, then Africa's longest-serving ruler; the army installed his son Faure Gnassingbé, prompting an international outcry over the unconstitutional handover. [eyadema-wiki, faure-wiki]
- April 2005: **Faure Gnassingbé elected amid violence** — After ECOWAS pressure forced him to step down, Faure Gnassingbé won a disputed election; the contested result was followed by violence that UN estimates put at hundreds of deaths. [faure-wiki, britannica-togo]

Figures:

- Gnassingbé Eyadéma (1935–2005) — President of Togo, 1967–2005. Soldier who took part in the 1963 killing of Olympio, seized power in 1967, and ruled Togo for nearly four decades through the RPT one-party state — Africa's longest-serving leader at his death in 2005.
- Faure Gnassingbé (born 1966) — President of Togo from 2005. Son of Eyadéma, installed by the army on his father's death in 2005 and confirmed in a disputed election; he has retained power since, including through constitutional changes shifting Togo toward a parliamentary system.
