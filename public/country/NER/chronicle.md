# The Chronicle of Niger: From the green Sahara and the caravan sultanate of Aïr to a French colony forged in violence, an independent republic, and a Sahelian state of uranium, drought and coups.

Founding: Independence from France — 3 August 1960. The Republic of Niger became a sovereign state on 3 August 1960, when French rule ended and Hamani Diori, leader of the Nigerien Progressive Party, became the country's first president. That date sits atop a very deep past. Lakeside peoples buried their dead at Gobero in the Ténéré during the 'Green Sahara' some ten thousand years ago; the Tuareg Sultanate of Aïr rose around the caravan city of Agadez in the early fifteenth century; the Songhai Empire pushed into Aïr around 1500; and the Hausa Sultanate of Damagaram at Zinder and the influence of Kanem-Bornu shaped the south and east. The colonial territory itself was assembled by France after the violent Voulet-Chanoine mission of 1899, organised as a military territory and then a colony within French West Africa, with the desert north pacified only in the 1920s. Independence in 1960 grew out of the post-war reforms that gave France's African territories autonomy within the French Community before full sovereignty.

Niger, a vast landlocked country straddling the Sahara and the Sahel, holds one of Africa's longest human records. During the early Holocene 'Green Sahara', lakeside peoples — the Kiffian and the later Tenerean — lived, fished and buried their dead at Gobero, in what is now the bone-dry Ténéré, while engraved and painted rock art recorded a world of cattle, giraffes and elephants long since vanished. As the desert returned, the great Sahelian trade and political networks reached into the region: the Tuareg founded the Sultanate of Aïr around the trading city of Agadez, a hub of the trans-Saharan caravan trade in salt, dates, hides and enslaved people; the Songhai Empire pushed eastward into Aïr around 1500; the Hausa states and, later, the Sultanate of Damagaram at Zinder grew rich on commerce; and the empire of Kanem-Bornu cast its influence over the east. In 1899 the brutal Voulet-Chanoine column carried French conquest across the land, and Niger was organised as a colony within French West Africa, with the desert Tuareg subdued only by the 1920s. Niger became independent on 3 August 1960 under Hamani Diori, but its modern history has been shaped by recurring Sahel droughts and famines, the uranium mines of Arlit, repeated Tuareg rebellions, and a long cycle of military coups, the most recent in July 2023.

## The Green Sahara and the peoples of the Ténéré — c. 8000 BCE – 2500 BCE

When the Sahara was green and watered, lakeside communities fished, herded and buried their dead at Gobero, leaving the oldest known cemetery in the desert and a wealth of rock art recording a vanished world.

- c. 8000 BCE: **Burials begin at Gobero** — Beside a lake in the present-day Ténéré, the Kiffian people began burying their dead at Gobero, creating what archaeologists identify as the oldest known cemetery in the Sahara. [gobero-wiki, uchicago-gobero, sereno-gobero]
- c. 5th millennium BCE: **The Tenerean herders** — After a dry interval, the more lightly built Tenerean people reoccupied Gobero, herding cattle as the lake shallowed; their graves include the famous burial of a woman with two children, hands interlinked. [uchicago-gobero, sereno-gobero, gobero-wiki]
- Holocene: **Rock art of a green Sahara** — Across the Aïr and Ténéré, engraved and painted rock art recorded cattle, giraffes and elephants, documenting a watered landscape utterly unlike the desert that exists today. [nigerheritage-archaeology, hist-niger-wiki]
- c. 2500 BCE: **The desert returns** — As the climate dried, the lakes retreated and the Sahara hardened into desert, pushing human settlement and trade southward toward the Sahel. [hist-niger-wiki, nigerheritage-archaeology]

Figures:

- Paul Sereno (born 1957) — Palaeontologist and Explorer. American palaeontologist whose Sahara expeditions, while hunting dinosaur fossils such as Nigersaurus in the Ténéré, identified the Gobero burial ground and revealed the Kiffian and Tenerean peoples of the green Sahara.

## Aïr, the caravans, and the eastern reach of Songhai — c. 1400 – 1700

The Tuareg founded the Sultanate of Aïr around the trading city of Agadez, a crossroads of the trans-Saharan caravans in salt, dates and slaves, until the Songhai Empire pushed eastward into the mountains around 1500.

- early 15th century (c. 1405): **Founding of the Sultanate of Aïr** — Tuareg clans of the Aïr massif organised the Sultanate of Aïr, choosing a sultan to mediate their rivalries; its capital Agadez became a major trans-Saharan trading and religious centre. (Some accounts place the city's rise nearer 1449.) [sultanate-agadez-wiki, agadez-wiki, hist-niger-wiki]
- c. 1500–1501: **Songhai pushes into Aïr** — The Songhai emperor Askia Muhammad campaigned into the Aïr mountains and established a Songhai presence at Agadez to control the northern caravan markets, the empire's eastern reach into present-day Niger. [askia-britannica, songhai-lumen, hist-niger-wiki]
- 15th–19th centuries: **The salt caravans of Bilma** — The azalai salt caravans carried Bilma and Fachi salt and dates southward to the Hausa markets, making Agadez and the eastern oases pillars of the trans-Saharan trade alongside the traffic in enslaved people. [agadez-wiki, hist-niger-wiki]
- 2013: **Agadez inscribed by UNESCO** — The Historic Centre of Agadez — the mudbrick capital of the Sultanate of Aïr, with its tall earthen mosque minaret — was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognising its role in trans-Saharan trade and Islam. [agadez-wiki, worldheritage-agadez]

Figures:

- Askia Muhammad (reigned 1493–1528) — Emperor of Songhai. Ruler under whom the Songhai Empire reached its greatest extent; he campaigned into the Aïr around 1500 and planted a Songhai colony at Agadez to command the trans-Saharan caravan markets of present-day Niger.

## Hausa states, Damagaram and the shadow of Bornu — c. 1500 – 1899

Across the southern savanna the Hausa states traded and warred, the empire of Kanem-Bornu cast its influence over the east, and the Sultanate of Damagaram at Zinder rose from a Bornu vassal into a powerful trading state.

- c. 1731: **Founding of the Sultanate of Damagaram** — Muslim aristocrats founded the Sultanate of Damagaram around Zinder; beginning as a vassal of the declining empire of Bornu, it would expand to dominate much of the southeast of present-day Niger. [damagaram-wiki, africanhistory-damagaram]
- 19th century: **Zinder, a trans-Saharan hub** — Damagaram's capital Zinder grew rich astride the desert route linking Tripoli to Kano, becoming a walled and well-armed trading state and one of the strongest powers in the region. [damagaram-wiki, africanhistory-damagaram]
- early 19th century: **Sokoto jihad reshapes the Hausa lands** — The Sokoto Caliphate's rise to the south drove Hausa rivals such as Gobir and the Katsina rulers northward into present-day Niger, recasting the politics of the southern savanna. [hist-niger-wiki, britannica-niger-ref]
- mid-19th century: **Bornu's enduring influence** — The empire of Kanem-Bornu near Lake Chad continued to project Islamic culture and political weight over Niger's east, where many small states had begun as its tributaries. [hist-niger-wiki, britannica-niger-ref]

Figures:

- Tanimoune Dan Souleymane (reigned mid-19th century) — Sultan of Damagaram. Among the most powerful rulers of Damagaram, he expanded the sultanate's territory and military strength from Zinder, consolidating it as a leading commercial and military power of the southeast.

## Conquest, the Voulet-Chanoine column and colonial Niger — 1899 – 1960

French conquest reached the region with the murderous Voulet-Chanoine mission of 1899; Niger was organised as a colony within French West Africa, the desert Tuareg subdued only in the 1920s, until independence in 1960.

- 1899: **The Voulet-Chanoine mission** — A French military column under Captains Voulet and Chanoine drove across the region with extreme brutality, massacring the people of Birni-N'Konni on 8 May 1899 before disintegrating into mutiny and the murder of Colonel Klobb. [voulet-chanoine-wiki, ictj-niger, hist-niger-wiki]
- 1916–1917: **The Kaocen revolt at Agadez** — During the First World War the Tuareg leader Kaocen led a major revolt against French rule, besieging the garrison at Agadez before the rising was crushed. [hist-niger-wiki, britannica-niger-ref]
- 1922: **Niger becomes a French colony** — After the desert Tuareg were finally subdued in the early 1920s, Niger was constituted as a colony within French West Africa, administered through the federation's governor-general in Dakar. [hist-niger-wiki, britannica-niger-ref]
- 1958: **Autonomous republic within the French Community** — In the 1958 referendum Niger voted to become a self-governing republic within the French Community, with Hamani Diori emerging at the head of its government on the road to independence. [diori-britannica, diori-blackpast, hist-niger-wiki]

Figures:

- Sarraounia (fl. 1899) — Hausa queen and war-leader of Lougou. Animist priestess-queen of the Azna of Lougou who led armed resistance against the Voulet-Chanoine column in 1899, becoming a lasting symbol of Nigerien resistance to colonial conquest.
- Kaocen Ag Mohammed (c. 1880–1919) — Tuareg revolt leader. Tuareg leader who launched a major anti-colonial revolt in 1916–1917, besieging French-held Agadez, before the rising was suppressed and he was killed.

## Independence, uranium and the long droughts — 1960 – 1991

Niger gained independence under Hamani Diori in 1960, opened the great uranium mines of Arlit, and was battered by the Sahel droughts, whose famines helped topple Diori in the 1974 coup that brought Seyni Kountché to power.

- 3 August 1960: **Niger gains independence** — Niger became a sovereign republic on 3 August 1960 with Hamani Diori as its first president; the United States recognised it the same day. Diori established a one-party state closely tied to France. [statedept-niger, diori-britannica, diori-blackpast]
- 1968–1974: **The Great Sahel drought** — A catastrophic drought devastated Niger's crops and herds; the famine of this period and the 1980s killed many and left hundreds of thousands dependent on food aid, deepening the crisis of the Diori regime. [sahel-drought-env, hist-niger-wiki]
- 1971: **Uranium mining begins at Arlit** — After deposits were developed from 1968 through the company SOMAÏR, the open-pit mine at Arlit began commercial uranium production in 1971, founding the industry that became Niger's leading export. [world-nuclear-niger, somair-wiki]
- 15 April 1974: **Kountché's coup ousts Diori** — Amid famine, corruption allegations over relief supplies, and a dispute with France over uranium prices, Lieutenant-Colonel Seyni Kountché overthrew President Diori, beginning thirteen years of military rule. [coup-1974-wiki, hist-niger-wiki, diori-britannica]

Figures:

- Hamani Diori (1916–1989) — First President of Niger (1960–1974). Nationalist leader and Pan-Africanist who led Niger to independence in 1960 and governed as its first president, maintaining close ties with France until he was overthrown in the 1974 coup amid drought and discontent.
- Seyni Kountché (1931–1987) — Military head of state (1974–1987). Army officer who seized power in the 1974 coup and ruled through a Supreme Military Council until his death, presiding over the uranium economy and a tightly controlled state.

## Democracy, Tuareg rebellions and the cycle of coups — 1991 – present

Since the early 1990s Niger has swung between fragile democracy and military rule, shaken by Tuareg rebellions in the north, jihadist insecurity across the Sahel, and a chain of coups culminating in the overthrow of President Bazoum in July 2023.

- 1991–1993: **Transition to multiparty democracy** — A 1991 national conference opened Niger's politics; elections in 1993 brought the first freely chosen government, beginning a turbulent experiment in multiparty rule. [hist-niger-wiki, britannica-niger-ref]
- 1990–1995: **First Tuareg rebellion** — Tuareg fighters in the north rebelled over marginalisation and the distribution of uranium wealth; the rising was ended by a 1995 peace accord promising decentralisation. [tuareg-90s-wiki, minorityrights-tuareg]
- 2007–2009: **Second Tuareg rebellion** — The Niger Movement for Justice (MNJ) launched a new insurgency, attacking the army and the uranium-mining zone around Agadez and Arlit, before a settlement was reached in 2009. [tuareg-2007-wiki, minorityrights-tuareg]
- 2010: **Coup ousts President Tandja** — The army removed President Mamadou Tandja after he manoeuvred to extend his term, one of a series of military interventions that punctuated Niger's democratic experiment. [hist-niger-wiki, coup-2023-wiki]
- 26 July 2023: **Coup against President Bazoum** — The presidential guard detained elected president Mohamed Bazoum and General Abdourahamane Tchiani proclaimed a military junta — Niger's fifth coup since independence — drawing condemnation from ECOWAS and the West. [coup-2023-wiki, hrw-niger-2023]

Figures:

- Mohamed Bazoum (born 1960) — President of Niger (2021–2023). Elected in 2021 in Niger's first transfer of power between elected presidents, he was overthrown and detained by his own presidential guard in the coup of 26 July 2023.
- Abdourahamane Tchiani (born c. 1960) — Military junta leader. Commander of the presidential guard who led the July 2023 coup against President Bazoum and proclaimed himself head of the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland.
