Founding: Independence from Haiti — 27 February 1844. The Dominican Republic dates its birth as a sovereign nation to 27 February 1844, when patriots of the secret society La Trinitaria, founded by Juan Pablo Duarte in 1838, rose in Santo Domingo and expelled the Haitian authorities who had governed the eastern part of the island since the unification of 1822. The conspirators proclaimed the independence of a new Dominican Republic, separate both from Haiti and from Spain, and the date remains the country's national independence day. Duarte, regarded as the father of Dominican independence, was abroad seeking support when the rising succeeded and was soon pushed aside by rival caudillos; he died in exile in Caracas in 1876, but his vision of a free Dominican state endured.

The Dominican Republic occupies the eastern two-thirds of Hispaniola, the island the Taino called Quisqueya, where Christopher Columbus made landfall in 1492 and the Spanish founded Santo Domingo, the oldest permanent European city in the Americas and seat of Spain's first New World power. After three centuries in which the island was divided between Spanish Santo Domingo and French Saint-Domingue, the colony experienced a brief Ephemeral Independence in 1821 before being unified with Haiti, and won its lasting independence from Haiti on 27 February 1844 under Juan Pablo Duarte and the secret society La Trinitaria. The turbulent century that followed brought a brief return to Spanish rule and the War of Restoration, a United States military occupation from 1916 to 1924, and the thirty-one-year dictatorship of Rafael Trujillo, whose regime included the 1937 massacre of Haitians along the border. Since the 1965 U.S. intervention, the Balaguer years, and the transition to democracy, the country has grown into one of the strongest economies in Latin America and the Caribbean, driven by tourism, services, free-trade-zone manufacturing, and remittances.

## Quisqueya, Columbus, and the First Colony — Pre-1492 - 1586

Long before European sails appeared, the Taino farmed and fished the island they called Quisqueya; Columbus's landfall in 1492 made Hispaniola the cradle of Spain's American empire and Santo Domingo its first city.

- 5 Dec 1492: **Columbus reaches Hispaniola** — On his first voyage Christopher Columbus lands on the island the Taino call Quisqueya and claims it for Spain as La Isla Espanola, opening the European conquest of the Americas. [britannica-dr, britannica-hispaniola]
- 1496 CE: **Founding of Santo Domingo** — Bartholomew Columbus founds Santo Domingo on the Ozama River; refounded soon after, it becomes the oldest permanent European city in the Americas and the seat of Spanish power. [britannica-santo-domingo, unesco-santo-domingo]
- 1538 CE: **First university in the Americas** — A royal charter establishes the University of Santo Domingo, the first university in the New World, in a city already home to its first cathedral and hospital. [britannica-santo-domingo, unesco-santo-domingo]
- 1586 CE: **Drake sacks Santo Domingo** — The English privateer Francis Drake captures and ransoms the decayed capital, a sign of how far the once-central colony had fallen as imperial wealth moved to the mainland. [britannica-santo-domingo]

Figures:

- Christopher Columbus (1451-1506) — Genoese navigator in Spanish service. Reached Hispaniola on 5 December 1492 and founded the first Spanish settlements on the island, making it the base of Spain's expansion into the Americas. His brother Bartholomew founded Santo Domingo in 1496.

## A Divided Island and the French Era — 1586 - 1821

While Spanish Santo Domingo languished, French buccaneers settled the island's neglected west, and by treaty Hispaniola was split between two empires, with the whole island briefly passing under French and Haitian control.

- 1697 CE: **Treaty of Ryswick** — Spain formally recognises French control of the western third of Hispaniola, creating the colony of Saint-Domingue beside Spanish Santo Domingo. [britannica-hispaniola]
- 22 Jul 1795: **Treaty of Basel** — By the Treaty of Basel, Spain cedes the colony of Santo Domingo to France, although the upheavals of the Haitian Revolution delay any effective handover. [britannica-basel, britannica-dr]
- 1804 CE: **Independence of Haiti** — After defeating France, the formerly enslaved people of Saint-Domingue proclaim Haiti, the first nation founded by a successful slave revolt, reshaping the whole island. [britannica-hispaniola]
- Dec 1821: **Ephemeral Independence** — Governor Jose Nunez de Caceres declares Santo Domingo independent of Spain, hoping to join Gran Colombia; the fragile state lasts only a matter of weeks. [britannica-dr]

## Haitian Rule and Dominican Independence — 1822 - 1865

Haiti unified the whole island for twenty-two years until the patriots of La Trinitaria proclaimed an independent Dominican Republic in 1844, only for the young nation to be annexed back to Spain and win it again in the War of Restoration.

- 1822 CE: **Haitian unification of the island** — President Jean-Pierre Boyer occupies the east and unifies all Hispaniola under Haiti for twenty-two years, abolishing slavery but alienating much of the Dominican population. [britannica-dr]
- 16 Jul 1838: **Founding of La Trinitaria** — Juan Pablo Duarte founds the clandestine society La Trinitaria, organised in three-man cells, to undermine Haitian rule and build a movement for Dominican independence. [britannica-duarte]
- 27 Feb 1844: **Dominican independence from Haiti** — The Trinitarios rise in Santo Domingo, expel the Haitian garrison, and proclaim the Dominican Republic, the date still marked as national independence day. [britannica-dr, britannica-duarte]
- 18 Mar 1861: **Annexation to Spain** — President Pedro Santana returns the republic to Spanish rule, an unprecedented step that soon provokes fierce popular resistance. [britannica-dr, loc-country-study]
- 1863-1865: **War of Restoration** — A guerrilla war against Spanish rule, launched from Santiago in 1863, forces Spain to abandon the colony in 1865 and restores Dominican independence. [britannica-dr, loc-country-study]

Figures:

- Juan Pablo Duarte (1813-1876) — Father of Dominican independence. Founder of the secret society La Trinitaria in 1838 and the leading ideologue of Dominican nationhood. Abroad seeking support when the 1844 rising succeeded, he was sidelined by rival caudillos and died in exile in Caracas, honoured today as a founding father.

## U.S. Occupation and the Trujillo Era — 1865 - 1961

Chronic instability and debt drew in the United States, which occupied the country from 1916 to 1924 and trained the guard that produced Rafael Trujillo, whose thirty-one-year dictatorship brought order and modernisation alongside terror and the 1937 massacre of Haitians.

- 1916-1924: **U.S. military occupation** — The United States occupies the Dominican Republic, governing it for eight years, suppressing an eastern insurgency, and creating the national guard from which Trujillo would emerge. [history-state-occupation, britannica-dr]
- 1930 CE: **Trujillo seizes power** — Rafael Trujillo, commander of the army built under the occupation, takes the presidency and establishes a personal dictatorship that lasts thirty-one years. [britannica-trujillo]
- Oct 1937: **The Parsley Massacre** — Trujillo orders the army to kill Haitians and Haitian-descended people along the border; the death toll, carried out largely by machete, is estimated in the thousands to tens of thousands. [britannica-parsley-massacre, britannica-trujillo]
- 30 May 1961: **Assassination of Trujillo** — Trujillo is ambushed and gunned down near Santo Domingo by conspirators including military officers, ending the long dictatorship and opening a contested transition. [britannica-trujillo]

Figures:

- Rafael Trujillo (1891-1961) — Dictator of the Dominican Republic. Trained in the U.S.-created national guard, he ruled the country from 1930 until his assassination in 1961 as an absolute dictator. His regime brought modernisation and growth alongside pervasive repression and the 1937 massacre of Haitians.

## Intervention, Balaguer, and Democracy — 1961 - 1996

Trujillo's death opened a struggle between reform and reaction that drew a second U.S. military intervention in 1965, after which Joaquin Balaguer dominated Dominican politics for three decades as the country edged toward genuine democracy.

- Dec 1962: **Election of Juan Bosch** — In the first free elections in decades, the reformist writer Juan Bosch wins the presidency, only to be overthrown by the military after seven months in 1963. [britannica-bosch, britannica-dr]
- Apr 1965: **U.S. intervention and civil war** — A Constitutionalist rising to restore Bosch sparks civil war; President Johnson sends some 22,000 U.S. troops in Operation Power Pack, the second U.S. intervention of the century. [history-state-milestones, britannica-dr]
- 1966 CE: **Balaguer wins the presidency** — In U.S.-backed elections after the intervention, the conservative Joaquin Balaguer defeats Bosch, beginning a dominance of Dominican politics that lasts three decades. [britannica-balaguer, history-state-milestones]
- 1978 CE: **Peaceful transfer of power** — Balaguer accepts an opposition electoral victory and hands over the presidency, a landmark in the country's slow transition to democracy. [britannica-balaguer]
- 1994-1996: **End of the Balaguer era** — After disputed 1994 elections, pressure forces Balaguer to shorten his term, opening the way to the competitive multiparty democracy of the modern republic. [britannica-balaguer]

Figures:

- Juan Bosch (1909-2001) — President, writer, and reformer. A celebrated writer and longtime opponent of Trujillo who won the first free elections after the dictatorship in 1962 and was overthrown in 1963. His attempted restoration in 1965 triggered the civil war and U.S. intervention.
- Joaquin Balaguer (1907-2002) — President of the Dominican Republic. A former functionary of the Trujillo regime who became the dominant figure of Dominican politics, serving as president across the period 1966-1996 and presiding over economic growth, authoritarian control, and an eventual democratic transition.

## The Modern Tourism Republic — 1996 - present

Stable democracy and one of the region's fastest-growing economies have transformed the Dominican Republic into the Caribbean's leading tourist destination, even as inequality and the fraught relationship with Haiti remain enduring challenges.

- 2004 CE: **Tourism-led growth takes off** — The Dominican Republic emerges as the Caribbean's leading tourist destination, with Punta Cana's resorts and Santo Domingo's colonial city anchoring a fast-growing services economy. [worldbank-dr, britannica-dr]
- 2013 CE: **Citizenship ruling on Haitian descent** — A constitutional court ruling affecting the nationality of Dominicans of Haitian descent draws international criticism and underscores the fraught relationship with Haiti. [britannica-dr]
- 2020 CE: **Abinader and a new political era** — Luis Abinader of the Modern Revolutionary Party wins the presidency, continuing the peaceful alternation of power in a consolidated democracy. [britannica-dr]
- 2024 CE: **Among the region's top economies** — The Dominican Republic ranks among the fastest-growing economies in Latin America and the Caribbean, driven by tourism, manufacturing, mining, and remittances. [worldbank-dr]
