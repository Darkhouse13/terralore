Founding: The Baptism of Poland under Mieszko I — 966. The conventional birth of the Polish state is dated to 966, when Mieszko I, duke of the Polanie and first historical ruler of the Piast dynasty, accepted Christianity from the Latin Church. The baptism, traditionally placed on 14 April 966, brought his realm into Western Christendom, helped shield it from forced conversion and absorption by the Holy Roman Empire, and is regarded as the founding act of the Polish nation. The state Mieszko consolidated, the Civitas Schinesghe, was the kernel of a kingdom that would endure in one form or another for a millennium. That continuity was shattered by the partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795, which wiped Poland from the map for 123 years, until independence was restored on 11 November 1918 in the wake of the First World War.

Poland's history is one of a nation that has repeatedly been erased from the map and remade by the will of its people. The Polish state emerged in the tenth century when the Piast duke Mieszko I accepted Christianity in 966, binding his realm to Latin Europe. Medieval Piast and Jagiellonian kings forged a powerful kingdom that, joined to Lithuania, became the vast Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, one of early modern Europe's largest states and an experiment in noble republicanism. Decline, foreign interference and the paralysis of its own institutions led to the partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795, when Russia, Prussia and Austria divided Poland out of existence for 123 years. Independence was restored in 1918, only for the Second Republic to be crushed in 1939 by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union; the German occupation made Polish soil the site of the Holocaust and of staggering civilian death. Liberated into Soviet domination, Poland endured four decades of communist rule until the Solidarity movement helped bring it down, and the country returned to democracy, joining the European Union in 2004.

## The Birth of the Piast State and the Christianization of Poland — c. 960 – 1138

A duke of the Polanie embraced Latin Christianity and bound a scattering of Slavic tribes into a state that would bear the name of Poland.

- 966: **The Baptism of Poland** — Duke Mieszko I accepted Christianity in the Latin rite, conventionally dated to 14 April 966, bringing his realm into Western Christendom and marking the traditional birth of the Polish state. [mieszko-britannica, culture-baptism]
- 1000: **Congress of Gniezno** — Emperor Otto III made pilgrimage to Gniezno and an independent Polish church province was established, recognising the young state's standing within Christian Europe. [poland-history-britannica]
- 1025: **Coronation of Boleslaw I the Brave** — Boleslaw I was crowned king at Gniezno, the first ruler to bear the royal title of Poland and to win recognition of the realm as a kingdom rather than a duchy. [poland-history-britannica, pma-crown]
- 1138: **Testament of Boleslaw III and fragmentation** — The dying Boleslaw III Wrymouth divided Poland among his sons, beginning nearly two centuries of fragmentation into rival duchies that weakened the state against its neighbours. [poland-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Mieszko I (c. 930 – 992) — First historical duke of Poland. The founder of the Polish state, Mieszko I united the lands of the Polanie and accepted Christianity in 966, anchoring Poland to Latin Europe. By his death his realm stretched from the Baltic toward the Carpathians.
- Boleslaw I the Brave (967 – 1025) — First king of Poland. Son of Mieszko I, Boleslaw expanded the kingdom in every direction and in 1025 obtained a royal crown, raising Poland to the rank of a kingdom shortly before his death.

## The Piast and Jagiellonian Kingdom — 1138 – 1569

Reunified by the last great Piast kings and joined to Lithuania through the marriage of a queen, Poland became a major power of late medieval Europe.

- 1333–1370: **Reign of Casimir III the Great** — The last Piast king reformed the law, founded the University of Krakow in 1364, expanded the kingdom and welcomed Jewish settlers, leaving Poland prosperous and consolidated. [casimir-britannica, poland-history-britannica]
- 1385: **Union of Krewo and the Jagiellonian dynasty** — The agreement at Krewo arranged the marriage of Queen Jadwiga to Jogaila of Lithuania, who was baptised as Wladyslaw II Jagiello, joining Poland and Lithuania under a new dynasty. [poland-history-britannica, lublin-britannica]
- 15 Jul 1410: **Battle of Grunwald** — Polish and Lithuanian forces under Wladyslaw II Jagiello destroyed the army of the Teutonic Knights at Grunwald, halting the Order's eastward expansion in the Baltic. [grunwald-britannica]
- 1543: **Copernicus publishes 'On the Revolutions'** — The Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus published his heliocentric theory, placing the Sun rather than the Earth at the centre of the cosmos and helping to launch modern astronomy. [copernicus-britannica]

Figures:

- Casimir III the Great (1310 – 1370) — Last Piast king of Poland. The only Polish king honoured as 'the Great', Casimir reformed law and administration, founded the University of Krakow, fortified the realm and extended protection to Jewish communities, leaving a stronger and wealthier kingdom.
- Nicolaus Copernicus (1473 – 1543) — Astronomer and polymath. Born in Torun in royal Prussia, Copernicus formulated the heliocentric model of the solar system, published in 1543, a turning point in the history of science and one of Poland's foremost contributions to world culture.

## The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth: Golden Age and Decline — 1569 – 1795

A noble republic of two nations rose to dominate eastern Europe, pioneered religious tolerance and a written constitution, then was paralysed from within and partitioned out of existence.

- 1 Jul 1569: **Union of Lublin** — Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania united into a single federal state, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, ruled by an elected monarch and a common Sejm, one of Europe's largest powers. [lublin-britannica, lublin-unesco]
- 12 Sep 1683: **Relief of Vienna** — King John III Sobieski led the allied cavalry charge that broke the Ottoman siege of Vienna, marking the high point of the Commonwealth's military reputation in Europe. [sobieski-britannica]
- 1655–1660: **The Deluge** — A devastating Swedish invasion during the Second Northern War ravaged the Commonwealth, causing enormous loss of life and population and accelerating the state's long decline. [deluge-reference, poland-history-britannica]
- 1772–1795: **The Partitions of Poland** — In three stages (1772, 1793, 1795) Russia, Prussia and Austria divided the Commonwealth among themselves, despite the reforming Constitution of 3 May 1791, ending Polish statehood for 123 years. [partitions-britannica, constitution1791-reference]

Figures:

- John III Sobieski (1629 – 1696) — King of Poland and military commander. Elected king in 1674, Sobieski was the Commonwealth's most celebrated soldier-monarch, remembered above all for leading the relief of Vienna in 1683 against the Ottoman army.
- Tadeusz Kosciuszko (1746 – 1817) — General and national leader. A veteran of the American Revolutionary War, Kosciuszko led the 1794 national uprising against partition. Its defeat precipitated the final partition of 1795; he became an enduring symbol of the struggle for Polish freedom.

## The Partitions and the Long Fight for Independence — 1795 – 1918

For 123 years no Polish state existed, yet through risings, exile and culture the idea of Poland was kept alive until the First World War made independence possible again.

- 1815: **Congress of Vienna and the 'Congress Kingdom'** — The post-Napoleonic settlement re-divided the Polish lands and created a nominally autonomous Kingdom of Poland under the Russian tsar, entrenching foreign rule. [partitioned-poland-reference, partitions-britannica]
- 1830–1831: **The November Uprising** — Poles in the Russian partition rose against the tsar; after initial successes the rising was crushed, bringing reprisals, the end of the Kingdom's autonomy and a wave of political exile. [partitioned-poland-reference]
- 1863–1864: **The January Uprising** — A protracted guerrilla insurrection against Russian rule ended in defeat and severe repression, including executions, deportations to Siberia and intensified Russification. [partitioned-poland-reference]
- 11 Nov 1918: **Restoration of independence** — As the partitioning empires collapsed at the end of the First World War, an independent Polish state was proclaimed under Jozef Pilsudski, ending 123 years without statehood. [partitioned-poland-reference, partitions-britannica]

Figures:

- Frederic Chopin (1810 – 1849) — Composer and pianist. Born near Warsaw and exiled to Paris after the November Uprising, Chopin poured a Polish national feeling into his music. His polonaises and mazurkas became enduring emblems of a nation that had lost its state.
- Jozef Pilsudski (1867 – 1935) — Statesman and head of state. A socialist and conspirator turned military commander, Pilsudski organised Polish legions during the First World War and became the central figure of the reborn state, taking power on 11 November 1918.

## The Second Republic, the Second World War and the Nazi-Soviet Catastrophe — 1918 – 1945

Barely two decades of restored independence ended in the joint German and Soviet invasion of 1939, an occupation of unparalleled brutality and the destruction of Poland's Jewish community in the Holocaust.

- Aug 1920: **Battle of Warsaw** — Polish forces under Pilsudski halted the advance of the Soviet Red Army outside Warsaw, securing the independence of the young republic in the Polish-Soviet War. [pilsudski-britannica, partitions-britannica]
- 1 Sep 1939: **German and Soviet invasion of Poland** — Nazi Germany invaded on 1 September, starting the Second World War in Europe; the Soviet Union invaded from the east on 17 September, and the two powers again partitioned Poland. [invasion-ushmm, invasion-britannica]
- 1940–1945: **Auschwitz-Birkenau and the Holocaust on Polish soil** — Germany operated its largest concentration and extermination camp at Auschwitz-Birkenau, where some 1.1 million people, overwhelmingly Jews, were murdered as part of the genocide carried out across occupied Poland. [auschwitz-unesco, polish-victims-ushmm]
- 1 Aug 1944: **The Warsaw Uprising** — The Polish Home Army rose against the German occupiers in Warsaw; after 63 days the rising was crushed, much of the city was destroyed and tens of thousands of civilians were killed. [polish-victims-ushmm]

Figures:

- Janusz Korczak (1878 – 1942) — Educator, physician and author. Director of a Jewish orphanage in Warsaw, Korczak refused offers of his own safety and accompanied the children of his orphanage to their deaths at the Treblinka extermination camp, becoming a symbol of moral courage under the occupation.
- Witold Pilecki (1901 – 1948) — Resistance officer. A cavalry officer of the Polish underground, Pilecki deliberately allowed himself to be imprisoned in Auschwitz to gather intelligence and organise resistance, later compiling some of the earliest detailed reports on the camp. He was executed by Poland's communist regime after the war.

## Communist Poland, Solidarity and the Modern Republic — 1945 – present

Liberated from German occupation into Soviet domination, Poland endured four decades of communist rule before Solidarity helped bring the system down and restored a democratic, European Poland.

- 1947: **Establishment of communist rule** — Backed by the Soviet Union and rigged elections, a communist government consolidated power, founding the Polish People's Republic within the Soviet bloc. [walesa-britannica, solidarity-britannica]
- Aug 1980: **Birth of Solidarity** — Strikes at the Gdansk shipyard led by Lech Walesa produced Solidarity, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, which rapidly grew to about ten million members. [solidarity-britannica, walesa-britannica]
- 13 Dec 1981: **Martial law** — The communist authorities imposed martial law, banned Solidarity and interned its leaders, including Walesa, driving the movement underground for the rest of the decade. [solidarity-britannica, walesa-britannica]
- 4 Jun 1989: **Semi-free elections and the fall of communism** — Round Table talks produced partly free elections that Solidarity won overwhelmingly, ending communist rule in Poland and helping trigger the collapse of the Soviet bloc. [solidarity-britannica, walesa-britannica]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to the European Union** — Poland joined the European Union, completing its return to the West after the fall of communism and accelerating its economic and political integration into Europe. [poland-eu-gov]

Figures:

- Lech Walesa (born 1943) — Solidarity leader and President. An electrician at the Gdansk shipyard, Walesa led the strikes that created Solidarity and became its enduring symbol. Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983, he was elected president of a democratic Poland in 1990.
- Pope John Paul II (1920 – 2005) — Pope and moral leader. Born Karol Wojtyla in Wadowice, he became archbishop of Krakow and in 1978 the first Polish pope. His pilgrimages home galvanised national hope and lent decisive moral support to the Solidarity movement.
