Founding: Independence sealed at the Battle of Maipu — 5 April 1818. Chile formally declared its independence from Spain on 12 February 1818, but the new state's survival was decided on the battlefield. On 5 April 1818, near Santiago, an army of Chilean patriots and Argentine troops commanded by Jose de San Martin, with Bernardo O'Higgins among its leaders, crushed the Spanish royalists at the Battle of Maipu. The six-hour fight left some 2,000 royalists dead and thousands captured and ended the struggle for Chilean independence on the mainland. The break had been prepared by the first national junta of 18 September 1810, formed after Napoleon's occupation of Spain shattered royal authority, and by San Martin's daring crossing of the Andes in 1817 and victory at Chacabuco. O'Higgins became Chile's first head of state as supreme director.

Chile's history reaches back to the Indigenous peoples who held the land for millennia: the Mapuche of the temperate south, who resisted outside rule for centuries, the Andean peoples of the desert north touched by the Inca, and, far across the Pacific, the Polynesian society of Rapa Nui who raised the great stone moai of Easter Island. Spanish forces under Pedro de Valdivia founded Santiago in 1541 and pressed south, only to meet fierce Mapuche resistance in the long Arauco War. Independence was won in the campaigns of Bernardo O'Higgins and Jose de San Martin and sealed at the Battle of Maipu in 1818, after which a stable republic grew wealthy on nitrates seized in the War of the Pacific (1879-1883). The democratic experiment of Salvador Allende ended in the military coup of 11 September 1973, opening seventeen years of dictatorship under Augusto Pinochet whose human-rights abuses were later documented by the Rettig and Valech commissions; democracy returned in 1990, and modern Chile has wrestled, through the protests of 2019 and a contested constitutional process, with the legacy of those years.

## Indigenous Peoples and the World of Rapa Nui — Pre-1500 - 1536

Long before the Spanish, the land of Chile was home to the Mapuche of the temperate south and the Andean peoples of the desert north, brushed by the expanding Inca, while three thousand kilometres out in the Pacific the Polynesian society of Rapa Nui raised the colossal stone moai of Easter Island.

- by c. 10,000 BCE: **Ancient peopling of the land** — Hunting and gathering peoples settle the long territory between the Andes and the Pacific over many millennia, the deep ancestors of the Mapuche and the Andean peoples of the north. [britannica-history-chile, britannica-mapuche]
- c. 10th-16th century: **The moai of Rapa Nui** — On Easter Island, the Polynesian society of Rapa Nui carves and raises some 900 colossal stone moai on hundreds of ceremonial platforms, an unrivalled monumental tradition later recognised by UNESCO. [unesco-rapa-nui]
- late 15th century: **Inca expansion reaches the Maule** — The Inca empire extends its influence into northern and central Chile but is unable to subdue the Mapuche, whose resistance checks imperial control near the Maule River. [britannica-history-chile, britannica-araucanian-wars]
- 1536-1537: **Almagro's expedition turns back** — Diego de Almagro leads the first Spanish entry into Chile as far as the Maule River; finding no gold or empire, the expedition returns to Peru, but the Spanish first clash with the Araucanians. [britannica-almagro, britannica-history-chile]

Figures:

- Diego de Almagro (c. 1475-1538) — Spanish conquistador. Partner and then rival of Francisco Pizarro who led the first Spanish expedition into Chile in 1536-37, reaching the Maule River before turning back, disappointed, to Peru.

## Conquest, Santiago, and the Long Arauco War — 1540 - 1810

Pedro de Valdivia founded Santiago in 1541 and carried Spanish rule south to the Biobio, but beyond that river the Mapuche fought the invaders to a standstill in the Arauco War, leaving the Captaincy General of Chile a poor and embattled frontier of empire for two and a half centuries.

- 12 February 1541: **Founding of Santiago** — Pedro de Valdivia founds Santiago de Nueva Extremadura in the central valley, the nucleus of Spanish Chile and its future capital. [britannica-valdivia, britannica-santiago]
- 1550 CE: **Conquest reaches the Biobio** — Valdivia extends Spanish rule south of the Biobio and founds Concepcion, pushing into the Mapuche heartland and igniting lasting resistance. [britannica-valdivia, britannica-araucanian-wars]
- December 1553: **Mapuche defeat and kill Valdivia** — At Tucapel, Mapuche forces led by Lautaro destroy a Spanish column and capture Valdivia, who is executed; the disaster opens the long Arauco War. [britannica-lautaro, britannica-araucanian-wars]
- 17th-18th century: **The frontier Captaincy General** — Chile is governed as a poor, militarised captaincy general of the Spanish empire, its southern frontier held against an independent Mapuche nation that the conquest never subdued. [britannica-history-chile, britannica-araucanian-wars]

Figures:

- Pedro de Valdivia (c. 1500-1553) — Conquistador and first governor. Spanish conqueror of Chile who founded Santiago in 1541 and extended Spanish rule to the Biobio before being captured and killed by the Mapuche at Tucapel in 1553.
- Lautaro (c. 1535-1557) — Mapuche war leader. Mapuche commander who, after serving in Valdivia's stables, escaped, unified Araucanian resistance, and defeated and killed the conquistador in 1553; revered as a Chilean national hero.

## Independence and the Building of a Republic — 1810 - 1879

The first national junta of 1810 began Chile's road to self-government; after defeat and reconquest, San Martin's army crossed the Andes, O'Higgins led the patriots to the founding victory at Maipu in 1818, and Chile built one of the most stable republics in nineteenth-century Latin America.

- 18 September 1810: **First national junta** — Creole leaders in Santiago form a governing junta after the fall of the Spanish king, the founding act of Chilean self-government and the national day. [britannica-history-chile]
- 12 February 1817: **Crossing the Andes and Chacabuco** — San Martin's Army of the Andes crosses the cordillera and defeats the royalists at Chacabuco, opening the road to Santiago and the liberation of Chile. [britannica-san-martin, britannica-ohiggins]
- 5 April 1818: **The Battle of Maipu** — Patriot forces under San Martin, with O'Higgins, crush the Spanish royalists near Santiago, sealing Chilean independence on the mainland weeks after its formal declaration. [britannica-maipu, britannica-ohiggins]
- 1833 CE: **The Constitution of 1833** — A conservative constitution shaped by Diego Portales establishes a strong centralised presidency, anchoring decades of unusual political stability. [britannica-history-chile]

Figures:

- Bernardo O'Higgins (1778-1842) — Liberator and first head of state. Leader of the Chilean independence movement who returned with San Martin in 1817, helped win Chacabuco and Maipu, and governed as supreme director until 1823, revered as the father of the nation.
- Jose de San Martin (1778-1850) — Liberator and general. Argentine soldier who forged the Army of the Andes, crossed the cordillera in 1817 to free Chile, and commanded the patriot victory at Maipu before carrying the war to Peru.

## The Nitrate Age and the Twentieth Century — 1879 - 1970

Victory in the War of the Pacific gave Chile the nitrate-rich deserts of the north and a generation of mineral wealth; the twentieth century brought a militant labour movement, a widening democracy, the poetry of Pablo Neruda, and, in 1970, the election of the socialist Salvador Allende.

- 1879-1883: **The War of the Pacific** — A dispute over nitrate taxes leads Chile to war with Peru and Bolivia; Chilean victories win the nitrate-rich Atacama and leave Bolivia landlocked. [britannica-war-of-the-pacific, britannica-history-chile]
- 1880-1883: **Pacification of Araucania** — After three centuries of independence, the Chilean state subdues the Mapuche south of the Biobio by military campaign and opens their territory to settlement. [britannica-history-chile, britannica-araucanian-wars]
- 1971 CE: **Neruda wins the Nobel Prize** — Pablo Neruda, poet, diplomat and senator, is awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature, crowning Chile's golden age of letters. [britannica-neruda]
- 1970 CE: **Election of Salvador Allende** — The Marxist Salvador Allende of the Popular Unity coalition wins a narrow plurality and is confirmed by Congress, the first freely elected Marxist head of state in the Americas. [britannica-allende, ofhistorian-allende]

Figures:

- Pablo Neruda (1904-1973) — Poet and Nobel laureate. Perhaps the foremost Latin American poet of the twentieth century, author of 'Twenty Love Poems' and 'Canto general', a communist senator and diplomat awarded the Nobel Prize in 1971; he died days after the 1973 coup.

## The 1973 Coup and the Pinochet Dictatorship — 1970 - 1990

Salvador Allende's socialist government, beset by polarisation and economic crisis and pressured from abroad, was overthrown in the military coup of 11 September 1973; the dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet that followed ruled for seventeen years, its grave human-rights abuses later documented by the Rettig and Valech commissions.

- 11 September 1973: **The military coup** — The armed forces under General Augusto Pinochet overthrow President Salvador Allende, who dies as troops storm La Moneda, ending Chile's democratic experiment. [britannica-coup-1973, britannica-allende, ofhistorian-allende]
- 1974-1990: **The Pinochet dictatorship** — Pinochet's regime represses opponents through detention, torture, killing and forced disappearance while imposing free-market reforms; the abuses are later documented by the Rettig and Valech commissions. [britannica-pinochet, rettig-report, valech-report]
- 1980 CE: **The 1980 Constitution** — A constitution drafted under the dictatorship is approved in a controlled plebiscite, entrenching military prerogatives and remaining, much amended, in force for decades. [britannica-pinochet, britannica-history-chile]
- 5 October 1988: **The 'No' plebiscite** — Asked to extend Pinochet's rule, Chileans vote No by 55 to 43 percent, compelling the open presidential election that opens the path back to democracy. [britannica-pinochet, britannica-history-chile]

Figures:

- Salvador Allende (1908-1973) — President of Chile. Physician and socialist who became the first freely elected Marxist head of state in the Americas in 1970; his government was overthrown in the 1973 coup, and he died in La Moneda that day.
- Augusto Pinochet (1915-2006) — General and dictator. Army commander who led the 1973 coup and ruled Chile until 1990. His dictatorship combined free-market reform with severe repression; after his fall he faced charges over human-rights abuses.

## Return to Democracy and Modern Chile — 1990 - present

Democracy returned in 1990 with the inauguration of Patricio Aylwin, who opened the long reckoning with the dictatorship's crimes; since then Chile has grown prosperous and stable while wrestling, through the protests of 2019 and a contested constitutional process, with deep inequality and the legacy of the Pinochet era.

- 11 March 1990: **Return to democracy** — Patricio Aylwin is inaugurated president, restoring civilian democratic rule and soon creating the Rettig Commission to investigate the dictatorship's crimes. [britannica-history-chile, rettig-report]
- 1998 CE: **Pinochet arrested in London** — Augusto Pinochet is detained in London on a foreign warrant for human-rights crimes, a landmark in the pursuit of accountability before his death in 2006. [britannica-pinochet]
- October 2019: **The 'estallido social' protests** — A metro fare rise in Santiago ignites the largest protests since the dictatorship, broadening into demands for reform of pensions, health, education and the constitution. [britannica-history-chile]
- September 2022: **New constitution rejected** — After a 2020 vote to rewrite the charter, Chileans reject a sweeping progressive draft 62 to 38 percent, a major blow to President Gabriel Boric; a later attempt also fails. [britannica-boric, britannica-history-chile]

Figures:

- Patricio Aylwin (1918-2016) — President, restorer of democracy. Christian Democrat inaugurated in March 1990 as the first president after the dictatorship; he established the Rettig truth commission and anchored Chile's democratic transition.
- Gabriel Boric (born 1986) — President of Chile. Former student leader who in 2021 became the youngest president in Chilean history; he championed a new constitution to replace the Pinochet-era charter, though successive drafts were rejected.
