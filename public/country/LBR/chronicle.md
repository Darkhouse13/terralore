Founding: Declaration of the Republic of Liberia — 26 July 1847. On 26 July 1847 delegates representing the settler communities along the coast issued the Liberian Declaration of Independence and proclaimed the Republic of Liberia, making it the first modern sovereign republic on the African continent. The settlers — free-born and formerly enslaved African Americans brought out from the United States from 1822 under the auspices of the American Colonization Society, together with 'recaptives' freed from intercepted slave ships — had outgrown rule by the Society and faced challenges to their authority from European traders. Their constitution, drafted with the help of the Harvard jurist Simon Greenleaf and modelled closely on that of the United States, established a republican government, and Joseph Jenkins Roberts, a free man of colour born in Norfolk, Virginia, became the new republic's first president in 1848. Britain recognised Liberia in 1848 and France in 1852, but the United States, distracted by the slavery question, withheld formal recognition until 1862. The republic so proclaimed rested on a stark imbalance: a small Americo-Liberian settler class held citizenship and power, while the indigenous African majority on whose land the state was built were long treated as subjects rather than equals.

The land that is Liberia today was long home to a mosaic of indigenous peoples — Mande speakers such as the Vai and Kpelle, Kwa speakers such as the Kru, Grebo, and Bassa, and the Mel-speaking Gola and Kisi — whose societies traded along the West African coast that Europeans called the Grain or Pepper Coast. From 1822 the American Colonization Society, an American body that sought to resettle free-born and freed African Americans in Africa, planted settlers at Cape Mesurado, founding the town that became Monrovia. On 26 July 1847 these settlers proclaimed the Republic of Liberia, the first modern republic in Africa, governed under a constitution modelled on that of the United States. For more than a century the Americo-Liberian settler minority, organised in the True Whig Party, dominated political and economic life and largely excluded the indigenous majority. In 1980 a coup by Master Sergeant Samuel Doe ended Americo-Liberian rule, and from 1989 the country was torn apart by two civil wars, associated above all with the warlord and later president Charles Taylor. After peace in 2003, Liberians elected Ellen Johnson Sirleaf in 2005 — Africa's first elected woman head of state — and the nation later weathered the catastrophic 2014 Ebola epidemic.

## Indigenous peoples of the Grain Coast — before 1822

Long before the settlers came, the forests and coast were home to Mande, Kwa, and Mel-speaking peoples — among them the Kru, Grebo, and the Vai, who created one of Africa's few indigenous scripts.

- before 15th century: **Settlement by Mande, Kwa, and Mel peoples** — Over centuries the region was settled by Niger-Congo-speaking peoples — Mande groups such as the Vai and Kpelle, Kwa groups such as the Kru, Grebo, and Bassa, and Mel groups such as the Gola and Kisi, the last regarded as among the oldest inhabitants. [britannica-liberia-people, minorityrights-liberia]
- 15th–16th centuries: **Europeans name the Grain Coast** — Portuguese and other European mariners charted the coast and called it the Grain or Pepper Coast after the melegueta pepper traded there; Kru and Grebo seafarers became known along the West African coast. [britannica-liberia-history, loc-liberia-history]
- c. 1832–1833: **Invention of the Vai script** — Momolu Duwalu Bukele and fellow Vai elders are credited with devising the Vai syllabary, one of the few indigenous writing systems created in sub-Saharan Africa, which remains in use; some accounts place its origin earlier. [bukele-wiki, vai-syllabary-wiki, britannica-liberia-people]

## The American Colonization Society settlement — 1816 – 1847

From 1822 the American Colonization Society planted settlers of free-born and freed African Americans at Cape Mesurado, founding the town that became Monrovia.

- 1816: **Founding of the American Colonization Society** — The American Colonization Society was established in the United States to resettle free-born and freed African Americans in West Africa, drawing together abolitionists and slaveholders with sharply differing motives. [blackpast-monrovia, loc-liberia-history, britannica-liberia-history]
- 1821: **Acquisition of Cape Mesurado** — Society agents, backed by a U.S. naval officer, secured Cape Mesurado from local rulers as the site for the colony, after an earlier attempt to settle at Sherbro Island had failed. [blackpast-monrovia, britannica-monrovia]
- 1822: **First settlers land and found the colony** — Free-born and freed African American settlers went ashore at Cape Mesurado and established the settlement of Christopolis, the nucleus of the colony of Liberia, with the agent Jehudi Ashmun organising its early administration. [blackpast-monrovia, britannica-monrovia, loc-liberia-history]
- 1824: **Christopolis renamed Monrovia** — The settlement was renamed Monrovia after U.S. President James Monroe, a patron of the Colonization Society, and the colony was named Liberia, 'land of the free'. [britannica-monrovia, blackpast-monrovia]

Figures:

- Jehudi Ashmun (1794–1828) — American Colonization Society agent. White American agent who organised the early government, defence, and commerce of the Liberia colony in the 1820s and is often described as a founder of the settlement.

## The republic and the True Whig order — 1847 – 1944

On 26 July 1847 the settlers proclaimed Africa's first modern republic — but power rested with the Americo-Liberian minority and the True Whig Party, while a 99-year concession in 1926 handed Firestone vast plantations.

- 26 July 1847: **The Republic of Liberia is proclaimed** — Delegates issued the Liberian Declaration of Independence and proclaimed the Republic of Liberia under a U.S.-modelled constitution, making it the first modern republic in Africa. [liberia-declaration-wiki, ebsco-liberia-independence, history-liberia-independence]
- 1848: **Joseph Jenkins Roberts inaugurated** — Joseph Jenkins Roberts, a free man of colour born in Virginia, was inaugurated as the first president of the Republic of Liberia. [britannica-liberia-history, history-liberia-independence]
- 1862: **United States recognises Liberia** — Having delayed during the era of slavery, the United States formally recognised the Republic of Liberia in 1862 and concluded a treaty of commerce and navigation with it. [statedept-liberia]
- 1878: **True Whig Party dominance begins** — From 1878 the True Whig Party, the party of the Americo-Liberian elite, held power virtually without interruption for more than a century, marginalising the indigenous majority. [true-whig-wiki, britannica-liberia-history]
- 1926: **The Firestone rubber concession** — The government granted the Firestone Tire and Rubber Company a 99-year concession to lease up to a million acres, creating one of the world's largest rubber plantations and binding Liberia's economy to a single foreign company. [britannica-liberia-history, firestone-statement]

Figures:

- Joseph Jenkins Roberts (1809–1876) — First President of Liberia. Virginia-born free man of colour who became the first president of the Republic of Liberia in 1848 and a leading figure of the early Americo-Liberian state.

## The Tubman era and the 1980 coup — 1944 – 1989

William Tubman opened Liberia to foreign investment and began to extend the franchise — but the old settler order endured until Master Sergeant Samuel Doe seized power in 1980, ending 133 years of Americo-Liberian rule.

- 1944: **William Tubman becomes president** — William V. S. Tubman took office as president and would govern until 1971, launching the 'Open Door' policy of foreign investment and the 'National Unification' policy toward the indigenous interior. [britannica-tubman, blackpast-tubman]
- 1971: **Tolbert succeeds Tubman** — On Tubman's death his vice-president William R. Tolbert became president, promising reform but facing mounting discontent over inequality and Americo-Liberian privilege. [tolbert-wiki, britannica-liberia-history]
- April 1979: **The rice riots** — Protests in Monrovia against a proposed increase in the price of rice turned deadly, exposing the fragility of the Tolbert government and the depth of popular grievance. [doe-coup-wiki, britannica-liberia-history]
- 12 April 1980: **Samuel Doe's coup ends settler rule** — Master Sergeant Samuel K. Doe led soldiers who killed President Tolbert and seized power, ending 133 years of Americo-Liberian and True Whig dominance; thirteen officials were soon publicly executed. [doe-coup-wiki, britannica-doe, statedept-liberia-bgn]
- 1985: **Doe confirmed in a flawed election** — Samuel Doe was declared the winner of a widely criticised election, formalising his presidency; his increasingly repressive and ethnically partisan rule deepened tensions across Liberia. [britannica-doe, statedept-liberia-bgn]

Figures:

- William V. S. Tubman (1895–1971) — President of Liberia, 1944–1971. Long-serving True Whig president, often called the father of modern Liberia, whose 'Open Door' and 'National Unification' policies courted foreign investment and began, cautiously, to extend rights to the indigenous majority.
- Samuel K. Doe (1951–1990) — Military ruler and President of Liberia. Krahn master sergeant who seized power in the 1980 coup, ending Americo-Liberian rule, then governed with growing violence until he was captured and killed in 1990 at the start of the civil war.

## The civil wars and Charles Taylor — 1989 – 2003

Two civil wars between 1989 and 2003 killed an estimated 200,000 or more Liberians; their central figure, Charles Taylor, ruled as president and was later convicted of war crimes for backing atrocities in Sierra Leone.

- 24 December 1989: **First Liberian Civil War begins** — Charles Taylor's National Patriotic Front of Liberia invaded from Côte d'Ivoire, beginning a brutal civil war marked by ethnic massacres and the widespread use of child soldiers. [npfl-wiki, cja-liberia, worldwithoutgenocide-liberia]
- September 1990: **Samuel Doe captured and killed** — President Samuel Doe was captured, tortured, and killed by a rival rebel faction, and a West African ECOMOG force deployed in an attempt to contain the spreading conflict. [britannica-doe, worldwithoutgenocide-liberia]
- 1997: **Charles Taylor elected president** — After a 1996 peace accord, Charles Taylor won the 1997 presidential election amid intimidation and war-weariness, ending the first civil war but not the underlying conflict. [britannica-taylor, cja-liberia]
- 1999: **Second Liberian Civil War begins** — The rebel group LURD launched a new insurgency against Taylor's government from the north, opening a second civil war that would eventually besiege Monrovia. [blackpast-second-civil-war, second-civil-war-wiki]
- August 2003: **Taylor resigns; Accra peace agreement** — With rebels besieging Monrovia and an international indictment over Sierra Leone hanging over him, Charles Taylor resigned and went into exile; a comprehensive peace agreement in Accra ended the war and a UN mission, UNMIL, deployed. [blackpast-second-civil-war, britannica-taylor, second-civil-war-wiki]
- 26 April 2012: **Taylor convicted of war crimes** — The Special Court for Sierra Leone convicted Charles Taylor of aiding and abetting war crimes and crimes against humanity in Sierra Leone and sentenced him to 50 years — the first former head of state convicted by an international tribunal since Nuremberg. [hrw-taylor-conviction, un-taylor-50years]

Figures:

- Charles Taylor (born 1948) — Warlord and President of Liberia. Founder of the NPFL who launched the 1989 invasion, served as president from 1997, resigned into exile in 2003, and was convicted in 2012 by the Special Court for Sierra Leone for backing atrocities in that country's war.

## Peace, Sirleaf, and the Ebola epidemic — 2003 – present

After the 2003 peace, Liberians elected Ellen Johnson Sirleaf in 2005 — Africa's first elected woman head of state — and the nation later endured the devastating 2014 Ebola epidemic.

- November 2005: **Ellen Johnson Sirleaf elected** — Ellen Johnson Sirleaf won the presidential run-off against George Weah and was inaugurated in January 2006 as the first woman elected to lead an African nation. [britannica-sirleaf, nobel-sirleaf]
- 2011: **Sirleaf shares the Nobel Peace Prize** — Ellen Johnson Sirleaf was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, jointly with two other women, for non-violent work on behalf of women's safety and participation in peacebuilding. [nobel-sirleaf]
- 2014–2015: **The Ebola epidemic** — Liberia was among the countries worst hit by the West African Ebola epidemic, the largest in history, which killed thousands and overwhelmed a health system already weakened by war before transmission was halted. [ebola-liberia-wiki, cdc-ebola-liberia]
- January 2018: **Peaceful transfer to George Weah** — Sirleaf handed power to George Weah, who won the 2017 election — Liberia's first peaceful democratic transfer of power between elected presidents in over seven decades. [britannica-sirleaf, statedept-liberia-bgn]
- 2023: **Boakai defeats Weah** — Joseph Boakai defeated the incumbent George Weah in the 2023 election and took office in 2024, marking another orderly transfer of power in post-war Liberia. [britannica-liberia-history]

Figures:

- Ellen Johnson Sirleaf (born 1938) — President of Liberia, 2006–2018. Harvard-trained economist who in 2005 became the first woman elected to lead an African nation, led Liberia's post-war recovery and the response to the 2014 Ebola epidemic, and shared the 2011 Nobel Peace Prize.
