Founding: The Federal Charter of the three forest cantons — 1291. Switzerland conventionally dates its birth to early August 1291, when men of the three valley communities of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden — the Waldstätte, or forest cantons — sealed the Federal Charter (Bundesbrief), an oath of mutual aid to keep the peace, settle disputes among themselves and refuse outside judges, in order to defend their autonomy against the encroaching Habsburgs. The parchment is Switzerland's oldest surviving constitutional document. Tradition places the oath on the Rütli meadow above Lake Lucerne and gives it a hero in the marksman William Tell, who defied the Habsburg bailiff Gessler. The 1291 date was only elevated into the official birth of the nation in the nineteenth century, and 1 August has been celebrated as the Swiss national day since 1891.

Switzerland's history is the story of how a defensive league of Alpine valleys became one of Europe's most durable states without ever becoming a centralised monarchy. By convention the Confederation is dated to the Federal Charter of 1291, when the three forest communities of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden swore mutual aid against outside lords; legend gave the moment its hero in William Tell. Peasant pikemen who broke Habsburg knights at Morgarten and Sempach and humbled Burgundy turned the Confederacy into the feared infantry power of late-medieval Europe, until defeat at Marignano in 1515 ended its expansion and turned it toward neutrality. The Reformation split the cantons along confessional lines, yet the loose Confederacy held together; the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 formally severed it from the Holy Roman Empire. Napoleon's Helvetic Republic briefly imposed a unitary state, but the Congress of Vienna in 1815 restored the cantons and recognised Switzerland's permanent armed neutrality. After the brief Sonderbund War of 1847, the federal constitution of 1848 created the modern federal state, and Geneva became the cradle of the Red Cross and of international humanitarian law. Neutral through both world wars, Switzerland kept apart from the European Union, granted women the federal vote only in 1971, and joined the United Nations as late as 2002.

## The Oath of the Forest Cantons and the Old Swiss Confederacy — 1291 – 1515

An alliance of Alpine valleys defied the house of Habsburg, and its pikemen grew into the most feared infantry in Europe.

- Aug 1291: **The Federal Charter of the forest cantons** — Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden sealed a charter of mutual aid against outside lords, refusing foreign judges and pledging to keep the peace; later regarded as the founding act of the Swiss Confederation and its oldest constitutional document. [aboutch-history, britannica-late-middle-ages]
- 15 Nov 1315: **Battle of Morgarten** — Men of Schwyz and Uri ambushed and destroyed a Habsburg army of knights under Duke Leopold I at Morgarten, the first great victory of the Confederation and a celebrated triumph of foot soldiers over mounted nobility. [britannica-morgarten]
- 9 Jul 1386: **Battle of Sempach** — The Confederates routed a Habsburg army at Sempach and killed Duke Leopold III of Austria, breaking Habsburg power in the region and confirming the Confederacy as a serious military force. [britannica-morgarten, britannica-late-middle-ages]
- 1476–1477: **The Burgundian Wars** — Swiss and allied armies defeated Charles the Bold of Burgundy at Grandson and Murten in 1476 and at Nancy in 1477, where Charles fell; victory destroyed Burgundy as a power and made Swiss pikemen the most feared infantry in Europe. [britannica-late-middle-ages, nationalmuseum-borders]
- 1513: **The Confederacy of thirteen cantons** — With the accession of Appenzell the league reached its early-modern form of thirteen cantons, a loose alliance of self-governing communities bound by oath rather than ruled by a single sovereign. [britannica-history, britannica-late-middle-ages]

Figures:

- William Tell (legendary) — Legendary marksman of Uri. The crossbowman who, in legend, refused to bow to the Habsburg bailiff Gessler's hat at Altdorf and was forced to shoot an apple from his son's head, then slew the tyrant — an emblem of the struggle for liberty. The tale first appears around 1470, and historians have found no evidence he existed.
- Arnold Winkelried (legendary) — Legendary hero of Sempach. By tradition the Unterwalden warrior who at Sempach in 1386 threw himself onto the Habsburg pikes to open a gap for his comrades, sacrificing himself for the Confederation. Like Tell, he belongs to legend rather than verified record.

## Marignano, the Reformation and the Turn to Neutrality — 1515 – 1648

Defeat in Italy ended Swiss expansion and pointed toward neutrality, while the Reformation split the cantons between Protestant and Catholic.

- 13–14 Sep 1515: **Battle of Marignano** — A French army under Francis I decisively defeated the Swiss near Milan, the first great Swiss defeat in two hundred years; the Confederates withdrew from Italy, a retreat traditionally seen as the dawn of Swiss neutrality. [aboutch-neutrality, britannica-history]
- 1519: **Zwingli's Reformation in Zürich** — Huldrych Zwingli began preaching reform from the Grossmünster in Zürich, launching the Swiss Reformation; the movement won over several cities while the inner cantons remained Catholic, dividing the Confederation by confession. [britannica-zwingli]
- 1536: **Calvin and the Reformation in Geneva** — John Calvin began the work that made Geneva the international centre of Reformed Protestantism, deepening the confessional division between the Protestant west and Catholic centre of Switzerland. [britannica-calvin]
- 24 Oct 1648: **Peace of Westphalia: independence from the Empire** — The settlement ending the Thirty Years' War formally recognised the Swiss Confederation's exemption from the Holy Roman Empire, confirming in law the independence the cantons had long held in fact. [britannica-westphalia, aboutch-history]

Figures:

- Huldrych Zwingli (1484 – 1531) — Reformer of Zürich. The leading figure of the Swiss Reformation and founder of the Reformed Church, Zwingli preached scriptural reform from Zürich's Grossmünster from 1519. His austere theology reshaped much of northern Switzerland; he was killed in battle at Kappel in 1531.
- John Calvin (1509 – 1564) — Reformer of Geneva. A French theologian who from 1536 made Geneva the model city and intellectual capital of Reformed Protestantism. His Institutes of the Christian Religion and doctrine of predestination shaped Calvinism across Europe and the wider world.

## The Helvetic Republic, Napoleon and the Recognition of Neutrality — 1648 – 1815

Revolutionary France swept away the old Confederation, but the Congress of Vienna restored the cantons and guaranteed Switzerland's permanent neutrality.

- 1798: **French invasion and the Helvetic Republic** — Revolutionary France conquered Switzerland and replaced the old Confederation with the Helvetic Republic, a centralised unitary state that abolished cantonal sovereignty and the privileges of the patrician towns. [britannica-helvetic, aboutch-history]
- 19 Feb 1803: **Napoleon's Act of Mediation** — After years of factional strife Napoleon imposed the Act of Mediation, restoring a confederal system and creating six new full cantons, including Vaud and Ticino, while keeping Switzerland tied to France. [britannica-helvetic]
- 20 Mar 1815: **Congress of Vienna recognises permanent neutrality** — The European powers declared the perpetual neutrality of Switzerland to be in Europe's interest and guaranteed its territory; confirmed by the Treaty of Paris later that year, this made armed neutrality the foundation of Swiss policy. [aboutch-neutrality, history-vienna-neutrality]
- 1815: **The borders of modern Switzerland fixed** — The Vienna settlement restored the cantons and set the Confederation's external borders, which have remained almost unchanged since, leaving curiosities such as the foreign enclaves of Büsingen and Campione d'Italia. [nationalmuseum-borders, history-vienna-neutrality]

Figures:

- Napoleon Bonaparte (1769 – 1821) — First Consul / Emperor of the French. Having conquered Switzerland and created the Helvetic Republic, Napoleon recast it through the 1803 Act of Mediation, restoring a confederal structure and enlarging it with new cantons. His defeat opened the way to the Vienna settlement of 1815.
- Charles Pictet de Rochemont (1755 – 1824) — Genevan diplomat. The Swiss negotiator at the Congress of Vienna and the Paris peace whose diplomacy secured international recognition of Switzerland's permanent neutrality and the inviolability of its territory in 1815.

## The Federal State and the Birth of Humanitarian Geneva — 1815 – 1914

A brief civil war gave way to the federal constitution of 1848, and from Geneva a Swiss businessman founded the Red Cross and modern humanitarian law.

- Nov 1847: **The Sonderbund War** — After seven Catholic cantons formed the separatist Sonderbund, the federal army under General Dufour defeated it in under a month with fewer than a hundred deaths, ending Switzerland's last civil war. [britannica-sonderbund, britannica-dufour]
- 1848: **The Federal Constitution** — Switzerland adopted a federal constitution that turned the loose confederation into a modern federal state with a Federal Assembly, a collegial executive and a single market, while preserving the cantons; the assembly first met in Bern in November 1848. [aboutch-history, britannica-history]
- 1859: **Dunant at the Battle of Solferino** — The Geneva businessman Henri Dunant witnessed the carnage of Solferino and organised relief for the wounded, an experience that inspired his 1862 book A Memory of Solferino and his call for protection of war victims. [icrc-founding, aboutch-geneva-conventions]
- 1863: **Founding of the Red Cross (ICRC)** — A committee of five Genevans took up Dunant's proposals and formed the body that became the International Committee of the Red Cross, adopting the red cross on white — the inverse of the Swiss flag — as the emblem of neutral medical aid. [icrc-founding]
- Aug 1864: **The first Geneva Convention** — A diplomatic conference convened by the Swiss government adopted the first Geneva Convention for the protection of the wounded in war, the foundation of modern international humanitarian law, of which Switzerland remains depositary. [icrc-founding, aboutch-geneva-conventions]

Figures:

- Guillaume-Henri Dufour (1787 – 1875) — General, engineer and cartographer. Four times commander-in-chief of the federal army, Dufour led the swift, restrained defeat of the Sonderbund in 1847, produced the first topographic map of Switzerland, and presided over the 1864 Geneva conference that created the Red Cross.
- Henri Dunant (1828 – 1910) — Founder of the Red Cross. A Geneva businessman whose horror at the suffering after Solferino led him to write A Memory of Solferino and to inspire the founding of the International Committee of the Red Cross in 1863. He shared the first Nobel Peace Prize in 1901.

## Neutral Switzerland: World Wars, Sovereignty and the Modern Confederation — 1914 – present

Switzerland kept out of both world wars, guarded its sovereignty outside the great alliances, and only slowly extended democracy and joined the world's institutions.

- 1920: **Joining the League of Nations** — Switzerland joined the new League of Nations, headquartered in Geneva, on terms that its permanent neutrality would be respected, confirming Geneva's role as a centre of international diplomacy. [aboutch-history, aboutch-neutrality]
- 1940: **Armed neutrality in the Second World War** — Encircled by the Axis, Switzerland mobilised under General Henri Guisan, who rallied the army on the Rütli and adopted the Alpine Réduit defence; the country stayed unoccupied but later faced reckoning over refugees and looted assets. [britannica-guisan, aboutch-neutrality]
- Feb 1971: **Women win the federal vote** — A referendum in 1971 finally granted Swiss women the right to vote and stand in federal elections, one of the last democracies in Europe to do so; the final canton was compelled to follow only in 1990. [swissinfo-suffrage]
- 3 Mar 2002: **Switzerland joins the United Nations** — After rejecting membership in 1986, Swiss voters narrowly approved joining the UN in a referendum on 3 March 2002, and Switzerland became the 190th member state that September, while still standing outside the European Union. [swissinfo-un, aboutch-history]
- present: **A neutral federal republic in the heart of Europe** — Switzerland remains a federal republic of twenty-six cantons and four languages, neutral and outside the EU yet enclosing the foreign enclaves of Büsingen and Campione d'Italia, with Geneva a global hub of diplomacy and humanitarian law. [nationalmuseum-borders, aboutch-neutrality]

Figures:

- Henri Guisan (1874 – 1960) — General of the Swiss Army in the Second World War. Elected commander-in-chief in 1939, Guisan embodied Swiss resolve during the war. His 1940 address to officers on the Rütli and the Réduit strategy of Alpine defence rallied the nation as it stood encircled by the Axis.
- Charles Pictet de Rochemont (1755 – 1824) — Architect of recognised neutrality. The Genevan diplomat whose negotiations in 1815 won the international guarantee of Swiss neutrality that carried the Confederation safely through the wars of the following century.
