# The Chronicle of Oman: From the copper of Magan and the frankincense roads to a seafaring empire that reached Zanzibar, reborn as a modern sultanate.

Founding: Founding of the Al Bu Said dynasty — 1744. The modern Omani state dates to the rise of Ahmad bin Said al-Busaidi, governor of Suhar, who drove out the occupying Persians and was elected imam around 1744-1749, founding the Al Bu Said dynasty that still rules Oman today. Its far deeper roots reach back to the ancient land of Magan, prized by Mesopotamia for its copper, and to the early adoption of Islam and the Ibadi imamate in the 8th century, which gave Oman its distinctive religious and political identity.

Oman occupies the southeastern corner of the Arabian Peninsula, a land of mountains, desert and a long Indian Ocean coast that has made it one of the oldest seafaring and trading societies on Earth. In antiquity it was the copper-rich land the Sumerians called Magan and the heartland of the frankincense trade that linked southern Arabia to the Mediterranean. It adopted Islam in the Prophet's lifetime and became the world's principal home of the Ibadi school, governed for centuries by elected imams. Under the Al Bu Said dynasty, founded in 1744, Oman built a maritime empire that stretched to Zanzibar and the East African coast, before a long decline, growing British influence, and the 1970 accession of Sultan Qaboos bin Said opened the country's modern renaissance.

## Magan, Copper and the Land of Frankincense — c. 3000 BCE - 6th century CE

Long before Islam, Oman was the copper-rich land the Sumerians called Magan and a heartland of the frankincense trade that perfumed the temples of the ancient world.

- c. 2300 BCE: **Magan supplies copper to Mesopotamia** — Sumerian texts record a land called Magan, identified with Oman, as a source of copper and diorite carried by sea to the city-states of Mesopotamia. [loc-ancient-oman, britannica-oman]
- 3rd millennium BCE: **Beehive tombs and towers at Bat** — The Bronze Age communities of Bat, Al-Khutm and Al-Ayn raised monumental stone towers and dry-stone beehive tombs, later inscribed by UNESCO in 1988 as the most complete such collection in the world. [unesco-bat]
- 2nd-1st millennium BCE: **The frankincense trade flourishes in Dhofar** — The resin of the Boswellia sacra tree, harvested in Dhofar, became one of antiquity's great luxury goods, carried by caravan and ship from southern Arabia to the Mediterranean. [unesco-frankincense, loc-ancient-oman]
- 4th century BCE onward: **Khor Rori and the incense ports** — The port of Khor Rori (Sumhuram) and later Al-Baleed served as gateways for the export of frankincense and goods across the Indian Ocean, anchoring the Land of Frankincense. [unesco-frankincense]

## Islam and the Ibadi Imamate — 7th - 16th century CE

Oman embraced Islam in the Prophet's lifetime and became the principal home of the Ibadi school, governed by elected imams who fused spiritual and temporal authority.

- c. 630 CE: **Oman accepts Islam** — According to tradition, the inhabitants of Oman embraced Islam during the lifetime of the Prophet Muhammad, beginning the country's long Islamic history. [britannica-oman, fanack-imamates]
- c. 750 CE: **The first Ibadi imamate** — Around 750 CE the Ibadis established their first imamate in Oman under al-Julanda ibn Masud, an elected imam uniting temporal and religious authority and giving Oman a distinctive political identity. [fanack-imamates, britannica-ibadiyyah]
- 8th century onward: **Ibadism becomes Oman's defining school** — The Ibadi school of Islam took root across the Omani interior, making Oman its principal home and shaping a tradition of imams chosen for learning and piety rather than heredity. [britannica-ibadiyyah, fanack-imamates]

## The Portuguese, Hormuz and the Yarubid Recovery — 1507 - 1744

Portugal seized Muscat and the Gulf in 1507; more than a century later the Yarubid imams drove them out and turned Oman into an Indian Ocean naval power.

- 1507: **Albuquerque captures Muscat** — The Portuguese under Afonso de Albuquerque stormed Muscat and other Omani coastal towns, beginning more than a century of Portuguese dominance over the coast. [britannica-oman, loc-ancient-oman]
- c. 1624: **Rise of the Yarubid imamate** — A member of the Yarub tribe was elected imam, founding the Yarubid dynasty that would unite Oman and lead the campaign to expel the Portuguese. [britannica-yarubid, britannica-oman]
- 1650: **Expulsion of the Portuguese from Muscat** — The Yarubid imams recaptured Muscat in 1650, ending Portuguese occupation of the Omani coast and launching Oman as an Indian Ocean naval power. [britannica-yarubid, britannica-oman]
- 1737: **Persian invasion amid Yarubid civil war** — A succession dispute fractured the Yarubid state, and the Persian ruler Nadir Shah's forces invaded and occupied parts of Oman, plunging the country into crisis. [britannica-oman, britannica-ahmad]

## The Al Bu Said Dynasty and the Empire of Zanzibar — 1744 - 1856

Ahmad bin Said expelled the Persians and founded the dynasty that still rules; under Said bin Sultan Oman became an Indian Ocean empire ruling Zanzibar and the East African coast.

- c. 1744-1749: **Ahmad bin Said founds the Al Bu Said dynasty** — Ahmad bin Said, governor of Suhar, expelled the Persian invaders and was elected imam, founding the Al Bu Said dynasty that still rules Oman. [britannica-ahmad, britannica-albusaid]
- 21 September 1833: **Treaty of Amity and Commerce with the United States** — Said bin Sultan's government concluded a Treaty of Amity and Commerce with the United States, the first bilateral treaty between Washington and an Arab Gulf state. [usembassy-history, britannica-said]
- 1840: **Said bin Sultan moves the capital to Zanzibar** — Said bin Sultan transferred his capital from Muscat to Zanzibar, making the island the commercial heart of his Indian Ocean empire and the chief power in East Africa. [britannica-said, britannica-albusaid]
- 1856: **Death of Said and the split of the empire** — On Said bin Sultan's death his realm was divided between his sons, one taking Muscat and Oman and the other Zanzibar, severing the African empire from Arabia. [britannica-said, britannica-albusaid]

Figures:

- Ahmad bin Said al-Busaidi (c. 1694 - 1783) — Founder and first imam of the Al Bu Said dynasty. Governor of Suhar who drove out the Persian invaders and was elected imam around 1744-1749, reuniting Oman and founding the dynasty that still rules the country.
- Said bin Sultan (1790 - 1856) — Sultan of Muscat, Oman and Zanzibar. Ruler from 1806 who built Oman's Indian Ocean empire, made Zanzibar the commercial capital of East Africa, and moved his seat there in 1840.

## Decline, British Influence and Isolation — 1856 - 1970

Shorn of Zanzibar and dependent on Britain, Oman turned inward; under Said bin Taimur the country froze into isolation as rebellion smouldered in the interior and Dhofar.

- 1861: **Zanzibar separated from Oman** — Following an arbitration award, Zanzibar was formally separated from Muscat and Oman as an independent sultanate, severing the African empire and its revenues for good. [britannica-albusaid, britannica-said]
- 1932: **Accession of Sultan Said bin Taimur** — Said bin Taimur came to the throne and ruled an increasingly isolated and undeveloped Oman, leaning on British support to hold the country together. [britannica-said-taimur, britannica-oman]
- 1965: **Outbreak of the Dhofar rebellion** — An uprising began in the neglected southern province of Dhofar against Said bin Taimur's rule, growing into a Marxist-led insurgency that would dominate Omani affairs for a decade. [britannica-oman, loc-ancient-oman]
- 1967: **Oil exports begin** — Oman began exporting oil in commercial quantities, the start of the revenue that would later finance the country's modern transformation. [britannica-restoration, britannica-oman]
- late 1930s or early 1940s: **Madha's elders choose Muscat; Nahwa stays with Sharjah** — According to local oral history, the leaders of the neighbouring ruling families asked the elders of the mountain village of Madha which ruler it wished to follow. Madha chose Oman, reportedly for its perceived wealth, stronger government and the protection of its water, while the nearby village of Nahwa stayed with the Qawasim of Sharjah. The choice left Madha an Omani exclave inside the Trucial Coast with a Sharjah village inside it. The date and the wording of the question are recorded only in oral accounts. [national-madha-pledge, national-hidden-piece]
- 1969: **The Madha and Nahwa boundary is fixed** — After British surveyors consulted local tribes and rulers, the boundary between Madha and Nahwa was settled in 1969, two years before the federation, making Madha an exclave of Oman and Nahwa a counter-enclave of Sharjah. The line was inherited by the United Arab Emirates in 1971. [wikipedia-madha, national-walker-obituary]

Figures:

- Said bin Taimur (1910 - 1972) — Sultan of Muscat and Oman, 1932-1970. Austere and isolationist ruler who kept Oman undeveloped and dependent on Britain, and whose refusal to modernize amid the Dhofar war led to his overthrow in 1970.

## The Renaissance of Qaboos and Modern Oman — 1970 - present

The 1970 accession of Sultan Qaboos ended Oman's isolation and launched a fifty-year renaissance; his successor, Sultan Haitham, now steers the country into a post-oil future.

- 23 July 1970: **Qaboos accedes in a palace coup** — Qaboos bin Said deposed his father Said bin Taimur and became sultan, ending Oman's isolation and beginning the modernization drive known as the Renaissance. [britannica-qaboos, britannica-restoration]
- 1975: **Defeat of the Dhofar rebellion** — With British and Iranian support and a policy of amnesty and development, Qaboos's forces overcame the Dhofar insurgency, securing the unity of the country. [britannica-oman, britannica-restoration]
- 1996: **Promulgation of the Basic Law** — Sultan Qaboos issued the Basic Law, Oman's first written constitution, defining the organs and guiding principles of the state. [britannica-qaboos, britannica-restoration]
- 22 June 2002: **Oman and the UAE sign their border agreement** — Oman and the United Arab Emirates signed in Abu Dhabi the Border Agreement on the borders from Eastern Uqaidat to Darah. Oman ratified it by Royal Decree 10/2003 on 27 January 2003, and the two states exchanged the instruments of ratification on 11 October 2003. [oman-rd-10-2003, khaleej-ratify-2003]
- 21 July 2008: **Final border maps include Madha and Nahwa** — Oman and the UAE signed the final coordinates and maps of their border: 51 maps and three lists of coordinates for the border posts in three sectors, one of them from Madha to Al Nahwa, closing the technical demarcation begun by the 2002 agreement. [emirates247-2008-border]
- 10-11 January 2020: **Death of Qaboos and accession of Haitham** — Sultan Qaboos died after nearly fifty years on the throne, and his cousin Haitham bin Tariq was sworn in as sultan the following day in a smooth succession. [britannica-haitham, britannica-qaboos]
- 11-12 January 2021: **New Basic Statute and Vision 2040** — Sultan Haitham issued a new Basic Statute establishing a formal crown prince and primogeniture succession, advancing Oman Vision 2040 and economic diversification. [britannica-haitham, oman-fm-history]
- 24 June 2026: **Oman opens temporary corridors in the Strait of Hormuz** — Oman declared the existing traffic separation scheme in the strait unsafe, opened two temporary corridors either side of it, and said no tolls would be charged. [thestandard-oman-corridors]

Figures:

- Qaboos bin Said (1940 - 2020) — Sultan of Oman, 1970-2020. Architect of modern Oman, who overthrew his father in 1970, ended the country's isolation, defeated the Dhofar rebellion and led a half-century renaissance.
- Haitham bin Tariq (born 1954) — Sultan of Oman since 2020. Cousin and successor of Qaboos, who acceded in January 2020 and has launched Oman Vision 2040, a new Basic Statute and a primogeniture succession.
