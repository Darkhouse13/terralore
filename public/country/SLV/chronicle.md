Founding: Independence from Spain — 15 September 1821. On 15 September 1821 the Act of Independence of Central America was signed at the Palacio Nacional in Guatemala City, declaring the former Captaincy General of Guatemala, of which the province of San Salvador formed part, independent of Spain. Salvadorans soon opposed the Guatemalan decision to join Agustin de Iturbide's Mexican empire, leading to armed confrontation. After the empire collapsed, a constituent assembly meeting in Guatemala proclaimed the United Provinces of Central America on 1 July 1823 and in 1824 adopted the constitution of the Federal Republic of Central America. El Salvador assumed fully sovereign powers as a separate republic in 1841, as the federation, weakened by civil war since 1838, fell apart.

El Salvador, the smallest and most densely populated country of Central America, takes its roots from the Pipil people, whose realm of Cuzcatlan, the "Land of the Jewel," dominated the region before the Spanish conquest, and from earlier Maya farmers whose village at Joya de Ceren was sealed under volcanic ash about AD 600, the "Pompeii of the Americas." After Pedro de Alvarado's expedition opened the conquest in 1524, the province shared in the general independence of Central America declared on 15 September 1821 and became fully sovereign in 1841 as the Federal Republic of Central America dissolved. A coffee economy enriched a narrow landed elite and bred deep inequality, igniting the 1932 peasant uprising and its violent suppression, la matanza, and ultimately the civil war of 1980 to 1992 between the military-led government and the FMLN, in which a United Nations Truth Commission found that more than 75,000 people died. The Chapultepec Peace Accords of 1992 ended the war, but a postwar epidemic of gang violence and mass emigration followed, until President Nayib Bukele's sweeping gang crackdown after 2022 transformed the security landscape amid debate over civil liberties.

## The Pipil, the Maya, and the Land of the Jewel — Pre-Columbian - 1524

Long before the Spanish came, the land that became El Salvador was home to Maya farmers, whose village at Joya de Ceren was sealed under volcanic ash about AD 600, and later to the Pipil, whose realm of Cuzcatlan, the Land of the Jewel, dominated the region west of the Lempa River.

- c. AD 600: **Joya de Ceren buried by ash** — An eruption of the Laguna Caldera volcano seals the Maya farming village of Joya de Ceren under volcanic ash, preserving the daily life of ordinary agriculturalists in extraordinary detail. [unesco-joya-ceren]
- c. 11th-12th century: **Rise of Pipil Cuzcatlan** — Nahua-speaking Pipil migrants establish the realm of Cuzcatlan, the Land of the Jewel, dominating the region west of the Lempa River with a society resembling that of the Aztecs. [britannica-cuscatlan, britannica-pipil]
- 1993: **Joya de Ceren named World Heritage** — UNESCO inscribes Joya de Ceren on the World Heritage List as a unique record of pre-Hispanic Maya farming life, the Pompeii of the Americas. [unesco-joya-ceren]

## Conquest and the Colonial Province — 1524 - 1821

From 1524 the conquistador Pedro de Alvarado's expeditions broke Pipil resistance and founded San Salvador, and for three centuries the territory was a modest, indigo-growing province of the Spanish Captaincy General of Guatemala.

- 1524: **Alvarado's conquest begins** — An expedition from Guatemala under Pedro de Alvarado enters the region, meeting fierce Pipil resistance west of the Lempa River as the Spanish conquest of the territory begins. [britannica-history-elsalvador, britannica-elsalvador-colonial]
- 1528: **San Salvador permanently founded** — After being abandoned and resettled several times in the face of Pipil resistance, the Spanish town of San Salvador is permanently established near the old Pipil capital. [britannica-history-elsalvador]
- 5 November 1811: **First cry of independence** — An early uprising in San Salvador, associated with the priest Jose Matias Delgado, is suppressed but remembered as a first stirring of the independence movement. [britannica-elsalvador-colonial]

## Independence and the Coffee Republic — 1821 - 1931

Independence from Spain in 1821 and the brief Federal Republic of Central America gave way by 1841 to a sovereign El Salvador, where a coffee boom enriched a small landed oligarchy, the so-called Fourteen Families, while dispossessing the rural poor.

- 15 September 1821: **Independence from Spain declared** — The Act of Independence of Central America, signed in Guatemala City, frees the region including the province of San Salvador from Spanish rule. [loc-acta-independencia, history-central-american-federation, britannica-central-america-independence]
- 1824: **Federal Republic of Central America** — The provinces adopt the constitution of the Federal Republic of Central America, a union of Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica in which the Salvadoran Francisco Morazan becomes a leading figure. [britannica-united-provinces, history-central-american-federation]
- 1841: **El Salvador becomes sovereign** — As the federation dissolves amid civil war, El Salvador assumes fully sovereign powers as a separate republic and adopts its modern name. [britannica-central-america-independence, history-el-salvador]
- 1881-1882: **Communal lands abolished** — Laws abolishing communal and Indigenous landholding open the highlands to coffee estates, dispossessing peasant communities and entrenching a landed oligarchy. [britannica-coffee-republic]

Figures:

- Francisco Morazan (1792-1842) — Liberal statesman and federation president. Honduran-born liberal leader who became the dominant figure and president of the Federal Republic of Central America, championing reform and union before the federation collapsed; he was executed in 1842. He is honoured across the region, and a Salvadoran department bears his name.

## La Matanza and the Age of Military Rule — 1931 - 1979

The peasant uprising of 1932 and its brutal suppression, la matanza, opened nearly half a century of military rule in alliance with the coffee oligarchy, as the demand for land and justice was repeatedly answered with force.

- January 1932: **Uprising and la matanza** — A peasant and Indigenous revolt in the coffee districts, linked to Farabundo Marti, is crushed by the army; in the ensuing repression, the matanza, estimates of those killed range from at least 10,000 to as many as 30,000. [britannica-matanza, britannica-military-dictatorships, britannica-marti]
- 1 February 1932: **Execution of Farabundo Marti** — Agustin Farabundo Marti, the communist organiser of the uprising, is captured and executed by firing squad; his name is later taken by the FMLN guerrilla front. [britannica-marti]
- December 1931: **Hernandez Martinez seizes power** — General Maximiliano Hernandez Martinez takes power in a coup, beginning his dictatorship and inaugurating nearly half a century of military rule allied with the oligarchy. [britannica-military-dictatorships]

Figures:

- Agustin Farabundo Marti (1893-1932) — Communist revolutionary. A founder of the Salvadoran Communist Party and organiser of the 1932 peasant uprising, executed by the regime in its aftermath. His name was later adopted by the Farabundo Marti National Liberation Front (FMLN), the guerrilla coalition of the civil war.
- Maximiliano Hernandez Martinez (1882-1966) — General and dictator. Seized power in December 1931 and ruled until 1944, ordering the suppression of the 1932 uprising in the killings remembered as la matanza and entrenching military rule on behalf of the landed elite.

## The Civil War — 1980 - 1992

Between 1980 and 1992 El Salvador was torn by civil war between the United States-backed, military-led government and the FMLN guerrillas; a United Nations Truth Commission later found that more than 75,000 people died, the great majority at the hands of state forces and death squads.

- 24 March 1980: **Assassination of Archbishop Romero** — Archbishop Oscar Romero is shot dead while celebrating Mass; the UN Truth Commission later concludes he was killed by a right-wing death squad, an act that helped ignite the war. [britannica-romero, britannica-civil-war]
- December 1981: **El Mozote massacre** — An army counterinsurgency operation kills hundreds of villagers at El Mozote and nearby hamlets; in 2012 President Funes apologised for the deaths of 936 people. [britannica-postconflict, britannica-civil-war]
- 16 January 1992: **Chapultepec Peace Accords** — The government and the FMLN sign the UN-brokered Chapultepec Peace Accords in Mexico City, ending the civil war, reforming the armed forces and turning the FMLN into a political party. [britannica-chapultepec, un-chapultepec-agreement]
- 15 March 1993: **Truth Commission report** — The UN Commission on the Truth for El Salvador issues its report, From Madness to Hope, documenting wartime atrocities in a conflict in which more than 75,000 people are estimated to have died. [un-truth-report, britannica-civil-war]

Figures:

- Saint Oscar Arnulfo Romero (1917-1980) — Archbishop of San Salvador and martyr. Archbishop who became a courageous critic of state violence and a defender of the poor, assassinated while saying Mass in March 1980. He was canonised by the Roman Catholic Church in 2018 and is a national and global symbol of the struggle for human rights.

## Postwar El Salvador and the Bukele Era — 1992 - 2026

Peace brought democracy and alternating governments but also an epidemic of gang violence and mass emigration, until President Nayib Bukele's dollarised, Bitcoin-adopting administration launched a sweeping crackdown after 2022 that slashed homicides while drawing concern over civil liberties.

- 2001: **Dollarisation** — El Salvador retires its national currency, the colon, and adopts the US dollar as legal tender, deepening ties to the United States economy and to migrant remittances. [britannica-postconflict]
- 2019: **Bukele wins the presidency** — Nayib Bukele, a former mayor of San Salvador, wins the presidency outside the FMLN and ARENA, ending decades of two-party dominance. [britannica-bukele, britannica-postconflict]
- September 2021: **Bitcoin made legal tender** — El Salvador becomes the first country in the world to adopt Bitcoin as legal tender alongside the US dollar. [britannica-bukele]
- March 2022: **State of exception and gang crackdown** — After a surge of gang killings, the government imposes a state of exception suspending constitutional protections and launches mass arrests; homicides fall sharply amid human-rights concerns over tens of thousands detained. [britannica-bukele, britannica-cecot]
- February 2024: **Bukele re-elected** — Despite a constitutional ban on consecutive terms, cleared by a loyal court, Bukele is re-elected by a landslide of about 85 percent of the vote. [britannica-bukele]

Figures:

- Nayib Bukele (born 1981) — President of El Salvador. Former mayor of San Salvador elected president in 2019, who made El Salvador the first nation to adopt Bitcoin as legal tender and, after 2022, led a sweeping gang crackdown that cut homicides dramatically while drawing criticism over mass detentions and civil liberties; re-elected by a landslide in 2024.
