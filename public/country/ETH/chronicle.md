# The Chronicle of Ethiopia: A cradle of humankind and one of the world's oldest states, never colonised, from Aksum to the modern federal republic.

Founding: Continuous Ethiopian statehood (Kingdom of Aksum / Solomonic dynasty) — 1st century CE (Aksum); restored 1270 (Solomonic dynasty). Unlike most nations, Ethiopia has no single founding moment but rather a deep continuity of statehood unmatched in Africa. A recognisable Ethiopian state crystallised in the Kingdom of Aksum, a powerful trading empire that flourished from around the first century CE and adopted Christianity under King Ezana in the fourth century, making it one of the earliest Christian realms on earth. That Christian polity passed through the Zagwe kings of Lalibela and, in 1270, to the Solomonic dynasty, which claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba and ruled almost without interruption until 1974. This continuity, and the fact that Ethiopia was never subjected to lasting colonisation, are the country's defining historical facts. Two later moments crowned that independence: on 1 March 1896 Emperor Menelik II's army annihilated an invading Italian force at the Battle of Adwa, the first decisive defeat of a European colonial power by an African state; and in 1974 a popular revolution ended the millennia-old monarchy, ushering in the republic.

Ethiopia is among the oldest independent nations on earth and the only African country never to be brought under sustained colonial rule. Its highlands yielded 'Lucy', the 3.2-million-year-old hominin that helped define human origins, and the early kingdom of D'mt before the rise of Aksum, a Red Sea trading empire that adopted Christianity in the fourth century under King Ezana. A continuous Christian state endured through the Zagwe kings—who carved the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela—and the Solomonic dynasty restored in 1270, which traced its line to King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. After the splendour of seventeenth-century Gondar came the fragmenting 'Era of the Princes', ended by Tewodros II's reunification from 1855. Under Menelik II, Ethiopia crushed an invading Italian army at Adwa in 1896, securing its sovereignty and becoming a symbol of African independence. Haile Selassie modernised the empire and survived Italian occupation from 1936 to 1941, but was overthrown in the 1974 revolution that brought the Marxist Derg, the Red Terror, the catastrophic 1983–85 famine and a long civil war. Since 1991 Ethiopia has been a federal republic, more recently scarred by the 2020–22 war in Tigray.

## Cradle of Humankind and the Kingdom of D'mt — c. 3.2 million years ago – c. 400 BCE

The Ethiopian highlands and Afar lowlands are among the richest sources of early hominin fossils on earth, and the setting of the first organised state, D'mt.

- c. 3.2 million years ago: **Lucy lives in the Afar region** — An Australopithecus afarensis individual, later catalogued AL 288-1, lives in what is now the Afar region; her fossilised skeleton would become one of the most famous evidences of early human ancestry. [britannica-lucy, nhm-afarensis]
- c. 10th century BCE: **Rise of the kingdom of D'mt** — The kingdom of D'mt emerges in the northern highlands, the region's first organised state, blending indigenous and South Arabian cultural elements with its centre near Yeha. [worldhistory-aksum, metmuseum-ethiopia]
- c. 8th–7th century BCE: **Great Temple of Yeha built** — A monumental temple dedicated to the moon-god Almaqah is raised at Yeha, reflecting close contact with Sabaean culture across the Red Sea. [worldhistory-aksum]
- c. 2nd–1st century BCE: **Transition toward Aksum** — D'mt declines and the northern highlands fragment into smaller states, out of which the Kingdom of Aksum would consolidate by the first century CE. [worldhistory-aksum]

Figures:

- Lucy (AL 288-1) (lived c. 3.2 million years ago) — Australopithecus afarensis fossil. A roughly 40-percent-complete female skeleton found at Hadar in 1974, whose anatomy showed that bipedal walking evolved well before the enlargement of the brain, transforming the study of human origins.

## The Kingdom of Aksum and Early Christianity — c. 1st – 7th century CE

Aksum became a major Red Sea trading empire, minted its own coinage, raised towering stelae and became one of the first states in the world to adopt Christianity.

- c. 1st–2nd century CE: **Aksum emerges as a trading empire** — Aksum consolidates control of the northern highlands and the Red Sea port of Adulis, growing into a major commercial power linking Rome, Arabia and India. [unesco-aksum, worldhistory-aksum]
- c. 3rd century CE: **Aksum issues its own coinage** — Aksumite kings begin minting gold, silver and bronze coins, among the first African states to do so, advertising the kingdom's wealth and reach. [unesco-aksum, worldhistory-aksum]
- c. 330 CE: **King Ezana adopts Christianity** — Ezana converts to Christianity, reportedly under the influence of Frumentius; his coins replace pagan emblems with the cross, making Aksum one of the world's earliest Christian states. [metmuseum-christianity, blackpast-ezana]
- c. 525 CE: **Aksumite intervention in Arabia** — Aksum sends forces across the Red Sea into Himyarite South Arabia, the high-water mark of its power before trade routes and the rise of Islam begin its decline. [worldhistory-aksum]
- c. 7th century CE: **Decline of Aksum** — The rise of Islam and the loss of Red Sea trade erode Aksum's wealth; the kingdom contracts and its centre of gravity shifts inland and southward. [unesco-aksum, worldhistory-aksum]

Figures:

- Ezana of Aksum (fl. 4th century CE) — King of Aksum (r. c. 320s–360 CE). The Aksumite king whose conversion made Christianity the religion of the state; his inscriptions and his cross-bearing coinage mark Ethiopia's entry into the Christian world.
- Frumentius (4th century CE) — First bishop of Aksum. A Syro-Phoenician brought to Aksum as a youth who, according to tradition, helped convert Ezana and was consecrated by Athanasius of Alexandria as the first bishop of the Ethiopian church.

## The Zagwe Kings, Lalibela and the Solomonic Restoration — c. 1137 – 1527

The Zagwe dynasty carved the rock churches of Lalibela; in 1270 the Solomonic line was restored, claiming descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba.

- c. 1137: **Zagwe dynasty established** — The Zagwe dynasty takes control of the Christian highlands, ruling from Roha (later Lalibela) and sustaining the Christian kingdom after Aksum's fall. [wikipedia-zagwe, metmuseum-ethiopia]
- late 12th–early 13th c.: **Rock-hewn churches of Lalibela carved** — King Lalibela commissions eleven monolithic churches cut from the rock to form a symbolic 'New Jerusalem', among the wonders of medieval Christian architecture. [unesco-lalibela, wikipedia-zagwe]
- 1270: **Solomonic dynasty restored** — Yekuno Amlak overthrows the last Zagwe ruler and founds the Solomonic dynasty, which would rule Ethiopia almost continuously until 1974, claiming descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. [worldhistory-solomonic, metmuseum-ethiopia]
- c. 1314–1322: **Compilation of the Kebra Nagast** — The national epic the Kebra Nagast is set down in Ge'ez, codifying the Solomonic descent of Ethiopia's emperors and the tradition of the Ark of the Covenant at Aksum. [worldhistory-solomonic]
- 1529: **Invasion of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi** — Forces of the Adal Sultanate under Ahmad 'Gran' invade the highlands, devastating churches and nearly destroying the Christian kingdom before being repelled with Portuguese aid in the 1540s. [metmuseum-ethiopia]

Figures:

- Gebre Mesqel Lalibela (fl. c. 1181–1221) — Zagwe king (r. late 12th–early 13th c.). The Zagwe king who, by tradition, commissioned the eleven rock-hewn churches at his capital to build a new Jerusalem in stone; the town now bears his name.
- Yekuno Amlak (d. 1285) — Founder of the Solomonic dynasty (r. 1270–1285). The Amhara noble who toppled the Zagwe in 1270 and, with the support of the church, restored the Solomonic line that would rule Ethiopia for seven centuries.

## Gondar, the Era of the Princes and Reunification — 1636 – 1855

Fasilides founded a permanent capital at Gondar; royal power then fractured during the Zemene Mesafint until Tewodros II reunified the empire.

- 1636: **Fasilides founds Gondar** — Emperor Fasilides makes Gondar his permanent capital, beginning the Gondarine period and the building of the Fasil Ghebbi castle complex. [unesco-gondar, wikipedia-gondarine]
- 1769: **Onset of the Zemene Mesafint** — Central royal authority collapses as regional lords seize real power, beginning the 'Era of the Princes' in which emperors at Gondar rule in name only. [wikipedia-gondarine]
- 1855: **Tewodros II reunifies Ethiopia** — Kassa Hailu defeats the regional warlords and is crowned Emperor Tewodros II, ending the Era of the Princes and launching efforts to centralise and modernise the state. [wikipedia-gondarine, britannica-ethiopia]
- 1868: **Siege of Magdala and death of Tewodros** — A British military expedition storms Tewodros's fortress at Magdala; the emperor takes his own life rather than surrender, ending his reign but not his unifying legacy. [britannica-ethiopia]

Figures:

- Fasilides (c. 1603–1667) — Emperor of Ethiopia (r. 1632–1667). Founder of Gondar as a permanent capital and builder of its first castle, who expelled the Jesuits and reaffirmed the primacy of the Ethiopian Orthodox church.
- Tewodros II (c. 1818–1868) — Emperor of Ethiopia (r. 1855–1868). Born Kassa Hailu, he ended the Era of the Princes and sought to forge a unified, modernised empire; cornered at Magdala by a British force, he died by his own hand.

## Menelik II, Adwa and the Defeat of Colonisation — 1855 – 1913

As Europe partitioned Africa, Menelik II expanded and modernised Ethiopia and shattered an invading Italian army at Adwa in 1896, preserving the country's independence.

- 1886: **Founding of Addis Ababa** — Menelik II and Empress Taytu establish a new capital, Addis Ababa ('New Flower'), which becomes the permanent seat of the empire and later a diplomatic hub of Africa. [britannica-ethiopia]
- 1889: **Treaty of Wuchale signed** — Menelik signs a treaty with Italy whose divergent Amharic and Italian texts spark a dispute over whether Ethiopia had become an Italian protectorate—setting the stage for war. [britannica-adwa, ebsco-adwa]
- 1 March 1896: **Battle of Adwa** — Ethiopian forces under Menelik II decisively defeat an invading Italian army at Adwa, the first major victory of an African state over a European colonial power and a guarantee of Ethiopian independence. [britannica-adwa, ebsco-adwa, blackpast-adwa]
- October 1896: **Treaty of Addis Ababa** — Italy recognises the full sovereignty and independence of Ethiopia, abrogating the Treaty of Wuchale, though it retains the separate coastal colony of Eritrea. [britannica-adwa]

Figures:

- Menelik II (1844–1913) — Emperor of Ethiopia (r. 1889–1913). Architect of modern Ethiopia who expanded its borders, founded Addis Ababa, modernised the state and led the victory at Adwa that secured the nation's independence.
- Taytu Betul (c. 1851–1918) — Empress consort and political leader. Menelik's powerful wife who opposed Italian designs, helped found Addis Ababa and played an active role in the Adwa campaign as a commander and strategist.

## Haile Selassie, the Derg, Civil War and Modern Ethiopia — 1916 – present

Haile Selassie modernised the empire and survived Italian occupation, but revolution in 1974 brought the Derg, the Red Terror, famine, civil war and, after 1991, a federal republic.

- 1936–1941: **Italian occupation and liberation** — Fascist Italy seizes Addis Ababa after the 1935 invasion and occupies Ethiopia, but never settles it as a colony; in 1941 British and Ethiopian forces expel the Italians and Haile Selassie, crowned emperor in 1930, returns and restores independence. [britannica-ethiopia, wikipedia-haileselassie]
- 12 September 1974: **Revolution overthrows the monarchy** — The Derg deposes Haile Selassie, ending the Solomonic dynasty after seven centuries; a Marxist military regime under Mengistu Haile Mariam takes power. [wikipedia-1974coup, britannica-ethiopia]
- 1983–1985: **The great famine** — Drought, civil war and Derg policies cause a famine that kills several hundred thousand to more than a million people; rights groups attribute much of the toll to government counter-insurgency tactics rather than drought alone. [wikipedia-famine8385, britannica-ethiopia]
- May 1991: **Fall of the Derg and a new federal order** — The EPRDF captures Addis Ababa and Mengistu flees into exile, ending the civil war and the Derg; a new ethnic-federal constitution follows, and Eritrea votes for independence in 1993, leaving Ethiopia landlocked. [wikipedia-fallderg, wikipedia-eritreareferendum]
- 2020–2022: **The Tigray War** — War erupts between federal forces and the Tigray People's Liberation Front, killing an estimated 380,000–600,000 people through combat, starvation and lack of medical care before the Pretoria agreement of November 2022 halts hostilities. [wikipedia-tigraypeace, cfr-ethiopia]

Figures:

- Haile Selassie I (1892–1975) — Emperor of Ethiopia (r. 1930–1974). Last emperor of the Solomonic dynasty, moderniser and global symbol of resistance to Italian aggression, deposed in the 1974 revolution and revered as a messianic figure in Rastafari belief.
- Mengistu Haile Mariam (b. 1937) — Derg chairman and head of state (1977–1991). Leader of the Marxist Derg whose rule encompassed the Red Terror, the wars of the 1980s and the famine of 1983–85; he fled to Zimbabwe in 1991 and was later convicted of genocide in absentia.
