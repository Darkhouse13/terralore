# The Chronicle of North Macedonia: From the heart of ancient Macedon to a young Balkan republic that had to win the right to its own name.

Founding: Peaceful independence from Yugoslavia — 8 September 1991. On 8 September 1991 the citizens of the Socialist Republic of Macedonia voted overwhelmingly, in a referendum approved by some 96 percent of those taking part, for a sovereign and independent state. Under President Kiro Gligorov the republic seceded from the disintegrating Yugoslav federation peacefully, the only one to do so without war or ethnic cleansing. Yet full international recognition was delayed for years by Greece's objection to the name 'Macedonia', which it regarded as implying a claim on its own northern province and on the heritage of ancient Macedon. The country was admitted to the United Nations in 1993 under the provisional designation 'the former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia', a name it carried until the dispute was finally resolved by the Prespa Agreement of 2018 and the country was renamed the Republic of North Macedonia.

The Republic of North Macedonia occupies the northern, 'Vardar' part of the wider geographic region of Macedonia, a crossroads whose history has been layered, contested and shared. The land was the cradle of the ancient kingdom of Macedon, home of Philip II and Alexander the Great, though the relationship of the modern Slavic state to that classical heritage is itself disputed. Roman and then Byzantine rule gave way, from the sixth century, to Slavic settlement; in the early Middle Ages the region became the centre of Tsar Samuel's Bulgarian state, with its capital at Ohrid and a celebrated Slavonic literary school founded by Saints Clement and Naum. Five centuries of Ottoman rule ended only with the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, which partitioned Macedonia among Serbia, Greece and Bulgaria, the Vardar portion passing to Serbia and, after 1945, becoming a republic of Tito's Yugoslavia, where the Macedonian language and nation were codified. The country declared independence peacefully in 1991, but its path was blocked for decades by a dispute with Greece over its very name, settled only by the 2018 Prespa Agreement, which renamed it the Republic of North Macedonia and opened the door to NATO membership in 2020. A 2001 ethnic Albanian insurgency was ended by the Ohrid Framework Agreement, and a continuing identity dispute with Bulgaria still complicates its bid to join the European Union.

## Ancient Macedon and the Classical Legacy — c. 800 BCE – 600 CE

The wider region gave its name to the kingdom of Philip II and Alexander the Great, then passed under Rome and Byzantium, even as the modern state's claim on that ancient inheritance remains contested.

- 359–336 BCE: **Reign of Philip II of Macedon** — Philip II reformed the Macedonian army and won mastery over the Greek city-states at Chaeronea, laying the foundations for the empire his son Alexander the Great would build across Asia. [philip-britannica, macedonia-region-britannica]
- 336–323 BCE: **Conquests of Alexander the Great** — Alexander III led Macedonian and Greek forces in the conquest of the Persian empire, carrying Hellenistic civilization from Egypt to the Indus before his death at Babylon in 323 BCE. [alexander-britannica]
- 148 BCE: **Macedonia becomes a Roman province** — After Rome defeated the last Macedonian claimant near Pydna, Macedonia was organised as a Roman province, beginning centuries of Roman and later Byzantine rule over the region. [macedonia-region-britannica]
- 518 CE: **Destruction of Scupi** — A devastating earthquake destroyed the Roman city of Scupi near present-day Skopje, an event marking the late-antique decline of the region's classical urban life on the eve of Slavic settlement. [nmk-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Philip II of Macedon (382 – 336 BCE) — King of ancient Macedon. The 18th king of Macedon, Philip II transformed a peripheral kingdom into the dominant power of Greece, reforming the army and defeating Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea, laying the foundations for his son's conquests.
- Alexander the Great (356 – 323 BCE) — King of Macedon and conqueror. Son of Philip II, Alexander III conquered the Persian empire and carried Hellenistic culture across the ancient world, one of history's most celebrated commanders and an enduring symbol over whose legacy modern states later contended.

## Slavic Settlement, Tsar Samuel and the Ohrid Archbishopric — c. 600 – 1018

Slavs settled the region, which became the heart of a Bulgarian state centred on Ohrid, a city whose Slavonic literary school and archbishopric made it a cradle of medieval Slavic Christianity.

- 6th–7th c.: **Slavic settlement of the region** — Slavic tribes migrated into the Macedonian interior from the sixth century, transforming the population of the region and laying the basis for its later Slavic-speaking identity. [macedonia-region-britannica, nmk-history-britannica]
- c. 886: **The Ohrid literary school of Clement and Naum** — Saint Clement of Ohrid, with Saint Naum, founded a Slavonic literary school at Ohrid, spreading the Slavic script and Christian learning and making the city a cradle of medieval Slavic culture. [ohrid-unesco, nmk-history-britannica]
- reigned to 1014: **Tsar Samuel's state centred on Ohrid** — Samuel built a powerful western Bulgarian state with its capital and a patriarchate at Ohrid, resisting Byzantine expansion for decades until his defeat by the emperor Basil II. [samuel-britannica, nmk-history-britannica]
- 1014: **Battle of Kleidion and the fall of Samuel** — The Byzantine emperor Basil II destroyed Samuel's army at Kleidion; the tsar died shortly afterwards, and by 1018 the region was fully reabsorbed into the Byzantine Empire. [samuel-britannica]
- 1018: **The Archbishopric of Ohrid** — After the Byzantine reconquest an autocephalous archbishopric was established at Ohrid, sustaining the city's role as a centre of Orthodox church life and Slavonic learning. [ohrid-unesco, nmk-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Saint Clement of Ohrid (c. 840 – 916) — Slavonic scholar and bishop. A disciple of Saints Cyril and Methodius, Clement established the literary school at Ohrid that spread the Slavic alphabet and Christian learning, becoming one of the founders of medieval Slavic literature and the region's patron saint.
- Tsar Samuel (died 1014) — Ruler of the western Bulgarian state. Samuel made Ohrid the capital and seat of a patriarchate of a powerful Balkan state that resisted the Byzantine emperor Basil II for decades, until his defeat at Kleidion in 1014 and death soon after.

## Ottoman Rule and the Macedonian Question — 1371 – 1912

Five centuries of Ottoman rule ended in a contest among neighbouring nations and competing revolutionaries, dramatised by the Ilinden Uprising of 1903 and the short-lived Kruševo Republic.

- late 14th c.: **Ottoman conquest of the region** — Ottoman forces conquered Macedonia in the late fourteenth century, beginning roughly five centuries of Ottoman rule during which the region's name lapsed as an administrative term. [macedonia-region-britannica, nmk-history-britannica]
- 1893: **Founding of IMRO** — The Internal Macedonian Revolutionary Organization was founded at Salonika to work for an autonomous Macedonia, its early leaders combining a Macedonian regional with a Bulgarian national identity. [imro-britannica]
- 2 Aug 1903: **The Ilinden Uprising** — On St Elijah's Day IMRO launched the Ilinden Uprising against Ottoman rule; though quickly crushed amid severe reprisals, it became a founding event of Macedonian national identity. [ilinden-ebsco, imro-britannica]
- Aug 1903: **The Kruševo Republic** — During the uprising the insurgents proclaimed a short-lived republic in the town of Kruševo, which the Ottoman army overran after about ten days. [ilinden-ebsco]

Figures:

- Gotse Delchev (1872 – 1903) — Revolutionary leader of IMRO. One of the most important leaders of the Internal Macedonian Revolutionary Organization, Delchev sought to broaden the movement beyond its Bulgarian core toward an autonomous Macedonia; he was killed shortly before the Ilinden Uprising of 1903.
- Damyan Gruev (1871 – 1906) — Co-founder of IMRO. A teacher and one of the founders of IMRO in 1893, Gruev was among the chief organisers of the revolutionary movement that mounted the Ilinden Uprising against Ottoman rule.

## Partition, 'Vardar Macedonia' and the Yugoslav Republic — 1912 – 1991

The Balkan Wars divided Macedonia among its neighbours; the Vardar portion passed to Serbia and Yugoslavia, where after 1945 a Macedonian republic, language and nation were formally established.

- 1912–1913: **The Balkan Wars and the partition of Macedonia** — The Balkan Wars ended Ottoman rule and divided historic Macedonia among Greece, Serbia and Bulgaria; the northern Vardar region passed to Serbia, becoming the core of the future republic. [macedonia-region-britannica, nmk-history-britannica]
- 1918: **Vardar Macedonia within Yugoslavia** — After the First World War the Vardar region was incorporated into the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia), where it was administered as part of Serbia and its Slavic identity discouraged. [nmk-history-britannica]
- 2 Aug 1944: **Proclamation of a Macedonian state (ASNOM)** — The Anti-Fascist Assembly for the National Liberation of Macedonia met on St Elijah's Day 1944 and proclaimed a Macedonian state within a future Yugoslav federation. [nmk-history-britannica]
- 1945: **Socialist Republic of Macedonia and the codified language** — A People's (later Socialist) Republic of Macedonia was constituted as one of Yugoslavia's six republics, and a standard Macedonian literary language was codified for the first time. [nmk-history-britannica]
- 1963: **The Skopje earthquake** — A powerful earthquake devastated Skopje, killing more than a thousand people; the city was rebuilt with international aid as the modern capital of the republic. [nmk-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Josip Broz Tito (1892 – 1980) — Leader of socialist Yugoslavia. The Partisan commander and lifelong leader of socialist Yugoslavia, Tito built a federation of six republics in which a Socialist Republic of Macedonia, with its own recognised nation and language, took shape after 1945.
- Krste Misirkov (1874 – 1926) — Philologist and national thinker. A scholar and publicist, Misirkov argued early in the twentieth century for a distinct Macedonian literary language and national consciousness, anticipating the codification of standard Macedonian that followed in 1945.

## Independence and the Name Dispute with Greece — 1991 – 2019

Independence came peacefully in 1991, but for nearly three decades the new state was blocked by Greece's objection to its name, until the Prespa Agreement renamed it North Macedonia.

- 8 Sep 1991: **Independence referendum** — Citizens voted by some 96 percent for independence, and the republic seceded peacefully from Yugoslavia under President Kiro Gligorov, alone among the Yugoslav republics in avoiding war. [nmk-independence-britannica, statedept-macedonia]
- 1993: **UN admission as 'FYROM'** — Owing to Greece's objection over the name, the country was admitted to the United Nations under the provisional designation 'the former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia'. [nmk-independence-britannica, statedept-macedonia]
- 13 Sep 1995: **The Interim Accord with Greece** — Greece and the country signed an Interim Accord under which Greece agreed not to object to its membership of international bodies referred to by the provisional name. [icj-interim-accord, statedept-macedonia]
- 5 Dec 2011: **ICJ rules against Greece** — The International Court of Justice held that Greece had breached the Interim Accord by objecting to the country's admission to NATO at the 2008 Bucharest summit. [icj-interim-accord]
- 17 Jun 2018: **The Prespa Agreement** — Greece and the country signed the Prespa Agreement, renaming it the Republic of North Macedonia (used erga omnes), terminating the 1995 accord and distinguishing the modern Slavic state from ancient Macedon. [prespa-text, icj-interim-accord]

Figures:

- Kiro Gligorov (1917 – 2012) — First President of independent Macedonia. A veteran politician who led the republic to a peaceful, negotiated independence from Yugoslavia in 1991, Gligorov steered the young state through its early years and the opening of the name dispute with Greece.
- Zoran Zaev (born 1974) — Prime Minister and signatory of the Prespa Agreement. As prime minister, Zaev negotiated and signed the 2018 Prespa Agreement with Greece, accepting the name 'North Macedonia' to unlock the country's NATO and EU prospects, a politically costly compromise.

## The Ohrid Framework, NATO and the Road to Europe — 2001 – present

An ethnic Albanian insurgency was settled by the Ohrid Framework Agreement; renamed, the country joined NATO in 2020, while a dispute with Bulgaria still complicates its European Union bid.

- 13 Aug 2001: **The Ohrid Framework Agreement** — The agreement ended the 2001 ethnic Albanian insurgency, rejecting territorial partition and granting the Albanian minority greater rights through decentralisation, equitable representation, language rights and double-majority voting. [ohrid-framework-pam]
- Dec 2005: **EU candidate status** — Having applied for membership in 2004, the country was granted European Union candidate status in December 2005, opening a long road toward accession. [eu-council-nmk]
- 27 Mar 2020: **North Macedonia joins NATO** — After the Prespa Agreement lifted Greece's objection, North Macedonia deposited its instrument of accession and became NATO's thirtieth member on 27 March 2020. [nato-membership, statedept-macedonia]
- 2020: **Bulgaria blocks EU accession talks** — Bulgaria vetoed the opening of EU accession negotiations over disputes about history, language and identity, including claims that Macedonian derives from Bulgarian. [bulgaria-ecfr, bulgaria-balkaninsight]
- 19 Jul 2022: **EU accession negotiations open** — Following a French-brokered compromise, North Macedonia formally opened European Union accession negotiations, though Bulgaria's conditions continued to complicate the process. [eu-council-nmk, bulgaria-ecfr]

Figures:

- Ali Ahmeti (born 1959) — Ethnic Albanian political leader. Leader of the ethnic Albanian National Liberation Army during the 2001 insurgency, Ahmeti turned to politics after the Ohrid Framework Agreement, founding a party that became a long-standing partner in the country's coalition governments.
- Boris Trajkovski (1956 – 2004) — President during the 2001 crisis. President of the republic during the 2001 conflict, Trajkovski helped steer the country toward the Ohrid Framework Agreement and its Euro-Atlantic course before his death in an air crash in 2004.
