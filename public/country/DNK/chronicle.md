# The Chronicle of Denmark: From the rune-stone kingdom of the Vikings to a Scandinavian monarchy that shed its empire and remade itself as a modern welfare state.

Founding: The Jelling Stone names Denmark — c. 965. Denmark's symbolic birth is carved into the larger of the two Jelling stones, raised about 965 by King Harald Bluetooth in memory of his parents, Gorm the Old and Thyra. Its inscription declares that Harald 'won for himself all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian', and it is the earliest written record of the name Denmark referring to a unified realm. Beside it stand two great burial mounds and a church, together a UNESCO World Heritage site often called 'Denmark's birth certificate', marking both the unification of the kingdom and its conversion to Christianity in the tenth century. The royal line that Gorm and Harald founded at Jelling is the ancestor of a monarchy that, in unbroken descent, endures to the present day, making Denmark one of the oldest kingdoms in Europe.

Denmark is one of the oldest continuous monarchies in Europe, its name first carved in stone on the great Jelling rune-stone of about 965, when King Harald Bluetooth proclaimed that he had won for himself all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian. From that Viking-Age core grew a North Sea power that briefly ruled England under Cnut the Great and that, under Queen Margaret I, joined Denmark, Norway and Sweden in the Kalmar Union of 1397. The Reformation made Denmark a Lutheran kingdom in 1536, but the early modern centuries brought repeated wars with Sweden and the loss of the eastern provinces of Scania at the Treaty of Roskilde in 1658. Defeat in the Napoleonic Wars cost Denmark Norway at the Treaty of Kiel in 1814, and absolute monarchy gave way to constitutional government with the June Constitution of 1849. The nineteenth century was scarred by the Schleswig-Holstein question, culminating in the catastrophic defeat of 1864; the present border was fixed only after the 1920 plebiscite returned Northern Schleswig to Denmark. Through the twentieth century Denmark endured German occupation, helped found NATO in 1949 and joined the European Community in 1973, while Greenland and the Faroe Islands remain self-governing parts of a Danish Realm whose last territorial dispute, over Hans Island, was settled with Canada only in 2022.

## The Viking Age and the Birth of the Kingdom — c. 800 – 1042

Out of the raiding and trading of the Viking Age, a royal dynasty at Jelling united the Danes, embraced Christianity and built a short-lived North Sea empire reaching across to England.

- c. 936–958: **Reign of Gorm the Old at Jelling** — Gorm the Old, the first historically recognised king of Denmark, ruled from Jelling in Jutland and raised the older Jelling stone in memory of his wife Thyra, founding the royal line that endures today. [denmark-viking-britannica, natmus-gorm-harald]
- c. 965: **The Jelling Stone names Denmark** — King Harald Bluetooth raised a great rune-stone at Jelling proclaiming that he had unified Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian; it carries the earliest written record of the name Denmark. [jelling-unesco, natmus-jelling-stone]
- c. 965: **Christianization of the Danes** — Harald Bluetooth accepted Christianity around 965, the official start of Denmark's conversion, binding the new kingdom to Latin Christendom and to the wider culture of medieval Europe. [harald-britannica, natmus-jelling-stone]
- 1016–1035: **Cnut the Great and the North Sea empire** — Cnut won the English throne in 1016, inherited Denmark in 1018 and conquered Norway in 1028, ruling a maritime empire across the North Sea until his death in 1035. [denmark-viking-britannica, natmus-gorm-harald]

Figures:

- Harald Bluetooth (c. 910 – c. 987) — King of Denmark, unifier and Christianizer. Harald I completed the unification of Denmark begun by his father Gorm, converted the Danes to Christianity around 965 and recorded both feats on the great Jelling rune-stone, the document that first names Denmark. His byname later gave its name to the wireless Bluetooth standard.
- Cnut the Great (c. 995 – 1035) — King of England, Denmark and Norway. Grandson of Harald Bluetooth, Cnut secured England in 1016, Denmark in 1018 and Norway in 1028, ruling a North Sea empire that made him the foremost ruler of northern Europe before its swift dissolution after his death.

## The Medieval Kingdom and the Kalmar Union — 1042 – 1523

A consolidating medieval monarchy reached its widest span when Queen Margaret I bound Denmark, Norway and Sweden into the Kalmar Union, the greatest of the Nordic states.

- 1397: **The Kalmar Union** — At Kalmar the magnates of Denmark, Norway and Sweden crowned Erik of Pomerania as common king under the regency of Margaret I, uniting the three Scandinavian kingdoms in a single monarchy. [kalmar-britannica, margaret-britannica]
- 1387–1412: **The rule of Margaret I** — Margaret I governed Denmark, Norway and Sweden as their effective ruler from the late 1380s until her death in 1412, the architect and guarantor of the Kalmar Union. [margaret-britannica, denmark-margaret-britannica]
- 1520: **The Stockholm Bloodbath** — King Christian II's execution of Swedish nobles after his coronation in Stockholm inflamed Swedish resistance and helped precipitate the collapse of the Kalmar Union. [kalmar-britannica]
- 1523: **Sweden leaves the union** — Gustav Vasa's rising drove out Danish rule and led to Sweden's secession from the Kalmar Union, leaving Denmark joined to Norway alone. [kalmar-britannica, denmark-margaret-britannica]

Figures:

- Margaret I (1353 – 1412) — Queen and regent, founder of the Kalmar Union. One of the ablest rulers of medieval Scandinavia, Margaret united Denmark, Norway and Sweden under a single crown in the Kalmar Union of 1397 and ruled the three kingdoms in fact until her death, an extraordinary feat of statecraft for a woman of her age.
- Erik of Pomerania (c. 1382 – 1459) — First king of the Kalmar Union. Margaret I's great-nephew, crowned common king of the three realms at Kalmar in 1397. His later reign, marked by wars and heavy taxation, strained the union and ended in his deposition.

## Reformation, Denmark-Norway and the Wars with Sweden — 1523 – 1720

A Lutheran kingdom rose from the Reformation and ruled a twin realm of Denmark-Norway, but a long contest with Sweden stripped away the rich eastern provinces of Scania.

- 1536: **The Reformation under Christian III** — After winning the Count's War, Christian III dissolved the authority of the Catholic bishops and established a Lutheran state church, making Denmark-Norway a Protestant realm and strengthening the crown. [christian-iii-britannica, denmark-reformation-britannica]
- 1563–1720: **The long wars with Sweden** — From the Northern Seven Years' War onward, Denmark and Sweden fought repeatedly for control of the Baltic and the Sound, a rivalry that steadily eroded Danish power in the region. [denmark-reformation-britannica, roskilde-britannica]
- 26 Feb 1658: **Treaty of Roskilde** — After Sweden's invasion across the frozen belts, Denmark ceded Scania, Halland, Blekinge, Bornholm and Trondheim; the loss of the eastern provinces was the largest in Danish history. [roskilde-britannica, copenhagen-treaty-britannica]
- 1660–1661: **The establishment of absolutism** — In the crisis following defeat by Sweden, Frederick III secured a hereditary and absolute monarchy, a system later codified in the Royal Law (Kongeloven) of 1665. [denmark-reformation-britannica]

Figures:

- Christian III (1503 – 1559) — King of Denmark and Norway, founder of the Lutheran church. Christian III won the Count's War and in 1536 established the Lutheran state church, arresting the Catholic bishops and seizing their lands. By tying the church to the crown he laid the groundwork for the later absolutist monarchy.
- Frederick III (1609 – 1670) — King and architect of Danish absolutism. Frederick III reigned through the disastrous wars with Sweden and the Treaty of Roskilde, then exploited the post-war crisis to establish a hereditary, absolute monarchy in 1660-1661, a system that endured until 1849.

## The Napoleonic Catastrophe and the Birth of Constitutional Denmark — 1720 – 1864

Drawn into the Napoleonic Wars on the losing side, Denmark lost Norway at the Treaty of Kiel, then dismantled its own absolutism with the June Constitution of 1849.

- 1807: **The bombardment of Copenhagen** — To stop the Danish fleet falling to Napoleon, a British force bombarded Copenhagen and carried off the navy, driving Denmark into alliance with France and toward ruin in the Napoleonic Wars. [denmark-history-state, kiel-britannica]
- 14 Jan 1814: **Treaty of Kiel: the loss of Norway** — Defeated alongside Napoleon, Denmark ceded Norway to Sweden, ending a four-century union, while keeping Iceland, the Faroe Islands and Greenland. [kiel-britannica]
- 5 Jun 1849: **The June Constitution** — Frederick VII signed the Grundlov, abolishing absolute monarchy and establishing a constitutional government with an elected parliament; 5 June is celebrated as Constitution Day. [frederick-vii-britannica, natmus-nation-state]
- 1840s: **The Danish Golden Age** — In the decades after the loss of Norway, Denmark experienced a flowering of philosophy, science, art and literature, from Soren Kierkegaard to Hans Christian Andersen and the physicist H. C. Orsted. [denmark-dk-history]

Figures:

- Frederick VII (1808 – 1863) — Last absolute and first constitutional king. Frederick VII renounced absolute rule and on 5 June 1849 signed the June Constitution, transforming Denmark into a constitutional monarchy. The Schleswig-Holstein question dominated and embittered his reign.
- Hans Christian Andersen (1805 – 1875) — Author of fairy tales. Denmark's most celebrated writer, Andersen gave the world fairy tales such as 'The Little Mermaid' and 'The Ugly Duckling' during the Golden Age, becoming one of his nation's enduring cultural emblems.

## The Schleswig Question and the Making of the Modern Border — 1864 – 1920

The disastrous war of 1864 stripped Denmark of Schleswig and Holstein; only the plebiscite of 1920 returned Northern Schleswig and drew the border that stands today.

- 1864: **The Second Schleswig War** — Prussian and Austrian armies invaded after Denmark sought to incorporate Schleswig; following defeat at Dybbol, Denmark ceded Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg, losing about a third of its territory. [german-danish-war-britannica, german-danish-settlement-britannica]
- 30 Oct 1864: **Cession of the duchies** — By the peace ending the German-Danish War, Denmark surrendered the duchies to Prussia and Austria, placing tens of thousands of Danes under German rule and reshaping the southern frontier. [german-danish-settlement-britannica, natmus-nation-state]
- 10 Feb & 14 Mar 1920: **The Schleswig plebiscites** — Under the Treaty of Versailles, Zone 1 (Northern Schleswig) voted about 75 per cent for Denmark and Zone 2 about 80 per cent for Germany, determining where the new border would run. [schleswig-plebiscite-nordics, german-danish-settlement-britannica]
- 15 Jun 1920: **Reunification of Northern Schleswig** — Northern Schleswig came under Danish sovereignty on 15 June 1920, marked by King Christian X's ceremonial ride across the old frontier; the boundary then fixed remains the Danish-German border today. [schleswig-plebiscite-nordics]

Figures:

- Christian IX (1818 – 1906) — King at the catastrophe of 1864. Christian IX came to the throne in 1863 amid the Schleswig crisis and reigned through the disastrous war of 1864. Through his children's marriages across Europe he became known as the 'father-in-law of Europe'.
- Christian X (1870 – 1947) — King of the 1920 reunification. Christian X rode across the former border on a white horse to mark the reunification of Northern Schleswig in 1920, an enduring image of national recovery. He later became a symbol of quiet resistance during the German occupation.

## Occupation, the Welfare State and the Modern Realm — 1920 – present

Through neutrality lost to German occupation, Denmark rebuilt as a NATO and European welfare state, while Greenland and the Faroes gained self-government and the last territorial dispute, Hans Island, was peacefully settled in 2022.

- 5 Apr 1933: **The Eastern Greenland case** — The Permanent Court of International Justice ruled that Denmark held sovereignty over the whole of Greenland, rejecting Norway's occupation of the eastern coast and confirming Danish title to the island. [eastern-greenland-pcij]
- 9 Apr 1940: **German occupation of Denmark** — Germany invaded on 9 April 1940 and Denmark surrendered within hours; a policy of negotiation gave way in August 1943 to direct German control and an active resistance movement. [natmus-occupation, denmark-history-state]
- Oct 1943: **Rescue of the Danish Jews** — Warned of a German round-up, Danes spirited more than 7,000 Jews across the sound to neutral Sweden, saving the great majority of the country's Jewish population from deportation. [natmus-occupation]
- 1949: **Founding member of NATO** — Abandoning its old neutrality after the occupation, Denmark became one of the twelve founding members of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in 1949. [nato-britannica, denmark-history-state]
- 1 Jan 1973: **Accession to the European Communities** — After a 1972 referendum, Denmark joined the European Communities on 1 January 1973, the forerunner of the European Union and a foundation of its modern foreign policy. [denmark-dk-history]
- 14 Jun 2022: **Hans Island agreement with Canada** — Denmark and Canada settled the 'Whisky War' by dividing Hans Island in the Kennedy Channel, ending a dispute dating to 1973 and creating Denmark's only land border with North America. [hans-island-cangeo, greenland-faroe-um]

Figures:

- Margrethe II (born 1940) — Queen of Denmark (reigned 1972-2024). Margrethe II reigned for fifty-two years as a popular constitutional monarch and accomplished artist before abdicating in January 2024 in favour of her son Frederik X, the first voluntary abdication by a Danish sovereign in nearly nine centuries.
- Niels Bohr (1885 – 1962) — Physicist and Nobel laureate. Founder of the Copenhagen school of quantum mechanics and 1922 Nobel laureate, Bohr was Denmark's foremost scientist; he escaped occupied Denmark in 1943 and contributed to wartime atomic research before championing the peaceful use of nuclear energy.
