Founding: Independence under the Zürich-London Agreements — 16 Aug 1960. The Republic of Cyprus was born on 16 August 1960, when British colonial rule ended on the basis of compromise agreements reached at Zürich and London in 1959. The new state had an intricate constitution sharing power between the Greek Cypriot majority and the Turkish Cypriot minority, a Treaty of Guarantee binding Britain, Greece and Turkey to uphold its independence and territorial integrity, and a provision allowing the United Kingdom to retain sovereignty over two military bases at Akrotiri and Dhekelia. The settlement explicitly ruled out both énosis (union with Greece), the goal of the Greek Cypriot nationalist movement, and partition. The arrangement proved fragile: within three years the power-sharing machinery had broken down, and the island has been entangled in one of the world's longest-running international disputes ever since.

Cyprus has been shaped by everyone who has ever sailed the eastern Mediterranean. Settled by some of the oldest Neolithic communities outside the Near East, it became a string of Greek city-kingdoms famous for the worship of Aphrodite, then passed in turn to Assyrians, Egyptians, Persians, the Hellenistic Ptolemies, Rome and Byzantium, becoming one of the first lands governed by a Christian convert. In the Middle Ages it was a Crusader kingdom under the French Lusignan dynasty, then a Venetian colony, before falling to the Ottomans in 1571 and passing to British administration in 1878. Independence came in 1960 under a complex Greek-Turkish power-sharing constitution guaranteed by Britain, Greece and Turkey, but the settlement broke down within three years; intercommunal violence in 1963-64 brought the deployment of the UN peacekeeping force UNFICYP, and a Greek-junta-backed coup in 1974 was followed by a Turkish military intervention that partitioned the island. A Turkish Cypriot state proclaimed in 1983 is recognised only by Turkey and was declared legally invalid by the United Nations; the Republic of Cyprus joined the European Union in 2004 with the island still divided.

## Neolithic Settlers, Greek City-Kingdoms and the Cult of Aphrodite — c. 7000 BCE – 58 BCE

From one of the Mediterranean's oldest farming villages to a constellation of Greek city-kingdoms famed for the worship of the goddess of love.

- c. 7000–4000 BCE: **The Neolithic settlement of Choirokoitia** — A farming community of round stone dwellings flourished at Choirokoitia, one of the best-preserved and most important prehistoric sites in the eastern Mediterranean, illuminating the early evolution of human society in the region. [choirokoitia-unesco, cyprus-history-britannica]
- from c. 1200 BCE: **Greek settlement and the city-kingdoms** — After the collapse of Mycenaean Greece, Greek-speaking settlers established the city-kingdoms of Cyprus, among them Salamis, Kition and Paphos, making Greek the island's predominant language. [cyprus-history-britannica, paphos-unesco]
- from c. 12th c. BCE: **The sanctuary of Aphrodite at Paphos** — Old Paphos became the principal centre of the worship of Aphrodite, whose temple bore witness to one of the oldest Mycenaean settlements and drew pilgrims from across the Greek world. [paphos-unesco, paphos-britannica]
- 8th–6th c. BCE: **Assyrian, Egyptian and Persian overlordship** — The Cypriot kingdoms paid tribute successively to the Assyrian, Egyptian and Persian empires, sharing in the wider Greek world's long struggle against Persian domination. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- c. 294–58 BCE: **Ptolemaic rule** — Following Alexander the Great's conquests, Cyprus passed to the Hellenistic Ptolemies of Egypt, who abolished the independent city-kingdoms and governed the island as a province until Rome took it in 58 BCE. [cyprus-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Aphrodite (Kypris) — Goddess of love, patron of the island. Greek myth held that Aphrodite rose from the sea-foam off the coast of Cyprus, and her sanctuary at Old Paphos became one of the most renowned cult centres of the ancient Mediterranean. So closely was she tied to the island that she was known simply as Kypris, 'the Cyprian'.
- Evagoras I of Salamis (c. 435 – 374 BCE) — King of Salamis. The most celebrated of the Cypriot Greek kings, Evagoras made Salamis a centre of Hellenic culture and led resistance to Persian control of the island, becoming a symbol of Cyprus's Greek identity in the classical age.

## Roman and Byzantine Cyprus: An Early Christian Island — 58 BCE – 1191

Under Rome and Byzantium, Cyprus became one of the first lands governed by a Christian convert and a stronghold of Greek Orthodox faith and art.

- 58 BCE: **Rome annexes Cyprus** — Cyprus was annexed by Rome and organised as a province; under Roman rule the island prospered, with Nea Paphos as its capital and a centre of the celebrated mosaic art later inscribed by UNESCO. [cyprus-history-britannica, paphos-unesco]
- c. 45–47 CE: **Paul and Barnabas convert the proconsul** — On their journey across Cyprus the apostles Paul and Barnabas reached the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus at Paphos, an early instance of a senior Roman official embracing Christianity. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- 5th c. CE: **An autocephalous Church** — The Church of Cyprus was recognised as self-governing (autocephalous), free from the authority of any neighbouring patriarch, a distinction its archbishops have defended throughout the island's history. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- from 649 CE: **Arab raids and a contested frontier** — From the mid-seventh century Cyprus suffered repeated Arab naval raids and for some three centuries lay as a contested zone between Byzantium and the Caliphate before Byzantine rule was firmly restored in 965. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- 11th–12th c. CE: **The painted churches of the Troödos** — The mountain region of Troödos accumulated one of the largest groups of Byzantine churches and monasteries, richly decorated with murals later inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. [troodos-unesco]

Figures:

- Saint Barnabas (1st century CE) — Apostle and patron of the Church of Cyprus. A native of Cyprus and companion of Paul, Barnabas evangelised his homeland and is venerated as the founder of the Cypriot Church. The later discovery of his tomb near Salamis was used to support the Church of Cyprus's claim to autocephaly.
- Sergius Paulus (1st century CE) — Roman proconsul of Cyprus. Roman governor of Cyprus who, according to the Acts of the Apostles, was converted to Christianity by Paul at Paphos, an episode marking the island as an early outpost of the faith within the Roman administration.

## Crusader Kingdom, Venetian Colony and Ottoman Conquest — 1191 – 1878

Seized by a Crusader king, ruled for three centuries by a French dynasty, sold to Venice and then taken by the Ottomans, Cyprus became a layered Mediterranean society.

- 1191: **Richard the Lionheart seizes Cyprus** — En route to the Third Crusade, King Richard I of England captured Cyprus from its Byzantine governor, ending Byzantine rule and opening the island to Latin Crusader control. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- 1192: **The Lusignan Kingdom of Cyprus** — Guy de Lusignan, former king of Jerusalem, took possession of Cyprus, founding a French feudal dynasty that ruled the island as a Latin Crusader kingdom for nearly three centuries. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- 1489: **Cyprus ceded to Venice** — Caterina Cornaro, widow of the last Lusignan king, ceded Cyprus to the Republic of Venice, which ruled it as a fortified colonial outpost and bulwark against the Ottomans. [cyprus-history-britannica]
- 1570–1571: **Ottoman conquest of Cyprus** — An Ottoman army invaded in 1570, taking Nicosia, and after a long siege captured Famagusta in 1571, ending Venetian rule and beginning more than three centuries of Ottoman administration. [cyprus-ottoman-britannica, cyprus-history-britannica]
- after 1571: **Two communities take shape** — Under Ottoman rule the Orthodox Church was restored and its archbishop made leader of the Greek Cypriots, while Muslim settlement established the Turkish Cypriot community alongside it. [cyprus-ottoman-britannica]

Figures:

- Guy de Lusignan (c. 1150 – 1194) — First Lusignan lord of Cyprus. A French knight and former king of Jerusalem, Guy acquired Cyprus in 1192 and founded the Lusignan dynasty, which transformed the island into a Latin feudal kingdom oriented toward Western Europe.
- Caterina Cornaro (1454 – 1510) — Last queen of Cyprus. Venetian-born queen and widow of the last Lusignan king, Caterina ceded Cyprus to the Republic of Venice in 1489, ending the Crusader kingdom and bringing the island under Venetian rule.

## British Rule, the Demand for Énosis and the EOKA Campaign — 1878 – 1959

Britain took over the island from the Ottomans in 1878 and held it as a colony, while many Greek Cypriots pressed for union with Greece and turned to armed struggle.

- 1878: **Britain takes over Cyprus** — Under the Cyprus Convention, Britain assumed the administration of Cyprus from the Ottoman Empire, which retained nominal sovereignty, gaining a strategic Mediterranean base near the route to India. [cyprus-ottoman-britannica, statedept-cyprus]
- 1914: **British annexation** — When the Ottoman Empire joined the First World War against Britain, Britain annexed Cyprus; Turkey renounced its claim to the island in the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne. [cyprus-ottoman-britannica, bbc-timeline]
- 1925: **Cyprus made a Crown Colony** — Cyprus was formally declared a British Crown Colony, consolidating colonial administration over the island. [cyprus-ottoman-britannica, bbc-timeline]
- 1950: **The énosis plebiscite** — The Orthodox Church organised a plebiscite in which the overwhelming majority of Greek Cypriots voted for union with Greece; Britain refused, and Turkish Cypriots opposed it, sharpening communal division. [eoka-britannica, cyprus-history-britannica]
- 1 Apr 1955: **EOKA launches its armed campaign** — EOKA, led by Colonel Georgios Grivas with the support of Archbishop Makarios III, began an armed struggle against British rule in pursuit of énosis, opening four years of insurgency and repression. [eoka-britannica]

Figures:

- Archbishop Makarios III (1913 – 1977) — Ethnarch and first President of Cyprus. Head of the Church of Cyprus and political leader of the Greek Cypriots, Makarios championed énosis, was exiled by the British, then accepted independence and became the republic's first president in 1960. He survived the 1974 coup against him and led Cyprus until his death.
- Georgios Grivas (1897 – 1974) — Military leader of EOKA. A Cypriot-born officer of the Greek army, Grivas organised and commanded the EOKA guerrilla campaign against British rule from 1955, remaining a hardline advocate of union with Greece long after independence.

## Independence, the Breakdown of Power-Sharing and UNFICYP — 1959 – 1974

A guaranteed republic of two communities was created in 1960, but power-sharing collapsed within three years, bringing intercommunal violence, a Green Line and UN peacekeepers.

- Feb 1959: **The Zürich-London Agreements** — Greece and Turkey reached a compromise at Zürich, endorsed in London by Britain and Cypriot leaders, setting the terms for an independent Cyprus that excluded both énosis and partition. [eoka-britannica, cyprus-history-britannica]
- 16 Aug 1960: **Independence and the Treaty of Guarantee** — The Republic of Cyprus became independent under a Greek-Turkish power-sharing constitution; the Treaty of Guarantee bound Britain, Greece and Turkey to its independence and territorial integrity, and Britain kept two Sovereign Base Areas. [treaty-guarantee-un, statedept-cyprus]
- Dec 1963: **Breakdown and the Green Line** — After President Makarios proposed constitutional changes, intercommunal violence broke out in December 1963; Turkish Cypriots withdrew from government and a ceasefire line dubbed the 'Green Line' was drawn through Nicosia. [unficyp-establishment, bbc-timeline]
- 4 Mar 1964: **UN Security Council Resolution 186 establishes UNFICYP** — The Security Council unanimously recommended, with the consent of the Government of Cyprus, the creation of the UN Peace-keeping Force in Cyprus to prevent a recurrence of fighting and help restore order; UNFICYP became operational on 27 March 1964. [unficyp-establishment, unficyp-peacekeeping, cyprusun-unficyp]
- 1964: **A UN mediator for the Cyprus question** — Resolution 186 also recommended that the Secretary-General designate a mediator to promote a peaceful and agreed settlement, beginning the long international effort to resolve the dispute. [unficyp-establishment, cyprusun-unficyp]

Figures:

- Fazıl Küçük (1906 – 1984) — First Vice-President of Cyprus. Leader of the Turkish Cypriot community and the republic's first vice-president under the 1960 constitution, Küçük defended the community's guaranteed rights and opposed the constitutional changes that preceded the 1963 breakdown.
- Archbishop Makarios III (1913 – 1977) — President of the Republic of Cyprus. As first president, Makarios led the new republic through its early crises, proposing the 1963 constitutional amendments that Turkish Cypriots rejected and presiding over the island as power-sharing collapsed and UN peacekeepers arrived.

## The 1974 Division, the TRNC and the European Union — 1974 – present

A coup and a military intervention partitioned the island; a breakaway state recognised only by Turkey was declared invalid by the UN, and a divided Cyprus joined the European Union.

- 15 Jul 1974: **The Greek-junta-backed coup** — The military junta ruling Greece, through the Cypriot National Guard, overthrew President Makarios in pursuit of énosis; Makarios escaped abroad and a hardline nationalist was installed. [cyprus-history-britannica, bbc-timeline]
- 20 Jul 1974: **Turkish military intervention** — Invoking its guarantor rights under the 1960 Treaty of Guarantee, Turkey landed forces on 20 July 1974 and in a second August offensive took the northern third of the island, partitioning Cyprus and displacing both communities; UNFICYP was thereafter tasked with supervising the ceasefire lines and the buffer zone. [cyprus-history-britannica, treaty-guarantee-un, unficyp-peacekeeping, bbc-timeline]
- 15 Nov 1983: **Declaration of the 'TRNC' and UN Resolution 541** — The Turkish Cypriot leadership proclaimed the 'Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus' on 15 November 1983; on 18 November the Security Council, in Resolution 541, considered the declaration legally invalid and called on all states not to recognise any Cypriot state other than the Republic of Cyprus. [unficyp-peacekeeping, cyprusun-unficyp, statedept-cyprus, bbc-timeline]
- 24 Apr 2004: **The Annan Plan referendums** — A UN reunification plan was put to simultaneous referendums; Turkish Cypriots approved it but about three-quarters of Greek Cypriots voting rejected it, leaving the island divided on the eve of EU membership. [bbc-timeline, cyprus-eu]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to the European Union** — The Republic of Cyprus joined the EU; the whole island is EU territory, but the application of EU law is suspended in the northern areas not under the government's effective control. [cyprus-eu]

Figures:

- Rauf Denktaş (1924 – 2012) — Turkish Cypriot leader. The dominant Turkish Cypriot political figure of the late twentieth century, Denktaş proclaimed the 'Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus' in 1983 and led it for two decades, championing a separate Turkish Cypriot statehood that won recognition only from Turkey.
- Archbishop Makarios III (1913 – 1977) — President of Cyprus. Target of the 1974 coup that sought to unite Cyprus with Greece, Makarios escaped the island and returned after the junta's fall to resume the presidency of a now-partitioned republic, which he led until his death in 1977.
