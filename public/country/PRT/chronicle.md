# The Chronicle of Portugal: From a frontier county of the Reconquista to a seaborne empire, holding one of Europe's oldest borders.

Founding: Afonso Henriques and the birth of the kingdom of Portugal — 1143 / 1179. Portugal grew out of the County of Portugal, a frontier territory of the kingdom of Leon. Its count, Afonso Henriques, asserted his independence and, by tradition following his victory over the Muslims at Ourique on 25 July 1139, began to style himself king. In 1143 his cousin Alfonso VII of Leon accepted Portugal's autonomy, a step usually associated with the conference at Zamora. Full recognition of the royal title came only on 23 May 1179, when Pope Alexander III, in the bull Manifestis Probatum, confirmed Afonso Henriques as king and placed the new kingdom under the protection of the Holy See in return for an annual tribute. Afonso pushed the Reconquista south, capturing Santarem and, with the help of crusaders, Lisbon in 1147; the conquest of the Algarve in 1249 completed the territory, and the Treaty of Alcanices with Castile in 1297 fixed a frontier that has scarcely changed since, making it one of the oldest stable borders in Europe.

Portugal is among the oldest nation-states in Europe, born as a frontier county on the Atlantic edge of the Iberian Reconquista. Afonso Henriques, count of Portugal, broke away from the kingdom of Leon, took the royal title after his victory at Ourique in 1139, won recognition of his autonomy in 1143, and in 1179 had his crown and the independence of Portugal confirmed by Pope Alexander III. The frontier with Castile, largely fixed by the Treaty of Alcanices in 1297, is one of the most stable borders in the world. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries Portugal led Europe's Age of Discovery, opening the sea route to India under Vasco da Gama and reaching Brazil under Cabral, and divided the unexplored world with Castile at Tordesillas in 1494. The country lost its independence to the Spanish Habsburgs in the Iberian Union of 1580 to 1640, recovered it under the House of Braganza, was devastated by the great Lisbon earthquake of 1755, invaded during the Napoleonic Wars, and saw Brazil declare independence in 1822. After the long Estado Novo dictatorship, the Carnation Revolution of 1974 restored democracy and led to decolonisation; Portugal joined the European Communities in 1986. Two territorial questions remain open with Spain: the town of Olivenca, held by Spain since 1801, and the maritime zone around the Savage Islands.

## From County to Kingdom: Afonso Henriques and the Reconquista — c. 1095 - 1297

A frontier county of Leon became an independent kingdom under Afonso Henriques, drove the Reconquista to the Algarve, and fixed a border with Castile that endures to this day.

- 25 Jul 1139: **Battle of Ourique** — By tradition, Afonso Henriques took the title of king of Portugal after defeating a Muslim army at Ourique, asserting the county's break from the kingdom of Leon. [afonso-britannica, portugal-reconquista-britannica]
- 1143: **Recognition of autonomy at Zamora** — Alfonso VII of Leon and Castile accepted the autonomy of Afonso Henriques, an early step toward the recognised independence of Portugal. [afonso-britannica, portugal-history-britannica]
- 1147: **Capture of Lisbon** — Afonso Henriques took Santarem and then Lisbon from the Muslims, the latter with the help of crusaders sailing to the Holy Land, advancing the Reconquista to the Tagus. [afonso-britannica]
- 23 May 1179: **Papal recognition in Manifestis Probatum** — Pope Alexander III confirmed Afonso Henriques as king and recognised Portugal as an independent kingdom under the protection of the Holy See, the founding act of Portuguese sovereignty. [afonso-britannica, portugal-reconquista-britannica]
- 1297: **Treaty of Alcanices** — King Dinis and Ferdinand IV of Castile confirmed Portuguese possession of the Algarve and fixed the frontier, creating one of the oldest stable borders in Europe. [alcanices-britannica, dinis-britannica]

Figures:

- Afonso Henriques (Afonso I) (c. 1109 - 1185) — First king of Portugal. Count and then first king of Portugal, Afonso Henriques broke from the kingdom of Leon, took the royal title after Ourique in 1139, won recognition of his autonomy in 1143 and papal confirmation in 1179, and drove the Reconquista south to Lisbon.
- Dinis I (1261 - 1325) — King of Portugal, 'the Farmer King'. A reforming and culturally minded monarch, Dinis settled the frontier with Castile by the Treaty of Alcanices in 1297, promoted agriculture and the Portuguese language, and founded the university that became the University of Coimbra.

## The Age of Discovery — 1415 - 1580

From the capture of Ceuta and the school of Henry the Navigator, a small Atlantic kingdom opened the sea routes to Africa, India and Brazil and divided the unexplored world with Castile.

- 1415: **Capture of Ceuta** — Portuguese forces took the North African port of Ceuta, the starting point of overseas expansion and of Prince Henry's interest in exploring the African coast. [henry-britannica]
- 1488: **Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope** — Bartolomeu Dias led the first European expedition to round the southern tip of Africa, showing that the Atlantic and Indian oceans were connected and opening the route to Asia. [dias-britannica]
- 7 Jun 1494: **Treaty of Tordesillas** — Portugal and Castile divided the unexplored world along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, an agreement that underpinned Portugal's claim to Brazil. [tordesillas-britannica]
- 1498: **Vasco da Gama reaches India** — Vasco da Gama sailed around the Cape of Good Hope to Calicut, completing the sea route from western Europe to India and inaugurating direct trade with Asia. [gama-britannica]
- 22 Apr 1500: **Cabral reaches Brazil** — A fleet under Pedro Alvares Cabral, en route to India, made landfall on the coast of Brazil and claimed it for the Portuguese crown. [cabral-britannica]

Figures:

- Henry the Navigator (1394 - 1460) — Prince and patron of exploration. Prince of Portugal who sponsored the early voyages of the Age of Discovery, drawing navigators and cartographers to Sagres and directing the exploration of Madeira, the Azores and the West African coast, though he never sailed on the expeditions himself.
- Vasco da Gama (c. 1460s - 1524) — Navigator. Commander of the first fleet to sail from Europe to India around the Cape of Good Hope, reaching Calicut in 1498 and opening the maritime spice route that made Portugal a global commercial power.

## The Iberian Union and the Braganza Restoration — 1580 - 1750

A succession crisis bound Portugal to the Spanish crown for sixty years; a nationalist revolution in 1640 restored independence under the House of Braganza.

- 1578: **Disaster at Alcacer Quibir** — King Sebastian was killed leading a crusade into Morocco, triggering the succession crisis that opened the way to Spanish rule. [iberian-union-britannica]
- 1580-1581: **Iberian Union under Philip II** — Philip II of Spain occupied Portugal and was accepted as king by the Cortes at Tomar in 1581, uniting the two crowns for sixty years. [iberian-union-britannica]
- 1 Dec 1640: **Restoration and the House of Braganza** — A nationalist revolution proclaimed the duke of Braganza as King John IV, restoring Portuguese independence and founding the Braganza dynasty. [iberian-union-britannica]
- 1668: **Spain recognises independence** — By the Treaty of Lisbon, Spain at last acknowledged Portuguese sovereignty, ending the war of Restoration begun in 1640. [iberian-union-britannica, portugal-history-britannica]

Figures:

- John IV (1604 - 1656) — First Braganza king of Portugal. Duke of Braganza proclaimed king on 1 December 1640, John IV led the Restoration of Portuguese independence and founded the dynasty that ruled Portugal until 1910.
- Philip II of Spain (1527 - 1598) — King of Spain and Portugal. As Philip I of Portugal he united the Iberian crowns in 1580, ruling a vast composite monarchy; Portuguese resentment of his successors' policies fuelled the revolution of 1640.

## Earthquake, Napoleon and the Loss of Brazil — 1750 - 1834

A catastrophic earthquake, the reforms of Pombal, the flight of the royal court to Brazil under Napoleonic invasion, and the independence of Brazil transformed the kingdom and its empire.

- 1 Nov 1755: **The great Lisbon earthquake** — An earthquake, tsunami and fire destroyed much of Lisbon and killed tens of thousands, prompting the reconstruction and reforms directed by the Marquis of Pombal. [lisbon-quake-britannica, pombal-britannica]
- 27 Nov 1807: **Court flees to Brazil** — As a French army invaded, the prince regent and royal court sailed for Brazil under British escort, moving the seat of the Portuguese monarchy to Rio de Janeiro. [peninsular-britannica, john-vi-britannica]
- 1808-1814: **The Peninsular War** — British, Portuguese and Spanish forces fought the French in the Iberian Peninsula, eventually expelling Napoleon's armies from Portugal and Spain. [peninsular-britannica]
- 7 Sep 1822: **Independence of Brazil** — Pedro, regent and son of John VI, declared Brazil independent and became its emperor, ending Portugal's rule over its largest and wealthiest colony. [pedro-i-britannica, john-vi-britannica]

Figures:

- Marquis of Pombal (1699 - 1782) — Chief minister and reformer. Sebastiao Jose de Carvalho e Melo directed the rebuilding of Lisbon after the 1755 earthquake and dominated Portuguese government for a generation, expelling the Jesuits and pursuing enlightened, centralising reforms.
- John VI (1767 - 1826) — King of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves. Prince regent and later king who moved the court to Brazil to escape Napoleon's invasion in 1807, raised Brazil to a kingdom in 1815, and returned to Lisbon in 1821 shortly before Brazil declared independence under his son Pedro.

## Republic, Dictatorship, Revolution and Europe — 1910 - present

The fall of the monarchy, the long Salazar dictatorship, the Carnation Revolution of 1974, decolonisation and accession to the European Communities reshaped modern Portugal, leaving two territorial disputes with Spain unresolved.

- 1932-1968: **Salazar and the Estado Novo** — Antonio de Oliveira Salazar, prime minister from 1932, built an authoritarian corporative dictatorship, the Estado Novo, that ruled Portugal for over four decades. [salazar-britannica, estado-novo-britannica]
- 1801 / 1815: **The Olivenca dispute** — Spain occupied Olivenca in 1801 and acquired it by the Treaty of Badajoz; the Congress of Vienna recommended its return to Portugal in 1815, but the sovereignty remains contested. [olivenca-eulex, alcanices-britannica]
- 25 Apr 1974: **The Carnation Revolution** — Officers of the Armed Forces Movement overthrew the Estado Novo in a nearly bloodless coup, ending the dictatorship and beginning Portugal's transition to democracy. [carnation-britannica]
- 1975: **Decolonisation of the African empire** — Following the revolution, Portugal's African colonies, including Angola and Mozambique, became independent in 1975, ending an empire begun in 1415. [carnation-britannica, estado-novo-britannica]
- 1 Jan 1986: **Accession to the European Communities** — Portugal joined the European Communities on 1 January 1986, together with Spain, anchoring its democracy in European integration. [portugal-eu]
- 2013: **The Savage Islands maritime dispute** — Spain disputed the exclusive economic zone Portugal claims around the Savage Islands, contending that the islets are rocks under the law of the sea, an issue examined in US State Department maritime studies. [selvagens-state]

Figures:

- Antonio de Oliveira Salazar (1889 - 1970) — Dictator and prime minister. An economics professor who became prime minister in 1932 and ruled Portugal for thirty-six years through the authoritarian Estado Novo, maintaining censorship, a single political movement and the African empire until illness ended his rule in 1968.
- Antonio de Spinola (1910 - 1996) — General and transitional president. A colonial war veteran whose book Portugal and the Future argued the African wars could not be won militarily, helping to incite the Carnation Revolution; he briefly served as president during the transition before resigning in 1974.
