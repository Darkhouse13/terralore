# The Chronicle of Bangladesh: From Buddhist Bengal and the wealth of the Mughals to the nation born in 1971.

Founding: Liberation War and independence — 26 Mar – 16 Dec 1971. Bangladesh declared independence from Pakistan on 26 March 1971, after the Pakistani army launched a brutal crackdown on the Bengali nationalist movement led by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, whose Awami League had won the 1970 election. After nine months of war and the flight of some ten million refugees to India, Indian forces intervened and the Pakistani army surrendered at Dhaka on 16 December 1971, the date now commemorated as Victory Day. The new state's civilisational roots, however, reach back more than a thousand years to the Buddhist Pala empire of Bengal.

The deltaic land of Bengal is one of the oldest cradles of civilisation in South Asia, ruled in turn by the Buddhist Pala emperors, the Hindu Senas, an independent Muslim sultanate and the fabulously wealthy Mughal province whose Dhaka muslin clothed the courts of the world. British conquest after the Battle of Plassey in 1757 reduced this rich land to a colonial periphery scarred by catastrophic famine. Partition in 1947 made it East Pakistan, but linguistic and political grievance — crystallised by the 1952 Language Movement and the 1970 election — erupted into the Liberation War of 1971. On 26 March 1971 independence was proclaimed and on 16 December the Pakistani army surrendered, founding the People's Republic of Bangladesh under Sheikh Mujibur Rahman.

## Buddhist and Hindu Bengal: the Pala and Sena Dynasties — c. 750 – 1204 CE

For nearly five centuries Bengal was ruled by the great Buddhist Pala emperors and then the Hindu Senas, an age of monasteries, learning and the splendour of Somapura.

- c. 750 CE: **Gopala founds the Pala dynasty** — Gopala is raised to power amid disorder and founds the Pala dynasty, a Buddhist imperial line that would rule Bengal and Bihar for some four centuries and spread Buddhism as far as Tibet. [britannica-pala]
- c. 800 CE: **Somapura Mahavihara built at Paharpur** — The Pala king Dharmapala builds the great monastery of Somapura at Paharpur, one of the largest Buddhist viharas south of the Himalayas and a centre of learning for centuries; today a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [unesco-paharpur, britannica-pala]
- c. 1204 CE: **Turkish conquest ends Sena rule** — The general Bakhtiyar Khalji conquers Bengal, ending the Hindu Sena dynasty and inaugurating centuries of Muslim rule in the delta. [banglapedia-islam, britannica-historybd]

Figures:

- Dharmapala (reigned c. 781–821 CE) — Pala emperor. The second and one of the greatest Pala kings, a champion of Buddhism who founded the Somapura Mahavihara at Paharpur, the supreme monument of Buddhist Bengal.

## The Bengal Sultanate and the Spread of Islam — 1204 – 1576

An independent Muslim kingdom emerged from Delhi's shadow, and through Sufi saints and settlers Islam took deep root in the Bengali countryside.

- 1342: **Iliyas Shah founds the Bengal Sultanate** — Shamsuddin Iliyas Shah unifies Bengal's principalities and establishes the independent Iliyas Shahi Sultanate at Lakhnauti, beginning more than two centuries of sovereign Muslim rule. [banglapedia-iliyas]
- 1494: **Reign of Husain Shah** — Alauddin Husain Shah presides over a golden age of the Bengal Sultanate, patronising Bengali literature and extending the kingdom's frontiers. [britannica-husainshah]
- 1576: **Mughal conquest of Bengal** — Akbar's forces defeat the last independent sultan at the Battle of Rajmahal, ending the Bengal Sultanate and absorbing the delta into the Mughal Empire. [britannica-historybd, banglapedia-islam]

Figures:

- Shamsuddin Iliyas Shah (reigned 1342–1358) — Founder of the Bengal Sultanate. The ruler who unified Bengal's warring principalities into a single independent sultanate and gave its people, for the first time, a common Bengali Muslim political identity.

## Mughal Bengal and the Wealth of the Delta — 1576 – 1757

As a Mughal province, Bengal became the empire's richest jewel — a land of rice, silk and the legendary muslin of Dhaka, woven so fine it was called woven air.

- 1610: **Dhaka becomes the Mughal capital of Bengal** — The Mughal governor establishes Dhaka as the capital of the Bengal subah, and the city grows into a hub of administration, military power and the global muslin trade. [britannica-historybd]
- 17th–18th century: **Dhaka muslin and the wealth of Bengal** — Mughal Bengal becomes the world's foremost exporter of fine muslin and a leading source of European trade with Asia, making the province the richest in the empire. [britannica-historybd]
- 1717: **The nawabs of Bengal become autonomous** — As Mughal central power fades after Aurangzeb's death, the governors of Bengal rule as near-independent nawabs over the empire's wealthiest province. [britannica-historybd]

## British Conquest and Colonial Bengal — 1757 – 1947

Plassey delivered Bengal to a trading company; what followed was famine, deindustrialisation, and the partitions that began to redraw the map of the delta.

- 23 Jun 1757: **Battle of Plassey** — Robert Clive's East India Company forces defeat Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah after the betrayal of Mir Jafar, beginning British political control of Bengal and, in time, of India. [britannica-plassey, britannica-siraj]
- 1769–1770: **The Great Bengal Famine** — An estimated ten million people — about a third of Bengal's population — die as monsoon failure compounds the East India Company's insistence on collecting land revenue from a starving peasantry. [britannica-historybd]
- 1905: **First Partition of Bengal** — Viceroy Lord Curzon divides Bengal into a Muslim-majority east (capital Dacca) and a Hindu-majority west, sparking the Swadeshi movement; the partition is annulled in 1911. [britannica-partition1905]

Figures:

- Siraj-ud-Daulah (c. 1733–1757) — Last independent Nawab of Bengal. The young nawab who resisted the East India Company's encroachments and was defeated and killed after the Battle of Plassey, the betrayal of his own commanders sealing the fall of independent Bengal.

## Partition, East Pakistan and the Language Movement — 1947 – 1970

The 1947 partition made the eastern delta a wing of Pakistan a thousand miles from its capital; the struggle to defend the Bengali language lit the fuse of a nation.

- 1947: **Partition creates East Pakistan** — The partition of British India makes Muslim-majority eastern Bengal into East Pakistan, a wing of the new state separated from its western half by a thousand miles of India. [britannica-historybd]
- 21 Feb 1952: **The Bengali Language Movement** — Students in Dhaka demanding that Bengali be a state language of Pakistan are shot by police; the martyrs become foundational to Bengali nationalism and, later, to UNESCO's International Mother Language Day. [banglapedia-language, unesco-motherlanguage]
- 1966: **The Awami League's Six-Point demand** — Sheikh Mujibur Rahman's Awami League issues a six-point programme for the political and economic autonomy of East Pakistan, the charter of Bengali self-determination. [britannica-mujib, britannica-awami]

Figures:

- Sheikh Mujibur Rahman (1920–1975) — Leader of the Awami League; founding father of Bangladesh. A co-founder of the Awami League and a superb orator jailed repeatedly by Pakistan's rulers, 'Bangabandhu' (Friend of Bengal) became the embodiment of Bengali nationalism and would lead his people to independence.

## Liberation War and Modern Bangladesh — 1970 – present

An election denied, a war of independence, and the long, turbulent making of a nation — from famine and coups to a garment-powered economy and the uprising of 2024.

- Dec 1970: **Awami League wins the election** — The Awami League secures 167 of the 169 East Pakistani seats and a National Assembly majority; West Pakistan's refusal to hand over power ignites the crisis that leads to war. [britannica-mujib, britannica-awami]
- 26 Mar 1971: **Declaration of independence** — After the Pakistani army launches Operation Searchlight on 25 March, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman proclaims Bangladesh's independence on 26 March, beginning the Liberation War. [britannica-liberation, britannica-mujib]
- 16 Dec 1971: **Victory and the birth of Bangladesh** — After a nine-month war in which possibly a million Bengalis were killed and ten million fled to India, Indian forces intervene and the Pakistani army surrenders at Dhaka, founding the People's Republic of Bangladesh. [britannica-liberation]
- 15 Aug 1975: **Assassination of Mujib and military rule** — Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and most of his family are killed in a coup; a counter-coup brings Ziaur Rahman to power, beginning fifteen years of military-dominated rule. [britannica-mujib, britannica-ziaur]
- 5 Aug 2024: **The 2024 uprising and Hasina's fall** — A student-led mass uprising over a civil-service job-quota system forces Sheikh Hasina to resign after fifteen years and flee to India; the Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus is appointed to head an interim government. [britannica-hasina]

Figures:

- Ziaur Rahman (1936–1981) — President of Bangladesh; founder of the BNP. An army officer of the Liberation War who took power after the 1975 coup, became president in 1977 and founded the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, emphasising food production and rural development before his assassination in 1981.
- Sheikh Hasina Wazed (born 1947) — Prime Minister of Bangladesh (1996–2001, 2009–2024). A surviving daughter of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman who led the Awami League and governed Bangladesh for fifteen unbroken years, overseeing rapid economic growth before being driven from power by the 2024 uprising.
