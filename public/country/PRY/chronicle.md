Founding: Independence from Spain — 14-15 May 1811. On the night of 14-15 May 1811, militia officers led by Pedro Juan Caballero and Fulgencio Yegros deposed the Spanish governor at Asuncion and ended colonial rule without bloodshed; both days are still celebrated as Paraguay's independence. The leaders of the province had refused to accept the authority claimed by Buenos Aires after the May Revolution of 1810 and chose instead to govern themselves. A junta was soon formed, dominated in practice by the lawyer Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia, and on 12 October 1813 a congress formally proclaimed the Republic of Paraguay, one of the first enduring republics of the new world.

Paraguay's history is rooted in the Guarani peoples of the Parana and Paraguay river basins, whose language remains co-official with Spanish and is spoken by most of the nation today. Spaniards founded Asuncion in 1537, the 'mother of cities' from which much of southern South America was settled, and in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Jesuit missions gathered the Guarani into reducciones until the order's expulsion in 1767. Independence from Spain came in May 1811, followed by the long isolationist dictatorship of Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia and the modernising rule of Carlos Antonio Lopez and his son Francisco Solano Lopez, whose War of the Triple Alliance (1864-1870) against Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay devastated the country and killed a vast, disputed share of its population. The twentieth century brought the Chaco War with Bolivia (1932-1935), the thirty-five-year dictatorship of Alfredo Stroessner (1954-1989), and finally a return to democracy and a new constitution in 1992, in an era defined economically by the binational Itaipu hydroelectric dam.

## The Guarani World and the Founding of Asuncion — Pre-1537 - 1607

Long before any European sail reached the rivers, the Guarani peoples cultivated and roamed the basins of the Parana and Paraguay; in 1537 the Spanish founded Asuncion, the 'mother of cities', from which they settled much of southern South America.

- by c. 8000 BCE: **Peopling of the river basins** — Indigenous peoples occupy the basins of the Parana and Paraguay rivers over many millennia, with Guarani-speaking farming and forest peoples coming to predominate in the historic era. [loc-paraguay-study, britannica-paraguay]
- 15 Aug 1537: **Founding of Asuncion** — Spaniards complete a stockade on the Paraguay River on the feast of the Assumption, founding Asuncion, the 'mother of cities' from which much of the south was settled. [britannica-asuncion, britannica-paraguay]
- 1541 CE: **Asuncion becomes the Spanish base** — After the first Buenos Aires is abandoned, its survivors flee to Asuncion, which becomes the headquarters of Spanish colonial activity in eastern South America. [britannica-asuncion]

## The Jesuit Missions and the Colonial Province — 1607 - 1811

For over 150 years the Jesuits gathered the Guarani into mission towns, the reducciones, whose ruins are now a World Heritage site; after the order's expulsion in 1767 and the rise of Buenos Aires, Paraguay's resentful leaders broke from Spain in 1811.

- 1609-1767: **The Jesuit reducciones** — The Jesuits gather up to 150,000 Guarani into some thirty mission towns in the Parana and Uruguay basins, blending Indigenous labour with Christian instruction and Baroque art. [britannica-paraguay-colonial, unesco-jesuit-missions]
- 1750-1757: **Treaty of Madrid and the Guarani War** — A planned transfer of seven mission towns to Portugal provokes Guarani resistance and a Spanish-Portuguese campaign, hardening official hostility to the Jesuits. [britannica-paraguay-colonial]
- 1767 CE: **Expulsion of the Jesuits** — Charles III expels the Jesuits from Spanish America; the Guarani missions are abandoned and fall into ruin, their populations scattered. [britannica-paraguay-colonial, unesco-jesuit-missions]
- 1776 CE: **Viceroyalty of the Rio de la Plata** — The new viceroyalty centred on Buenos Aires makes Asuncion and Paraguay dependent on the river port, feeding Paraguayan resentment of its decline. [britannica-paraguay-colonial]

## Independence, Dr Francia and the Lopez Dynasty — 1811 - 1862

After the break with Spain in 1811, Paraguay was sealed off from the world by the iron dictatorship of Dr Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia; his successors Carlos Antonio Lopez and Francisco Solano Lopez reopened the country and built it into an ambitious, militarised state.

- 14-15 May 1811: **Independence from Spain** — Paraguayan officers depose the Spanish governor at Asuncion without bloodshed, ending colonial rule; a republic is proclaimed in 1813. [britannica-history-paraguay, britannica-paraguay-colonial]
- 1814-1840: **The dictatorship of Dr Francia** — Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia rules as Supreme Dictator, sealing Paraguay off from the world in near-total isolation until his death. [britannica-francia, britannica-history-paraguay]
- 1841-1862: **Carlos Antonio Lopez modernises Paraguay** — Ending Francia's isolation, Carlos Antonio Lopez opens the country, signs commercial treaties, and builds railways, foundries and schools while enlarging the army. [britannica-carlos-lopez, britannica-history-paraguay]
- 1862 CE: **Francisco Solano Lopez takes power** — On his father's death, Francisco Solano Lopez becomes leader, inheriting a large army and ambitions that would soon plunge Paraguay into war. [britannica-solano-lopez]

Figures:

- Jose Gaspar Rodriguez de Francia (1766-1840) — Supreme Dictator of Paraguay. Lawyer and leader of the independence movement who, as 'El Supremo', ruled Paraguay from 1814 until his death in one of the most absolute dictatorships of nineteenth-century Latin America, sealing the country off from the outside world.
- Carlos Antonio Lopez (1792-1862) — President of Paraguay. Successor to Francia who ended Paraguay's isolation, opened it to foreign trade and undertook wide-ranging modernisation, while building the army his son would later lead into a catastrophic war.

## The War of the Triple Alliance — 1864 - 1870

Paraguay's war against the combined forces of Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay became the deadliest conflict in South American history, ending in the death of Francisco Solano Lopez and the loss of a vast, bitterly disputed share of the nation's people.

- 1864 CE: **Paraguay invades Mato Grosso** — Francisco Solano Lopez opens hostilities by invading the Brazilian province of Mato Grosso, the first move in a war that would soon include Argentina and Uruguay. [britannica-triple-alliance, britannica-solano-lopez]
- 1 May 1865: **Treaty of the Triple Alliance** — Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay sign a secret alliance against Paraguay, committing to wage war until the fall of Lopez. [britannica-triple-alliance]
- 24 May 1866: **Battle of Tuyuti** — The allies inflict a heavy defeat on Paraguay at Tuyuti, one of the largest battles of the war, beginning the slow destruction of Lopez's army. [britannica-solano-lopez]
- 1 Mar 1870: **Death of Lopez at Cerro Cora** — Brazilian troops kill Francisco Solano Lopez at Cerro Cora, ending the war; Paraguay has lost territory and a vast, disputed share of its population (figures are estimates). [britannica-triple-alliance, britannica-solano-lopez, britannica-history-paraguay]

Figures:

- Francisco Solano Lopez (1827-1870) — President and commander, War of the Triple Alliance. Son of Carlos Antonio Lopez who led Paraguay into the War of the Triple Alliance against Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay. He refused to surrender even as the country was overrun and was killed at Cerro Cora in 1870, leaving Paraguay devastated.

## Rebuilding and the Chaco War — 1870 - 1947

A shattered Paraguay rebuilt slowly under unstable governments and foreign economic influence, then fought and won the gruelling Chaco War against Bolivia (1932-1935), only to fall into political turmoil and a brief but bitter civil war.

- 1870s-1880s: **Rebuilding and the party system** — Paraguay slowly rebuilds after the war as foreign capital returns and land is sold off; the Colorado and Liberal parties emerge to dominate national politics. [britannica-history-paraguay]
- 1932-1935: **The Chaco War** — Paraguay and Bolivia fight a costly war over the Gran Chaco; about 100,000 men die before a 1935 truce, with most of the region awarded to Paraguay by the 1938 treaty. [britannica-chaco-war, britannica-history-paraguay]
- 1947 CE: **Paraguayan Civil War** — A brief, bloody civil war ends in victory for the Colorado Party, entrenching one-party dominance and clearing the path to authoritarian rule. [britannica-history-paraguay]

## The Stroessner Era, Democracy and Itaipu — 1954 - present

General Alfredo Stroessner seized power in 1954 and ruled for thirty-five years; his overthrow in 1989 opened a democratic era, marked by a new constitution that made Guarani co-official and an economy transformed by the binational Itaipu dam.

- 4 May 1954: **Stroessner seizes power** — General Alfredo Stroessner overthrows President Federico Chaves and assumes the presidency, beginning a thirty-five-year dictatorship under the Colorado Party. [britannica-stroessner, britannica-history-paraguay]
- 1984 CE: **Itaipu dam begins generating** — The binational Itaipu hydroelectric dam on the Parana, built with Brazil under a 1973 treaty, starts producing power, transforming Paraguay's economy. [britannica-itaipu]
- 3 Feb 1989: **Overthrow of Stroessner** — General Andres Rodriguez ousts Stroessner in a coup and wins elections later that year, opening Paraguay's transition to democracy. [britannica-stroessner, britannica-history-paraguay]
- 20 Jun 1992: **New democratic constitution** — A Constituent Assembly enacts a constitution establishing a presidential republic and making Guarani co-official with Spanish. [britannica-history-paraguay, britannica-guarani-language]
- 2008 CE: **First opposition victory** — Former bishop Fernando Lugo wins the presidency, ending more than sixty years of Colorado Party rule in Paraguay's first electoral transfer of power between parties. [britannica-lugo]

Figures:

- Alfredo Stroessner (1912-2006) — Military dictator of Paraguay. Army general who seized power in 1954 and ruled Paraguay for thirty-five years through the Colorado Party and the military, in one of Latin America's longest dictatorships, until his overthrow in 1989.
- Fernando Lugo (1951-) — President of Paraguay. Former Roman Catholic bishop who won the presidency in 2008, ending more than six decades of Colorado Party rule in the first transfer of power between parties in Paraguay's history; he was removed by a disputed impeachment in 2012.
