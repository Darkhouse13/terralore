# The Chronicle of Montenegro: A mountain stronghold that defied an empire, lost its crown to a union, and won back its statehood by a single percentage point.

Founding: Independence recognised at the Congress of Berlin — 1878. Montenegro had been effectively self-governing in its mountains for generations, but its sovereignty was first recognised in international law at the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Article XXVI of the Treaty of Berlin, signed on 13 July 1878, declared that 'the independence of Montenegro is recognized by the Sublime Porte and by all those of the High Contracting Parties who had not hitherto admitted it,' ending Ottoman suzerainty. The settlement also enlarged the principality and granted it the Adriatic port of Antivari (Bar), making Montenegro the twenty-seventh recognised independent state of its era. Montenegrins themselves trace the modern restoration of that status directly to 1878: when the country voted to leave the State Union with Serbia in 2006, official statements described the act as regaining the international standing first recognised at the Congress of Berlin.

Montenegro's history is the story of a small Balkan people that built and rebuilt its sovereignty against far greater powers. Its medieval root was the South Slavic principality of Duklja, later known as Zeta, which produced the first crowned king of the region in the eleventh century. As Ottoman power overwhelmed the Balkans, a remnant of free highlanders gathered around the Orthodox see of Cetinje, and from 1696 the Petrovic-Njegos dynasty turned this theocratic Prince-Bishopric, or Vladika, into a famous mountain redoubt of armed resistance to the Ottoman Empire, immortalised by the poet-prince Petar II Petrovic-Njegos. In 1852 the bishopric became a secular principality under Danilo; Montenegro's full independence was recognised by the Great Powers at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, and in 1910 Nikola I raised it to a kingdom. After the First World War the contested Podgorica Assembly of 1918 deposed the dynasty and merged Montenegro into the new Yugoslav state, dividing the nation between unionist "Whites" and pro-independence "Greens". For most of the twentieth century Montenegro was a Yugoslav republic; it remained with Serbia in the rump Federal Republic of Yugoslavia and the State Union of Serbia and Montenegro, until the narrow independence referendum of 21 May 2006 restored the sovereign state. Independent Montenegro joined NATO in 2017 and pursues European Union membership.

## Duklja, Zeta and the Coming of the Name Montenegro — c. 1000 – 1496

A South Slavic principality on the Adriatic produced the region's first crowned king, passed into the Serbian realm as Zeta, and under its last dynasty took the name by which the country is still known.

- 1042: **Duklja wins independence from Byzantium** — Stefan Vojislav led a revolt that freed the principality of Duklja from Byzantine control and established the Vojislavljevic dynasty, the first medieval state on the territory of Montenegro. [montenegro-history-britannica, duklja-familysearch]
- 1077: **Mihailo crowned king of Duklja** — Mihailo of Duklja became the first ruler of the region to bear the royal title, the crown bestowed on him by Pope Gregory VII, marking Duklja's standing among Christian states. [montenegro-history-britannica, duklja-familysearch]
- 1356–1421: **Principality of Zeta under the Balsic** — After the fall of the Serbian Empire an independent Zeta emerged, ruled by the House of Balsic and then from 1431 by the House of Crnojevic, under whom the name Montenegro came into use. [montenegro-history-britannica, duklja-familysearch]
- 4 Jan 1494: **The Oktoih and the Crnojevic printing press** — Djurdje Crnojevic founded the first South Slavic printing house at Cetinje, which produced the Oktoih, the first Cyrillic book printed in southeastern Europe, before closing around 1496 amid the Ottoman advance. [crnojevic-press-nbcg, montenegro-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Mihailo of Duklja (reigned c. 1050 – 1081) — First king of the medieval principality. Ruler of Duklja who in 1077 received a royal crown from Pope Gregory VII, becoming the first king of the South Slavic state that lay at the root of Montenegro.
- Djurdje Crnojevic (reigned 1490 – 1496) — Ruler of Zeta and founder of the first Slavic press. The last Crnojevic ruler of Zeta, who established at Cetinje the first printing house among the South Slavs; its Oktoih of 1494 was the first Cyrillic book printed in southeastern Europe.

## The Prince-Bishopric and the Petrovic-Njegos Resistance — 1516 – 1851

A free remnant of highlanders gathered around the see of Cetinje and became a theocratic state whose hereditary prince-bishops led a long armed resistance to the Ottoman Empire.

- c. 1516: **Rise of the theocratic Prince-Bishopric** — Authority over the free Montenegrin highlands passed to the Orthodox bishops of Cetinje, who combined spiritual and temporal power as vladikas in a theocratic state that renounced Ottoman overlordship. [vladika-britannica, montenegro-history-britannica]
- 1696: **Petrovic-Njegos dynasty established** — Metropolitan Danilo Petrovic-Njegos secured the right to name his successor, founding the hereditary Petrovic-Njegos line of prince-bishops that ruled Montenegro until 1918, the title passing from uncle to nephew. [danilo-metropolitan-britannica, vladika-britannica]
- 1830–1851: **Rule of Petar II Petrovic-Njegos** — The poet-prince Petar II strengthened the state, founded schools and reformed taxation, and wrote The Mountain Wreath, becoming Montenegro's most celebrated cultural figure. [njegos-britannica, mountain-wreath-rastko]
- 1847: **Publication of The Mountain Wreath** — Njegos published Gorski vijenac (The Mountain Wreath), a dramatic epic in verse set in eighteenth-century Montenegro that became a defining work of the national literature. [mountain-wreath-rastko, njegos-britannica]

Figures:

- Danilo I Petrovic-Njegos (c. 1670 – 1735) — First hereditary prince-bishop. Metropolitan of Cetinje who in 1696 founded the Petrovic-Njegos dynasty by securing the right to nominate his successor, anchoring the theocratic state and its resistance to the Ottomans.
- Petar II Petrovic-Njegos (1813 – 1851) — Prince-bishop and national poet. Vladika of Montenegro from 1830, a reforming ruler and the greatest poet of his language, whose 1847 epic The Mountain Wreath made him the most revered figure in Montenegrin culture.

## Secular Principality, the Congress of Berlin and the Kingdom — 1852 – 1914

Danilo turned the bishopric into a secular principality; international recognition came at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, and in 1910 Nikola I crowned himself king.

- 1852: **Montenegro becomes a secular principality** — Danilo renounced the office of prince-bishop, married and proclaimed himself prince, ending the theocracy; his 1855 code gave the principality its first modern legal framework. [montenegro-history-britannica, danilo-prince-britannica]
- 13 Jul 1878: **Independence recognised at the Congress of Berlin** — The Treaty of Berlin recognised Montenegro's independence under Article XXVI, ending Ottoman suzerainty, and under Article XXIX enlarged the state and granted it the port of Antivari (Bar). [berlin-treaty-frus, berlin-congress-search, montenegro-history-britannica]
- 1905: **First Montenegrin constitution** — Prince Nikola I granted Montenegro its first constitution, establishing an assembly and modern institutions as the principality consolidated its recognised statehood. [nikola-i-britannica, montenegro-history-britannica]
- 28 Aug 1910: **Montenegro proclaimed a kingdom** — On the fiftieth anniversary of his reign, Prince Nikola I proclaimed Montenegro a kingdom and assumed the title of king, the last monarchy created in the Balkans. [nikola-i-britannica, montenegro-history-britannica]
- 1912–1913: **The Balkan Wars** — Montenegro joined Serbia, Bulgaria and Greece in the First Balkan War against the Ottoman Empire and besieged Scutari, but was forced by the Great Powers to give up the captured city. [montenegro-history-britannica, nikola-i-britannica]

Figures:

- Danilo I, Prince of Montenegro (1826 – 1860) — First secular prince. The vladika who in 1852 secularised the state and became prince, issuing the 1855 legal code; his assassination in 1860 brought his nephew Nikola to power.
- Nikola I (1841 – 1921) — Prince and then King of Montenegro. Ruler for nearly six decades who won recognition of independence in 1878, granted a constitution in 1905 and crowned himself king in 1910, the last sovereign of an independent Montenegrin monarchy.

## War, the Podgorica Assembly and Yugoslav Montenegro — 1914 – 1991

Occupied in the First World War, Montenegro was merged into Serbia and Yugoslavia by the disputed Podgorica Assembly of 1918, splitting the nation before a wartime rebirth as a Yugoslav republic.

- Jan 1916: **Battle of Mojkovac and Austro-Hungarian occupation** — The Montenegrin army won the Battle of Mojkovac (7-8 January 1916) to cover the Serbian retreat, but the kingdom was overrun and occupied by Austria-Hungary, and King Nikola I fled into exile. [ww1-montenegro-online, mojkovac-traditiones]
- Nov 1918: **The Podgorica Assembly** — An assembly in Podgorica deposed King Nikola I and the Petrovic-Njegos dynasty and proclaimed the unconditional union of Montenegro with Serbia in the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. [ww1-montenegro-online, montenegro-history-britannica]
- Jan 1919: **The Christmas Uprising and the Greens-Whites split** — Pro-independence 'Greens' rose against the union proclaimed at Podgorica but were defeated by the unionist 'Whites', a schism over Montenegro's statehood that endured for generations. [ww1-montenegro-online, mojkovac-traditiones]
- 1945: **Socialist Republic of Montenegro** — After wartime occupation and partisan resistance, Montenegro became one of the six federal republics of socialist Yugoslavia, with its capital at Titograd, today's Podgorica. [montenegro-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Nikola I in exile (1841 – 1921) — Last King of Montenegro. Driven from his kingdom by the Austro-Hungarian occupation in 1916, Nikola I was deposed in absentia by the Podgorica Assembly in 1918 and died in exile in France in 1921, the dynasty's rule ended.
- Janko Vukotic (1866 – 1927) — Montenegrin army commander. Serdar who led the Montenegrin army to victory at the Battle of Mojkovac in January 1916, shielding the retreating Serbian forces in one of the country's most celebrated military feats.

## Rump Yugoslavia, the State Union and the Road to the 2006 Referendum — 1991 – 2006

As Yugoslavia broke apart, Montenegro stayed with Serbia in a rump federation and then a loose State Union, before a narrow referendum in 2006 restored independence.

- 1992: **Federal Republic of Yugoslavia** — As the other republics broke away, Montenegro joined Serbia in forming the rump Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, remaining bound to Belgrade through the wars and sanctions of the 1990s. [montenegro-history-britannica, princeton-self-determination-search]
- 2002: **Unilateral adoption of the euro** — Montenegro made the euro its sole legal tender from March 2002, a unilateral euroisation undertaken without European Central Bank agreement that distanced its economy from Serbia's. [cbcg-euroisation]
- 2003: **State Union of Serbia and Montenegro** — European mediation transformed the federation into the loose State Union of Serbia and Montenegro, whose Constitutional Charter allowed either republic to hold an independence referendum after three years. [coe-referendum-report, montenegro-history-britannica]
- 21 May 2006: **Independence referendum** — On a turnout above 86 percent, 55.5 percent of voters chose independence, narrowly passing the EU-set 55 percent threshold; the OSCE/ODIHR found the vote met international standards. [coe-referendum-report, odihr-referendum, montenegro-independence-britannica]
- 3 Jun 2006: **Restoration of independence** — The parliament in Podgorica formally declared Montenegro an independent sovereign state, recognised by Serbia, restoring the statehood lost at the Podgorica Assembly of 1918. [coe-referendum-report, montenegro-independence-britannica]

Figures:

- Milo Djukanovic (born 1962) — Leader of the independence movement. Dominant Montenegrin politician who steered the republic away from Belgrade, championed the 2006 independence referendum and led the country's bids for NATO and EU membership.
- Javier Solana (born 1942) — EU High Representative and mediator. European Union foreign-policy chief whose mediation produced the 2003 State Union of Serbia and Montenegro and whose office set the 55 percent threshold for the 2006 referendum.

## Independent Montenegro, NATO and Europe — 2006 – present

The restored state pursued Euro-Atlantic integration, joining NATO in 2017 and negotiating EU membership, while working to settle its borders with Croatia and Kosovo.

- 2008: **Application for EU membership** — Montenegro applied to join the European Union, beginning the accession process that would make it one of the leading candidates among the Western Balkan countries. [ec-enlargement-montenegro]
- 17 Dec 2010: **EU candidate status** — The European Union granted Montenegro candidate status, a milestone toward the accession negotiations the country has pursued while retaining the euro as its currency. [ec-enlargement-montenegro]
- 2015–2018: **Kosovo border demarcation** — Montenegro and Kosovo signed a demarcation of their 79-kilometre border in 2015; fiercely contested in Kosovo, it was finally ratified by Pristina's parliament in March 2018. [kosovo-border-ewb]
- 5 Jun 2017: **Accession to NATO** — Montenegro became NATO's twenty-ninth member, depositing its instrument of accession in Washington, despite strong Russian opposition and a divided domestic public. [nato-accession, nato-accession-search]

Figures:

- Dusko Markovic (born 1958) — Prime Minister at NATO accession. Prime minister who led Montenegro into NATO in June 2017, attending the accession ceremony in Washington as the country completed its principal Euro-Atlantic goal.
- Jens Stoltenberg (born 1959) — NATO Secretary General. Secretary General who welcomed Montenegro as the Alliance's twenty-ninth member in 2017, hailing its entry 'with a seat at the table as an equal' and its independence guaranteed.
