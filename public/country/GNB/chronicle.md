Founding: Independence from Portugal — 24 September 1973 (declared); recognised 10 September 1974. On 24 September 1973 the PAIGC's People's National Assembly, meeting in the liberated southeastern region of Boé, unilaterally proclaimed the Republic of Guinea-Bissau, some eight months after the assassination of the party's founder, Amílcar Cabral, in January 1973. The new state was swiftly recognised by dozens of countries and endorsed by a 93-to-7 vote of the UN General Assembly in November 1973, though Portugal still claimed sovereignty. That changed after the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 toppled the Estado Novo dictatorship in Lisbon: the guerrilla war in Guinea had been among the conflicts that exhausted the regime. The new Portuguese government negotiated with the PAIGC, signing an accord at Algiers in August 1974, and on 10 September 1974 Portugal formally recognised Guinea-Bissau's independence. The United States acknowledged independence the same day. Guinea-Bissau thus became independent twice over — by its own declaration in 1973, and by Portuguese recognition in 1974 — making it the first of Portugal's African colonies to win statehood.

Guinea-Bissau is a small West African nation of mainland river country and the offshore Bijagós archipelago, wedged between Senegal and Guinea. Before the Europeans came, its interior lay within the reach of the Mali Empire and then of Kaabu (Gabu), a Mandinka federation of warrior nobility that long outlasted Mali, while the coast and islands were home to the Balanta, Fula, Papel, Manjaco, Biafada and the seafaring Bijagós. From the mid-fifteenth century Portuguese navigators reached the "Rivers of Guinea", and the region — administered for centuries through Cape Verde — became an artery of the Atlantic slave trade centred on Cacheu and, later, Bissau. Portugal's actual conquest of the interior came strikingly late, completed only through brutal "pacification" campaigns into the 1910s and the subjection of the Bijagós islands by 1936. Amílcar Cabral's PAIGC waged one of Africa's most effective anti-colonial guerrilla wars from 1963; Cabral was assassinated in January 1973, the party declared independence on 24 September 1973, and Portugal recognised it on 10 September 1974 after the Carnation Revolution. Independence gave way to chronic instability — a 1980 coup, the 1998–1999 civil war, repeated coups, and a notorious role as a transit point for cocaine bound from Latin America to Europe.

## Kaabu and the peoples of the Rivers of Guinea — c. 1200 – 1867

The interior fell within the Mali Empire and then to Kaabu, a Mandinka federation of warrior nobility, while the coast and islands held the Balanta, Fula, Papel, Manjaco, Biafada and the seafaring Bijagós.

- 13th century: **Mali's reach and the rise of Kaabu** — The Mali Empire expanded west into the region, traditionally under Sundiata Keita's general Tiramakhan Traoré; the conquered lands became Kaabu (Gabu), a Mandinka province that would outlast Mali itself. [kaabu-wiki, history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- c. 14th–15th centuries: **Kaabu becomes an independent federation** — As Mali declined, Kaabu emerged as an independent federation of Mandinka kingdoms led by the Nyancho warrior nobility, centred on Kansala and dominant across the Senegambian interior. [kaabu-wiki, history-gnb-wiki]
- pre-colonial: **The coastal and island peoples** — The coast and rivers held the Balanta, Jola, Fula, Papel, Manjaco and Biafada, while the offshore Bijagós formed matrilineal warrior societies of seafarers and raiders — a fragmented mosaic of peoples and small states. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb, statedept-gnb]
- 1867: **The Battle of Kansala ends Kaabu** — A Fula army linked to Alfa Molo besieged and destroyed the Kaabu capital of Kansala; tradition holds the mansaba Dianke Walli blew up the gunpowder store rather than surrender, ending Kaabu and giving rise to the Fula kingdom of Fuladu. [kaabu-wiki, kansala-wiki, history-gnb-wiki]

## The Rivers of Guinea and the Atlantic slave trade — 1455 – 1850s

From the mid-fifteenth century Portuguese ships reached the Rivers of Guinea; administered through Cape Verde, the coast became an artery of the Atlantic slave trade centred on Cacheu and Bissau.

- 1455–1456: **Portuguese ships reach the Rivers of Guinea** — Expeditions under Alvise Cadamosto, sailing for Portugal's Prince Henry the Navigator, explored the coast and estuaries of Guinea, opening European trade in the labyrinth of rivers that gave the region its name. [portuguese-guinea-wiki, history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- c. 1588: **Cacheu fortified as a slaving station** — The Portuguese established a fortified post at Cacheu, which became the principal slave-trading centre on the rivers; captives were funnelled through it to Cape Verde and across the Atlantic. [portuguese-guinea-wiki, statedept-gnb, history-gnb-wiki]
- 16th–18th centuries: **The Atlantic slave trade via Cape Verde** — Administered through Cape Verde, the Rivers of Guinea became a major source of enslaved people; tens of thousands were carried to the islands' plantations and onward to the Americas, especially during the Iberian Union (1580–1640). [britannica-gnb, history-gnb-wiki, statedept-gnb]
- 1765: **Bissau founded as a fortified post** — The Portuguese founded Bissau in 1765 as a military and slave-trading station; it grew into the region's main commercial centre and, in 1942, replaced Bolama as the colonial capital. [portuguese-guinea-wiki, statedept-gnb, britannica-gnb]
- 1869: **Slavery abolished, contract labour follows** — Portugal abolished slavery in its territories, but replaced it with coercive "contract labour" arrangements; the transatlantic trade through Guinea's rivers had already declined across the nineteenth century. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]

## Portuguese Guinea and the late conquest — 1879 – 1956

Separated from Cape Verde in 1879, the colony was "pacified" only very late: a brutal conquest of the interior into the 1910s and the subjection of the Bijagós islands by 1936.

- 1879: **Guinea separated from Cape Verde** — Portuguese Guinea was detached from Cape Verde and constituted as a separate colony; after the Berlin Conference its frontiers with French possessions were fixed by agreement with France. [portuguese-guinea-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 1892: **A separate military district** — Lisbon made Guinea a distinct military district to promote "effective occupation", but Portuguese authority still reached little beyond the coastal forts and trading towns. [portuguese-guinea-wiki]
- c. 1912–1915: **Teixeira Pinto's pacification of the mainland** — The officer João Teixeira Pinto, with the auxiliary Abdul Injai, led brutal campaigns that crushed mainland resistance and brought the interior under Portuguese control for the first time. [history-gnb-wiki, portuguese-guinea-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 1935–1936: **The Bijagós islands subdued** — After repeated campaigns against the seafaring Bijagós, the Estado Novo finally imposed control over the archipelago in 1935–1936, completing the conquest more than four centuries after first Portuguese contact. [history-gnb-wiki, portuguese-guinea-wiki]
- 1951: **Renamed an "overseas province"** — Responding to international criticism, Portugal reclassified Guinea and its other colonies as "overseas provinces", recasting empire as an integral part of Portugal while colonial conditions persisted. [portuguese-guinea-wiki, history-gnb-wiki]

## Cabral, the PAIGC and the war of independence — 1956 – 1974

Amílcar Cabral's PAIGC waged one of Africa's most effective guerrilla wars from 1963; Cabral was assassinated in January 1973, independence was declared that September, and Portugal recognised it in 1974.

- 1956: **Amílcar Cabral founds the PAIGC** — The agronomist Amílcar Cabral and a small group founded the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde (PAIGC), the movement that would lead the struggle against Portuguese rule. [cabral-wiki, history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 3 August 1959: **The Pidjiguiti massacre** — Portuguese police opened fire on striking dockworkers at the Pidjiguiti quay in Bissau, killing scores; the killings turned the PAIGC away from peaceful protest and toward armed struggle. [pidjiguiti-wiki, history-gnb-wiki]
- January 1963: **The war of independence begins** — The PAIGC launched its armed campaign with attacks on Portuguese garrisons in the south, opening a guerrilla war that would steadily expand the "liberated zones" across the interior. [war-gnb-wiki, history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 20 January 1973: **Cabral assassinated in Conakry** — Amílcar Cabral was shot dead at a roadblock in Conakry by a disgruntled PAIGC member, Inocêncio Kani, and others, in a plot widely linked to Portuguese agents — eight months before independence was proclaimed. [cabral-wiki, war-gnb-wiki, history-gnb-wiki]
- 24 September 1973: **Independence declared at Boé** — The PAIGC's People's National Assembly unilaterally proclaimed the Republic of Guinea-Bissau in the liberated southeast; the new state was rapidly recognised abroad and endorsed by a 93–7 UN General Assembly vote in November. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb, statedept-gnb]
- 10 September 1974: **Portugal recognises independence** — Following the Carnation Revolution and an accord at Algiers, Portugal formally recognised Guinea-Bissau's independence on 10 September 1974; the United States acknowledged it the same day, with Luís Cabral as first president. [statedept-gnb, history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]

Figures:

- Amílcar Cabral (1924–1973) — Founder and leader of the PAIGC. Agronomist, poet and revolutionary theorist who founded the PAIGC in 1956 and led one of Africa's most effective anti-colonial guerrilla wars from his base in Conakry. Internationally admired as a thinker on national liberation and culture, he was assassinated at a roadblock in Conakry on 20 January 1973, eight months before independence was proclaimed.

## Instability, civil war and the cocaine route — 1974 – present

Independence gave way to chronic instability — a 1980 coup, the 1998–1999 civil war, repeated coups and assassinations, and a notorious role as a transit point for cocaine bound from Latin America to Europe.

- 14 November 1980: **Nino Vieira seizes power** — A coup led by João Bernardo "Nino" Vieira overthrew President Luís Cabral, fracturing the planned union with Cape Verde and beginning Vieira's dominance of national politics. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb, statedept-gnb]
- 1994: **First multi-party elections** — After the introduction of multi-party politics in 1991, Guinea-Bissau held its first competitive elections in 1994, which confirmed Vieira as president — though military factionalism persisted. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 1998–1999: **The civil war** — A military mutiny led by Ansumane Mané escalated into a civil war that drew in Senegalese and Guinean troops, devastated Bissau and displaced hundreds of thousands before Vieira was ousted in May 1999. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- 2 March 2009: **Vieira assassinated** — Having returned to the presidency in 2005, João Bernardo Vieira was assassinated by soldiers in March 2009, hours after the killing of the army chief of staff — a vivid sign of the army's grip on politics. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb]
- April 2012: **Coup amid the cocaine trade** — A military coup interrupted a presidential election in April 2012, deepening a pattern of intervention by a politicised army increasingly entangled with cocaine trafficking. [history-gnb-wiki, britannica-gnb, unodc-westafrica]
- mid-2000s onward: **A cocaine transit "narco-state"** — Weak institutions, porous borders and an archipelago of uninhabited islands made Guinea-Bissau a major transit point for Latin American cocaine bound for Europe; the UN described it as the first African "narco-state", with the drug trade rivalling the legal economy. [unodc-westafrica, globalinitiative-gnb, bbc-narcostate]

Figures:

- Luís Cabral (1931–2009) — First President of Guinea-Bissau (1974–1980). Half-brother of Amílcar Cabral and a PAIGC leader who became the independent republic's first president in 1974, governing a one-party state until he was overthrown in the 1980 coup led by Nino Vieira.
- João Bernardo "Nino" Vieira (1939–2009) — President of Guinea-Bissau (1980–1999; 2005–2009). A PAIGC war veteran who seized power in 1980 and dominated politics for decades; ousted in the 1998–1999 civil war, he returned as elected president in 2005 before being assassinated by soldiers in March 2009.
