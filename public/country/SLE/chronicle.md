Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 27 April 1961. On 27 April 1961 the Colony and Protectorate of Sierra Leone became an independent sovereign state within the British Commonwealth, ending more than 150 years of British rule that had begun with the Crown Colony of 1808 and the inland Protectorate of 1896. Its first prime minister was Sir Milton Augustus Strieby Margai, a Mende physician and moderate nationalist whose Sierra Leone People's Party had guided the territory through self-government. Independence was the culmination of a long and unusual history: a coast scarred by the Atlantic slave trade, the founding of Freetown as a settlement for freed and formerly enslaved people, a century as the Royal Navy's anti-slavery base and home of the Krio, and decades of constitutional advance under colonial rule. Sierra Leone became a republic on 19 April 1971; its founding moment as a modern nation, however, remains the transfer of sovereignty of 27 April 1961, still marked each year as Independence Day.

Sierra Leone takes its name from the Portuguese Serra Lyoa, the 'Lion Mountains' that ring the great natural harbour of Freetown, charted in the fifteenth century. Its coast was home to the Sherbro, Temne, Mende, and other peoples, reshaped in the sixteenth century by the Mane incursions, and from the late seventeenth century it became a notorious hub of the transatlantic slave trade, above all at Bunce Island. In a remarkable reversal, the Sierra Leone peninsula became a haven for freed and formerly enslaved people: the 'Province of Freedom' of 1787, then Freetown, settled by London's Black Poor, Nova Scotian settlers, and Jamaican Maroons. As a British Crown Colony from 1808 it served as the base for the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, whose courts emancipated more than 80,000 'Liberated Africans' who, with the earlier settlers, formed the Krio community. Britain declared an inland Protectorate in 1896, provoking the 1898 Hut Tax War led by Bai Bureh. Sierra Leone won independence from the United Kingdom on 27 April 1961 under Sir Milton Margai, slid into one-party rule under Siaka Stevens, and endured a devastating civil war (1991–2002) fuelled by 'blood diamonds', followed by the 2014–2016 Ebola epidemic and a long recovery.

## The Rice Coast peoples and the Mane — c. 1400 – 1600

Long before Europeans came, the Sherbro, Temne, Bullom, and others farmed and traded along the 'Lion Mountains' coast, a world reshaped in the sixteenth century by the Mane incursions from the interior.

- c. 1460s: **Portuguese chart the 'Lion Mountains'** — Portuguese mariners, an expedition associated with Pedro de Sintra reaching the area about 1462, charted the great harbour and named the surrounding hills Serra Lyoa, the 'Lion Mountains' — the origin of the country's name. [britannica-sierra-leone, pedro-de-sintra-wiki]
- before c. 1550: **The Rice Coast chiefdoms** — The Sherbro, Bullom, Temne, and other peoples farmed rice, fished, and traded along the coast and rivers, organised in chiefdoms and ritual societies rather than a single state. [britannica-sierra-leone, encyclopaedia-africana-sl]
- c. 1540s–1560s: **The Mane incursions** — Mande-speaking Mane raiders pushed to the coast and by the 1550s had subdued the Temne and other peoples, imposing a new ruling elite in a contested episode that reshaped the region's politics. [mane-people-wiki, history-of-sl-wiki]
- late 16th century: **Emergence of the Mende** — Out of the mingling of Mane aristocrats with indigenous Bullom and other populations the Mende identity and language took shape, spreading as a lingua franca and becoming one of Sierra Leone's largest ethnic groups. [mane-people-wiki, history-of-sl-wiki]

## The Atlantic slave trade and Bunce Island — c. 1560 – 1807

For more than two centuries the coast was drawn into the transatlantic slave trade, with Bunce Island in the Sierra Leone River becoming one of the chief British slave-trading castles on the Rice Coast.

- 16th century: **The coast enters the Atlantic trade** — European ships drew the rivers of the Sierra Leone coast into the transatlantic slave trade, exchanging goods and firearms for captives seized in raids and wars across the region. [slavery-remembrance-sl, britannica-sierra-leone]
- 1670s: **Bunce Island slave fort established** — The Royal African Company built a fortified post on Bunce Island in the Sierra Leone River, beginning the island's long and notorious role in the British slave trade. [sl-heritage-bunce, yale-glc-bunce]
- c. 1750: **Bunce Island at its height** — Under the London firm of Grant, Sargent and Oswald, Bunce Island became one of the largest British slave castles on the Rice Coast, with many captives — valued as skilled rice-growers — sold to planters in South Carolina and Georgia. [sl-heritage-bunce, yale-glc-bunce, rhs-bunce]
- 1807: **Britain abolishes the slave trade** — The British Parliament outlawed the transatlantic slave trade, ending Bunce Island's slave-trading operations and clearing the way for Freetown to become a base for naval suppression of the traffic. [britannica-sierra-leone, rhs-bunce]

## The Province of Freedom and the founding of Freetown — 1787 – 1808

On the same coast that had fed the slave trade, British abolitionists founded a settlement for freed and formerly enslaved people — the 'Province of Freedom' of 1787, then Freetown, settled by the Black Poor, Nova Scotians, and Jamaican Maroons.

- 1787: **The 'Province of Freedom' founded** — Abolitionists associated with Granville Sharp settled about 400 of London's 'Black Poor' on the Sierra Leone peninsula, founding the 'Province of Freedom' and the settlement of Granville Town. [blackpast-founding-sl, branch-foundings-sl]
- 1791: **The Sierra Leone Company chartered** — After Granville Town's destruction, the abolitionist-backed Sierra Leone Company was chartered to revive and govern the colony as a base for legitimate commerce and Christian settlement. [britannica-sierra-leone, blackpast-freetown]
- 1792: **Nova Scotian settlers found Freetown** — Some 1,100–1,200 Black settlers from Nova Scotia — African Americans who had sought freedom with the British in the American Revolution — landed and founded Freetown beneath the Lion Mountains. [blackpast-freetown, nova-scotian-settlers-wiki]
- 1800: **Jamaican Maroons arrive** — About 550 Jamaican Maroons, exiled after the Second Maroon War, arrived in Freetown and helped put down a revolt by the Nova Scotian settlers against the Sierra Leone Company. [blackpast-freetown, britannica-sierra-leone]

Figures:

- Granville Sharp (1735–1813) — Abolitionist and founder of the Province of Freedom. British abolitionist whose campaigning and financing established the 1787 'Province of Freedom' on the Sierra Leone peninsula, the settlement of free Black people that became Freetown.

## Crown Colony, Liberated Africans, and the Krio — 1808 – 1895

As a British Crown Colony from 1808, Freetown became the base of the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, whose courts freed more than 80,000 'Liberated Africans' who, with the earlier settlers, formed the Krio people.

- 1808: **Sierra Leone becomes a Crown Colony** — The British Crown took over from the Sierra Leone Company, making Freetown a Crown Colony and the principal base for the Royal Navy's suppression of the Atlantic slave trade. [britannica-sierra-leone, liberated-africans-wiki]
- 1808–1860s: **The West Africa Squadron frees the recaptives** — Courts at Freetown emancipated more than 80,000 'Liberated Africans' rescued from slave ships by the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, who were resettled in and around the colony. [liberated-africans-wiki, britannica-sierra-leone]
- 18 February 1827: **Fourah Bay College founded** — The Church Missionary Society founded Fourah Bay College in Freetown, the first Western-style university in sub-Saharan Africa, helping earn the city the name 'the Athens of West Africa'. [blackpast-fourah-bay, fourah-bay-wiki]
- mid-19th century: **The Krio community takes shape** — The Liberated Africans and earlier settlers fused into the Krio (Creole) people, with their English-based Krio language, Christianity, and Western education spreading influence across West Africa. [liberated-africans-wiki, saro-people-wiki]

Figures:

- Samuel Ajayi Crowther (c. 1809–1891) — First African Anglican bishop. A Yoruba recaptive freed at Freetown and the first student enrolled at Fourah Bay College, who became a linguist, missionary, and the first African bishop of the Anglican Church, emblematic of the Krio era's reach.

## Protectorate, the Hut Tax War, and independence — 1896 – 1971

Britain declared an inland Protectorate in 1896, provoking the 1898 Hut Tax War led by Bai Bureh; six decades later Sierra Leone won independence under Milton Margai, before drifting toward one-party rule.

- 1896: **The Protectorate proclaimed** — Britain declared a Protectorate over the interior of Sierra Leone, extending colonial rule over the Temne, Mende, and other peoples beyond the coastal Crown Colony. [britannica-sierra-leone, hut-tax-war-wiki]
- 1898: **The Hut Tax War** — The imposition of a tax on dwellings sparked armed resistance led by the Temne chief Bai Bureh in the north and spreading to the Mende in the south; Britain suppressed the revolt and exiled Bai Bureh. [hut-tax-war-wiki, bai-bureh-wiki]
- 1951: **Rise of the Sierra Leone People's Party** — Constitutional reform and the founding of the Sierra Leone People's Party under Milton Margai broadened African political participation and set the colony on the road to self-government. [britannica-sierra-leone, blackpast-margai]
- 27 April 1961: **Independence from the United Kingdom** — Sierra Leone became an independent state within the Commonwealth under Prime Minister Sir Milton Margai, ending British colonial rule begun in 1808. [statedept-sierra-leone, blackpast-margai]
- 19 April 1971: **Sierra Leone becomes a republic** — Sierra Leone adopted a republican constitution; two days later Prime Minister Siaka Stevens became the nation's first president, beginning a long consolidation of personal power. [britannica-stevens, usembassy-sl-history]

Figures:

- Bai Bureh (c. 1840–1908) — Temne war-leader of the 1898 Hut Tax War. Temne ruler and military strategist who led the northern revolt against the British Hut Tax in 1898, waging a celebrated guerrilla campaign before his capture and exile; a national hero of anti-colonial resistance.
- Sir Milton Margai (1895–1964) — First Prime Minister of Sierra Leone. Mende physician and moderate nationalist who led the Sierra Leone People's Party and guided the country to independence on 27 April 1961, serving as its first prime minister until his death in 1964.

## One-party rule, civil war, and recovery — 1971 – present

Siaka Stevens's one-party state hollowed the country out, setting the stage for a brutal civil war (1991–2002) fuelled by 'blood diamonds', followed by international justice, the 2014 Ebola epidemic, and a fragile recovery.

- 1978: **One-party state under the APC** — Siaka Stevens's new constitution made Sierra Leone a one-party state under the All People's Congress, entrenching personal rule, patronage, and economic decline. [britannica-stevens, usembassy-sl-history]
- 23 March 1991: **Civil war begins** — The Revolutionary United Front under Foday Sankoh, backed by Liberia's Charles Taylor, invaded from Liberia, beginning an eleven-year civil war marked by atrocities and funded by 'blood diamonds'. [blackpast-sl-civil-war, foday-sankoh-wiki]
- May 2000: **British intervention and UNAMSIL** — As the RUF again advanced on Freetown, Britain's Operation Palliser and a strengthened UN Mission (UNAMSIL) turned the war; Foday Sankoh was captured, breaking the rebellion's momentum. [operation-palliser-wiki, sl-civil-war-wiki]
- 18 January 2002: **The civil war ends** — With disarmament completed, the eleven-year civil war was declared over; estimates put the death toll above 120,000, with millions displaced and thousands maimed. [blackpast-sl-civil-war, sl-civil-war-wiki]
- 2012: **Charles Taylor convicted** — The UN-backed Special Court for Sierra Leone convicted former Liberian president Charles Taylor of aiding and abetting war crimes and sentenced him to 50 years — the first head of state convicted by an international tribunal since Nuremberg. [rscsl-taylor, un-news-taylor]
- 2014–2016: **The Ebola epidemic** — An Ebola outbreak reached Sierra Leone in May 2014, killing nearly 4,000 people and overwhelming health services before the WHO declared the outbreak over in November 2015, with recovery following. [who-ebola-sl, ebola-sl-wiki]

Figures:

- Siaka Stevens (1905–1988) — First President of Sierra Leone. Trade unionist and APC leader who became prime minister in 1968 and first president in 1971, turning Sierra Leone into a one-party state in 1978 and ruling until 1985 amid deepening corruption and decline.
- Foday Sankoh (1937–2003) — Leader of the Revolutionary United Front. Founder and commander of the RUF, who launched the 1991 invasion and led a campaign of terror through Sierra Leone's civil war; captured in 2000, he died in 2003 while awaiting trial for war crimes.
