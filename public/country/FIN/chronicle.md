# The Chronicle of Finland: Six centuries with Sweden, a century with Russia, and a hard-won independence between two great powers.

Founding: Independence from Russia — 6 Dec 1917. Finland's existence as a sovereign state dates to 6 December 1917, when the Parliament of the autonomous Grand Duchy approved a declaration of independence in the chaos that followed the Russian Revolution. The Bolshevik government in Petrograd recognised Finnish sovereignty on 31 December 1917. The new state had behind it a long history: six centuries as the eastern part of the Kingdom of Sweden, and more than a century (1809-1917) as an autonomous Grand Duchy under the Russian tsar, who held the title of Grand Duke of Finland. Independence was almost immediately tested by a brutal civil war in 1918 between socialist Reds and conservative Whites, and over the following decades by territorial disputes with Sweden over the Aland Islands and with the Soviet Union over Karelia.

Finland's history is shaped by its position on the seam between the Western and Eastern worlds. Settled after the last Ice Age by Finnic-speaking peoples, with the Sami in the north, the land was gradually drawn into Latin Christendom and incorporated into the Kingdom of Sweden, of which it formed the eastern half for some six centuries. In 1809 Sweden lost Finland to Russia, and for over a century Finland was an autonomous Grand Duchy under the tsar, who reigned as Grand Duke; the era saw Helsinki made the capital, a national awakening fed by the Kalevala epic, and, at its close, a programme of Russification that hardened the desire for self-rule. Finland declared independence on 6 December 1917 and at once endured a bitter civil war between Reds and Whites. The new republic faced repeated trials over its borders: the Aland Islands dispute, settled by the League of Nations in 1921; the Winter War of 1939-40 and the Continuation War of 1941-44 against the Soviet Union, which cost it Karelia, Petsamo and the resettlement of some 400,000 evacuees; and a long Cold War of neutrality. Finland joined the European Union in 1995 and, abandoning generations of non-alignment after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, became a member of NATO on 4 April 2023.

## The Finnic Peoples and Six Centuries as Part of Sweden — c. 8800 BCE – 1809

Settled by Finnic peoples and the Sami after the Ice Age, Finland was drawn into Latin Christendom and became, for six centuries, the eastern half of the Kingdom of Sweden.

- c. 8800 BCE: **First settlement of Finland** — People moved into Finland as the Ice Age glaciers retreated, settling along the coasts and waterways; the land was later inhabited by Finnic-speaking peoples and, in the north, the Sami. [infofinland-history, sami-britannica]
- 13th century: **Turku and the Christianization of Finland** — Carried by the Swedish crusades, Latin Christianity spread through Finland; Turku (Abo), founded in the thirteenth century, became the principal town and episcopal seat of the eastern realm. [finland-swedish-rule-britannica, mainoutlines-finland]
- 1323: **Treaty of Noteborg** — The peace of Noteborg fixed an approximate frontier between the Swedish and Russian spheres in Karelia, assigning Finland to Sweden's orbit and splitting the Karelians between Catholic west and Orthodox east. [mainoutlines-finland, finland-swedish-rule-britannica]
- 1548: **Mikael Agricola and written Finnish** — The Reformation-era bishop Mikael Agricola created written Finnish, translating the New Testament in 1548 and laying the foundation of Finnish literary culture. [mainoutlines-finland]
- 1808–1809: **The Finnish War and the end of Swedish rule** — Russia conquered Finland from Sweden in the war of 1808-09, ending some six centuries during which Finland had formed the eastern half of the Kingdom of Sweden. [finland-swedish-rule-britannica, history-of-finland-britannica]

Figures:

- Mikael Agricola (c. 1510 – 1557) — Bishop and father of written Finnish. A reformer and scholar, Agricola gave the Finnish language its written form, translating the New Testament into Finnish in 1548 and providing the foundation on which a later national literature would be built.
- The Sami — Indigenous people of the north. A Finno-Ugric indigenous people of the European Arctic, the Sami long inhabited much of Finland before the spread of Finnish settlement pushed their communities northward; they remain the only recognised indigenous people of the European Union.

## The Grand Duchy of Finland under the Russian Empire — 1809 – 1917

Joined to Russia as an autonomous Grand Duchy under the tsar, Finland kept its own laws, gained Helsinki as its capital, found a national voice in the Kalevala, and was hardened by late-century Russification.

- 1809: **Finland becomes an autonomous Grand Duchy** — By the Treaty of Fredrikshamn Sweden ceded Finland to Russia; it became the autonomous Grand Duchy of Finland, joined to the empire through the tsar, who reigned as Grand Duke, and kept its own laws and religion. [history-of-finland-britannica, infofinland-history]
- 1812: **Helsinki made the capital** — The capital was moved from Turku to Helsinki, nearer to Saint Petersburg, and the town was rebuilt as a neoclassical capital of the Grand Duchy. [infofinland-history, mainoutlines-finland]
- 1835: **Elias Lonnrot publishes the Kalevala** — Lonnrot published the Kalevala, a national epic assembled from oral folk poetry collected in Karelia; it strengthened the Finnish language and fed the national awakening that would culminate in independence. [lonnrot-britannica, kalevala-finland]
- 1899: **Russification and Finlandia** — Nicholas II's February Manifesto curtailed Finnish autonomy, beginning a campaign of Russification; resistance found voice in Jean Sibelius's tone poem Finlandia, composed that same year. [history-of-finland-britannica, finlandia-finland]
- 1906: **A modern parliament and votes for women** — Finland adopted a unicameral parliament elected by universal suffrage, becoming the first country in Europe to grant women the right to vote and to stand for election. [history-of-finland-britannica]

Figures:

- Elias Lonnrot (1802 – 1884) — Folklorist and creator of the Kalevala. A country physician and philologist, Lonnrot travelled through Karelia gathering oral folk poetry, which he wove into the Kalevala (1835, enlarged 1849). The epic gave Finns a national mythology and raised the standing of their language.
- Jean Sibelius (1865 – 1957) — Composer. Finland's foremost composer, Sibelius poured national feeling into his music. His tone poem Finlandia (1899), written amid the Russification crisis, became an unofficial anthem of Finnish resistance and identity.

## Independence and the Finnish Civil War — 1917 – 1920

In the wreckage of the Russian Revolution Finland declared independence on 6 December 1917, only to be torn apart within weeks by a savage civil war between Reds and Whites.

- 6 Dec 1917: **Declaration of independence** — The Parliament of Finland approved a declaration of independence amid the turmoil of the Russian Revolution, ending more than a century as an autonomous Grand Duchy of the Russian Empire. [history-of-finland-britannica, mainoutlines-finland]
- 31 Dec 1917: **Soviet Russia recognises Finland** — Lenin's Bolshevik government formally recognised Finnish independence on the last day of 1917, calculating that it could not retain the periphery of the former empire. [history-of-finland-britannica, infofinland-history]
- Jan–May 1918: **The Finnish Civil War** — From late January to 15 May 1918 Finland was convulsed by civil war between socialist Reds and conservative Whites under Mannerheim; the Whites won, but some 36,600 died, many in executions and prison camps. [civilwar-finland, history-of-finland-britannica]
- 1919: **Republican constitution** — Finland adopted a republican constitution in 1919, establishing a parliamentary republic with a strong presidency rather than the monarchy that conservatives had briefly favoured after the civil war. [history-of-finland-britannica]

Figures:

- P. E. Svinhufvud (1861 – 1944) — Head of the senate and statesman. Chairman of the senate that proclaimed Finnish independence in December 1917 and secured its recognition by Soviet Russia, Svinhufvud led the Whites' government during the civil war and later served as president of the republic.
- Carl Gustaf Mannerheim (1867 – 1951) — Military commander and statesman. A former general of the Russian imperial army, Mannerheim commanded the White forces in the 1918 civil war and would later lead Finland's defence in the wars against the Soviet Union, becoming the country's most revered soldier-statesman and, briefly, its president.

## The Aland Islands Dispute and the Interwar Republic — 1920 – 1939

The young republic's first great test of its borders was settled not by war but by the League of Nations, whose 1921 decision on the Aland Islands became a landmark of peaceful conflict resolution.

- 1920: **Aland Autonomy Act** — Finland granted the Swedish-speaking Aland Islands wide-ranging cultural and political autonomy, anticipating the conditions the League of Nations would attach to its sovereignty the following year. [aland-um, aland-finland]
- 24 Jun 1921: **League of Nations awards Aland to Finland** — The Council of the League of Nations awarded sovereignty over the Aland Islands to Finland, while obliging it to guarantee the islanders' Swedish language, culture and self-government, a landmark of peaceful conflict resolution. [aland-um, aland-finland, aland-un-legal]
- Oct 1921: **Convention on the demilitarisation of Aland** — An international convention confirmed the demilitarisation of Aland dating from the 1856 Treaty of Paris and added its neutralisation, keeping the islands outside any theatre of war. [aland-nordics, aland-um]
- 1932: **The far-right coup attempt fails** — An attempted uprising by the extreme-right Lapua movement collapsed within days when the majority of Finns rejected it, and the parliamentary republic held firm through the interwar years. [civilwar-finland]

Figures:

- K. J. Stahlberg (1865 – 1952) — First President of Finland. A jurist and the principal author of the 1919 constitution, Stahlberg became Finland's first president and pursued reconciliation after the civil war, pardoning many Red prisoners and helping to anchor the new republic as a parliamentary democracy.
- The people of Aland — Swedish-speaking islanders. The overwhelmingly Swedish-speaking inhabitants of the Aland Islands sought union with Sweden after 1917. The League of Nations left them under Finnish sovereignty but secured for them lasting guarantees of autonomy and their Swedish language.

## The Winter War, the Continuation War and the Loss of Karelia — 1939 – 1947

Twice invaded by the Soviet Union, Finland fought ferociously to survive, kept its independence, but lost Karelia and Petsamo and had to resettle some 400,000 evacuees.

- 30 Nov 1939: **The Winter War begins** — The Soviet Union invaded Finland on 30 November 1939 after Finland refused to cede territory near Leningrad; the outnumbered Finns mounted a celebrated defence through the winter of 1939-40. [russofinnish-britannica, infofinland-history]
- Mar 1940: **Moscow Peace Treaty cedes Karelia** — The Winter War ended in March 1940 with Finland keeping its independence but ceding much of Karelia, including Viipuri; the inhabitants of the lost lands were evacuated into the rest of Finland. [russofinnish-britannica, finland-wwii-britannica]
- 1941–1944: **The Continuation War** — Allied with Germany after its 1941 invasion of the USSR, Finland fought the Continuation War to recover Karelia, but a Soviet offensive in 1944 forced it to seek an armistice. [finland-wwii-britannica, russofinnish-britannica]
- 19 Sep 1944: **Moscow Armistice and cession of Petsamo** — The Moscow Armistice ended the Continuation War, restoring the 1940 border, returning Petsamo to the Soviet Union, leasing Porkkala as a naval base, and imposing reparations. [finland-wwii-britannica, frus-finland-treaty]
- 1947: **Paris Peace Treaty and the Karelian evacuees** — The 1947 Paris Peace Treaty confirmed the cession of Karelia and Petsamo and reparations of 300 million dollars; some 400,000 Karelian evacuees were resettled within Finland's new borders. [frus-finland-treaty, finland-wwii-britannica]

Figures:

- Carl Gustaf Mannerheim (1867 – 1951) — Commander-in-chief and President. Marshal of Finland, Mannerheim led the defence in the Winter and Continuation Wars and became president in 1944, steering Finland out of the war and into an armistice that preserved its independence.
- The Karelian evacuees — Displaced population. Around 400,000 people, more than a tenth of Finland's population, were evacuated from the parts of Karelia and other regions ceded to the Soviet Union and resettled across Finland under a state-organised relocation, leaving a lasting mark on Finnish society.

## Neutrality, the European Union and NATO — 1947 – present

From the careful neutrality of the Cold War to membership of the European Union and, after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, of NATO, Finland transformed its place in the world.

- 1948: **Treaty with the Soviet Union and neutrality** — A 1948 treaty of friendship with the Soviet Union framed Finland's Cold War policy of strict neutrality and non-alignment, later known as 'Finlandization', under which it preserved its democracy and independence. [history-of-finland-britannica, infofinland-history]
- 1975: **The Helsinki Final Act** — Under President Kekkonen, neutral Finland hosted the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, whose Helsinki Final Act of 1975 became a landmark of Cold War detente. [history-of-finland-britannica]
- 1 Jan 1995: **Finland joins the European Union** — After a 1994 referendum in which about 57 per cent voted yes, Finland joined the European Union on 1 January 1995 with Sweden and Austria, and later adopted the euro. [mainoutlines-finland, infofinland-history]
- May 2022: **Finland applies to join NATO** — Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 reversed Finnish opinion on military alignment; together with Sweden, Finland applied to join NATO, abandoning its long-standing non-alignment. [nato-finland, infofinland-history]
- 4 Apr 2023: **Finland becomes NATO's 31st member** — Finland deposited its instrument of accession and joined NATO as its 31st member on 4 April 2023, ending generations of neutrality and more than doubling the Alliance's land border with Russia. [nato-finland]

Figures:

- Urho Kekkonen (1900 – 1986) — President of Finland. President from 1956 to 1982, Kekkonen embodied Finland's Cold War policy of neutrality, managing relations with the Soviet Union while securing Finland's room for manoeuvre and hosting the 1975 Helsinki conference.
- Sauli Niinisto (born 1948) — President of Finland. President from 2012 to 2024, Niinisto led Finland through its historic decision to abandon non-alignment and join NATO in 2023 in response to Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
