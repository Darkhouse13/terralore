# The Chronicle of Canada: From Indigenous homelands and New France to a confederated dominion that grew into an autonomous, multicultural nation.

Founding: Confederation — the Dominion of Canada — 1 July 1867. Canada became a self-governing federation on 1 July 1867, when the British North America Act (passed by the United Kingdom Parliament and given royal assent on 29 March 1867) united the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick into one Dominion under the name of Canada. This founding rested on far deeper foundations: Indigenous peoples had lived across the land for millennia, and the French had built New France around the St. Lawrence from 1608 before the British conquest of 1763. Confederation created a country still tied to Britain; full autonomy came in stages. The Statute of Westminster (1931) granted Canada legislative independence, and the patriation of the constitution in 1982 — through the Constitution Act, 1982, and the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms — gave Canada the power to amend its own constitution without recourse to the British Parliament, completing its journey to full sovereignty.

Canada's history begins with Indigenous peoples — First Nations, Inuit, and later the Métis — whose ancestors had occupied the land for thousands of years before European contact. Norse mariners reached Newfoundland around the year 1000, but sustained European settlement came with the French, who built New France around the St. Lawrence fur trade from 1608. Britain conquered New France in the Seven Years' War, confirmed by the Treaty of Paris in 1763, and governed a string of British North American colonies until they federated. On 1 July 1867 the British North America Act created the Dominion of Canada, a self-governing federation that swiftly expanded across the continent — an expansion built on railways, immigration, and the dispossession of Indigenous nations through treaties, reserves, and residential schools. Two world wars deepened Canadian autonomy, formalised by the 1931 Statute of Westminster, and the 1982 patriation of the constitution made Canada fully sovereign. Modern Canada is a bilingual, officially multicultural country still reckoning with the legacy of colonialism and the work of reconciliation.

## Indigenous peoples and First Nations before contact — before 1497

For thousands of years before Europeans arrived, the land that became Canada was home to diverse Indigenous nations with their own languages, governments, trade networks, and ways of life.

- c. 13,000 BCE or earlier: **Peopling of the continent** — The ancestors of First Nations were established across the Americas by the end of the last Ice Age, developing diverse societies, languages, and economies over thousands of years before European contact. [cdn-enc-indigenous, brit-canada]
- c. 1000: **Norse arrival at L'Anse aux Meadows** — Norse explorers established a short-lived base at L'Anse aux Meadows on the northern tip of Newfoundland around the year 1000 — the first known European presence in the Americas, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [unesco-lanse, brit-lanse]
- 1497: **John Cabot reaches the Atlantic coast** — Sailing for England, John Cabot reached the coast of present-day Atlantic Canada in 1497, beginning the sustained European interest in the region's fisheries and lands that would follow. [brit-canada, cdn-enc-indigenous]

Figures:

- The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) — Confederacy of nations. A long-standing alliance of nations of the eastern woodlands, governed by the Great Law of Peace, whose settled agriculture, longhouse villages, and council-based governance shaped the politics of the northeast for centuries before and after contact.

## New France and the fur trade — 1534 – 1763

French explorers and traders built a colony along the St. Lawrence anchored in the fur trade and in alliances with Indigenous nations, founding Quebec in 1608 and a society that endures in French Canada today.

- 1534: **Jacques Cartier explores the St. Lawrence** — Jacques Cartier explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence and claimed the region for France, opening the way for later French interest in the fur trade and settlement. [brit-canada, cdn-enc-champlain]
- 1608: **Champlain founds Quebec** — Samuel de Champlain established Quebec as a fortified trading post on the St. Lawrence, founding the colony of New France and anchoring the French fur trade in alliance with Indigenous nations. [cdn-enc-champlain, vmnf-champlain]
- 1642: **Founding of Montreal** — French missionaries and settlers founded Ville-Marie, the future Montreal, as a mission and trading settlement that became a hub of the fur trade and a gateway to the interior. [brit-canada]
- 13 September 1759: **Battle of the Plains of Abraham** — British forces defeated the French outside Quebec City in the decisive Battle of the Plains of Abraham, the turning point of the Seven Years' War in North America that led to the fall of New France. [brit-canada, cdn-enc-quebec-act]

Figures:

- Samuel de Champlain (c. 1567–1635) — Explorer and founder of New France. French navigator and cartographer who founded Quebec in 1608 and built the alliances and trade that sustained the early colony, earning the title Father of New France.

## British conquest and colonial British North America — 1763 – 1867

Britain's conquest of New France brought a French Catholic population under British rule, an influx of Loyalists after the American Revolution, rebellion and reform, and the gradual move toward responsible self-government.

- 1763: **Treaty of Paris cedes New France to Britain** — The Treaty of Paris ended the Seven Years' War, with France ceding its North American mainland territories to Britain and bringing the French Catholic population of Canada under British rule. [brit-bna-act, cdn-enc-quebec-act]
- 1774: **Quebec Act** — The Quebec Act restored French civil law, guaranteed freedom of Catholic worship, and enlarged Quebec's boundaries, securing the loyalty of French Canadians while inflaming the Thirteen Colonies. [cdn-enc-quebec-act, ukparl-quebec-act]
- 1791: **Constitutional Act divides the Canadas** — The Constitutional Act split Quebec into Upper Canada and Lower Canada, creating separate English- and French-majority colonies each with an elected assembly. [brit-canada]
- 1837–1838: **Rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada** — Armed uprisings against oligarchic colonial rule in both Canadas were suppressed but led to Lord Durham's report and, ultimately, to responsible government. [brit-canada]

Figures:

- Lord Durham (John George Lambton) (1792–1840) — Governor General and reformer. British politician whose 1839 Report on the Affairs of British North America recommended uniting the Canadas and granting responsible government, shaping the path toward self-rule.

## Confederation and nation-building across the continent — 1867 – 1914

The British North America Act created the Dominion of Canada in 1867, a federation that swiftly expanded to the Pacific — bound together by a transcontinental railway and built on the dispossession of Indigenous nations.

- 1 July 1867: **Confederation — the Dominion of Canada** — The British North America Act united Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick into the self-governing Dominion of Canada, with Sir John A. Macdonald as first prime minister. [brit-bna-act, cdn-enc-confederation, cmh-bna-act]
- 1869–1870: **Red River Resistance and Manitoba** — Métis led by Louis Riel resisted the transfer of Rupert's Land to Canada, securing rights that shaped the creation of the province of Manitoba in 1870. [brit-canada, cdn-enc-confederation]
- 1876: **The Indian Act** — The Indian Act consolidated federal control over the lives, lands, and governance of First Nations peoples, becoming a central instrument of assimilation and dispossession that remains in force in amended form. [cdn-enc-indigenous, nctr-reports]
- 7 November 1885: **Canadian Pacific Railway completed** — The driving of the last spike at Craigellachie completed the transcontinental Canadian Pacific Railway, binding the new nation together and fulfilling a promise made to British Columbia at Confederation. [brit-canada, cdn-enc-confederation]
- 1885: **North-West Resistance and the execution of Riel** — Métis and First Nations grievances on the prairies erupted in the North-West Resistance; its defeat and the hanging of Louis Riel deepened the divide between French and English Canada. [brit-canada]

Figures:

- Sir John A. Macdonald (1815–1891) — First Prime Minister of Canada. Leading architect of Confederation and Canada's first prime minister, who championed the transcontinental railway and the National Policy — and whose government built the residential school system and policies of Indigenous assimilation.
- Louis Riel (1844–1885) — Métis leader. Founder of Manitoba and leader of Métis resistance at Red River and in the North-West; executed for treason in 1885, he is now widely honoured as a defender of Métis rights and a Father of Confederation for Manitoba.

## The World Wars and growing autonomy — 1914 – 1945

Service and sacrifice in two world wars forged a stronger sense of nationhood and won Canada a distinct voice in the world — autonomy formally recognised by the Statute of Westminster in 1931.

- 1914–1918: **Canada in the First World War** — Canada fought in the First World War as part of the British Empire but as a distinct national force; the costly victory at Vimy Ridge in 1917 became a symbol of emerging nationhood. [vac-vimy, brit-canada]
- 1917: **Conscription Crisis** — The imposition of compulsory military service deeply divided English and French Canada, becoming one of the most divisive episodes in Canadian political history. [brit-canada]
- 1931: **Statute of Westminster** — The Statute of Westminster granted Canada legislative independence from the British Parliament, a landmark in its evolution toward full sovereignty. [cdn-enc-westminster, brit-canada]
- 1939–1945: **Canada in the Second World War** — Canada entered the Second World War by its own parliamentary decision and mobilised on a vast scale, contributing major forces to the Allied victory and emerging more industrialised and autonomous. [brit-canada]

Figures:

- Sir Robert Borden (1854–1937) — Prime Minister during the First World War. Led Canada through the First World War, introduced conscription in 1917, and pressed for Canada's separate recognition at the peace table and in the League of Nations.

## Modern Canada — multiculturalism, patriation, and reconciliation — 1945 – present

Post-war Canada built a welfare state, embraced official bilingualism and multiculturalism, patriated its constitution in 1982, and began a long reckoning with the harms of colonialism and the residential school system.

- 1965: **A new national flag and a maturing identity** — Canada adopted the red-and-white maple leaf flag in 1965, a symbol of a distinct national identity as the post-war country built a welfare state and universal health care. [brit-canada]
- 8 October 1971: **Official multiculturalism policy** — Canada became the first country to adopt multiculturalism as official government policy, within a bilingual framework, recognising the contributions of diverse cultural communities. [pier21-multiculturalism, cdn-enc-multiculturalism]
- 17 April 1982: **Patriation of the Constitution** — The Constitution Act, 1982, patriated Canada's constitution, entrenched the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, recognised Aboriginal rights, and ended the need for British legislation — completing Canada's path to full sovereignty. [cdn-enc-constitution-1982, brit-canada]
- 30 October 1995: **Quebec sovereignty referendum** — Quebec voters narrowly rejected sovereignty in the 1995 referendum, the closest of two votes on independence, underlining the enduring tension over the place of Quebec within Canada. [brit-canada]
- 2015: **Truth and Reconciliation Commission reports** — After years of survivor testimony, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented the harms of the residential school system, described it as cultural genocide, and issued 94 Calls to Action for reconciliation. [nctr-reports, cmhr-residential-schools]

Figures:

- Pierre Elliott Trudeau (1919–2000) — Prime Minister and architect of patriation. Introduced official multiculturalism in 1971 and championed the 1982 patriation of the constitution and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, shaping the legal foundations of modern Canada.
