Founding: Independence from the Soviet Union — 31 August 1991. Modern Kyrgyzstan dates its statehood to 31 August 1991, when the Supreme Soviet of the republic declared independence as the Soviet Union disintegrated in the wake of the failed August coup in Moscow. Askar Akayev, a physicist who had been chosen head of the republic in 1990, became the first president of independent Kyrgyzstan, and the Soviet Union was formally dissolved at the end of December 1991. The deeper roots of Kyrgyz identity reach back more than a thousand years to the Yenisei Kyrgyz of the steppe and to the epic of Manas, which tells of the unification of scattered tribes into one people. Independence in 1991 gave a sovereign state to a nation that had spent the previous century under the rule of the Khanate of Kokand, the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, and whose homeland in the Tian Shan it now governs from Bishkek.

Kyrgyzstan's story begins with the Yenisei Kyrgyz of the Siberian steppe, whose khaganate destroyed the Uyghur capital in 840, and with the great oral epic of Manas that carried the people's memory across centuries. Along the Silk Road through the Tian Shan rose Karakhanid Balasagun, the sacred mountain of Sulaiman-Too above Osh, and the caravan halts of Lake Issyk-Kul, while Mongol conquest and later pressures drew the Kyrgyz tribes into the Tian Shan that became their homeland. Absorbed by the Khanate of Kokand and then by the Russian Empire, the Kyrgyz rose in the catastrophic 1916 revolt before being organized as the Kara-Kyrgyz Autonomous Oblast and the Kirghiz SSR of the Soviet Union. Kyrgyzstan declared independence on 31 August 1991, and since then has lived a turbulent, competitive politics — the 2005 Tulip Revolution, the 2010 uprising and the ethnic violence in the south, and a return to a presidential republic in 2021.

## The Yenisei Kyrgyz and the Epic of Manas — c. 200 BCE – 1200 CE

An ancient Turkic people of the upper Yenisei built a steppe power that briefly dominated Mongolia, and carried into later centuries the vast oral epic of Manas.

- 840: **The Yenisei Kyrgyz destroy the Uyghur Khaganate** — The Yenisei Kyrgyz overthrew the Uyghur Khaganate and sacked its capital Ordu-Baliq, briefly making the Kyrgyz the dominant power of the eastern steppe and driving the Uyghurs out of Mongolia. [kyrgyz-people-britannica, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- c. 9th–12th c.: **Transmission of the epic of Manas** — The Kyrgyz preserved the vast oral epic of Manas, recited by bards (manaschi), telling of the legendary hero who united the Kyrgyz tribes; it remains the cornerstone of Kyrgyz cultural memory. [manas-noble-britannica, manas-unesco-ich]
- 2013: **Manas trilogy recognized by UNESCO** — UNESCO inscribed the Kyrgyz epic trilogy of Manas, Semetey and Seytek on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognizing one of the world's longest oral epics. [manas-unesco-ich]

Figures:

- Manas — Legendary Kyrgyz hero. The central hero of the Kyrgyz national epic, Manas is celebrated as the warrior who united the scattered Kyrgyz tribes into a single people; the forty rays of the sun on the national flag are said to represent the forty tribes he brought together.

## The Silk Road, the Tian Shan and the Mongol Age — c. 900 – 1700

Caravan cities and sacred mountains flourished along the Silk Road through the Tian Shan, while Mongol conquest and later upheavals drew the Kyrgyz into the mountains that became their home.

- 10th–12th c.: **Karakhanid Balasagun and the Burana tower** — Under the Karakhanid dynasty, the first Turkic Muslim power in Central Asia, Balasagun in the Chüy valley flourished as a capital; its minaret, the Burana tower, survives as the best-known monument of northern Kyrgyzstan. [burana-unesco-silkroad, silkroad-corridor-unesco]
- early 13th c.: **Mongol conquest of Central Asia** — The empire of Genghis Khan overran Central Asia, drawing the Kyrgyz lands into the Mongol world and, after the empire's division, into the Chagatai realm and its successor states. [kyrgyzstan-history-britannica, loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan]
- by the 16th–17th c.: **Kyrgyz consolidate in the Tian Shan** — Through the upheavals of the post-Mongol centuries the Kyrgyz tribes settled in the Tian Shan, living as mountain nomads organized in tribes and clans, the homeland they hold to this day. [kyrgyz-people-britannica, loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan]
- 2009: **Sulaiman-Too inscribed by UNESCO** — The sacred mountain of Sulaiman-Too above Osh, with its petroglyphs, places of worship and reconstructed 16th-century mosques, became Kyrgyzstan's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized for its blend of pre-Islamic and Islamic belief. [sulaiman-too-unesco]

## The Khanate of Kokand and the Russian Conquest — 1700 – 1916

The Kyrgyz fell under the Khanate of Kokand and then under the Russian Empire, whose colonial settlement provoked the catastrophic revolt of 1916.

- early 19th c.: **Kyrgyz under the Khanate of Kokand** — The Khanate of Kokand extended its rule over the Kyrgyz tribes, building the fortress of Pishpek (later Bishkek) to control the Chüy valley and recruiting Kyrgyz fighters, though its rule was widely resisted. [kokand-britannica, loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan]
- 1876: **Russia annexes Kokand; Kyrgyzstan under imperial rule** — After suppressing an uprising, the Russian Empire abolished the Khanate of Kokand and annexed its lands as the Fergana Province, bringing the Kyrgyz country under direct Russian colonial rule. [kokand-britannica, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- 1916: **The 1916 revolt (Urkun)** — A decree conscripting Central Asians for wartime labour triggered a revolt across Russian Turkestan; in the Kyrgyz lands it was suppressed with great violence, and tens of thousands fled toward China, with heavy loss of life. Casualty figures remain estimates. [urkun-rferl, loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan]

## Soviet Kirghizia — 1917 – 1991

After the Russian Revolution the Kyrgyz were given their own Soviet territory, from an autonomous oblast to a full union republic, transformed by collectivization, industry and a literate national culture.

- 1924: **Kara-Kyrgyz Autonomous Oblast created** — In the Soviet national-territorial delimitation of Central Asia, the Kyrgyz lands were organized as the Kara-Kyrgyz Autonomous Oblast within the Russian SFSR, giving the Kyrgyz their own named territory for the first time. [loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- 5 Dec 1936: **The Kirghiz SSR becomes a union republic** — Kyrgyzstan was elevated to the Kirghiz Soviet Socialist Republic, a full constituent republic of the Soviet Union, with its capital at Frunze (formerly Pishpek, today Bishkek). [loc-country-study-kyrgyzstan, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- 1963: **Chingiz Aitmatov wins the Lenin Prize** — The Kyrgyz writer Chingiz Aitmatov was awarded the Lenin Prize for 'Tales of the Mountains and Steppes', bringing Kyrgyz literature to a worldwide audience and becoming the republic's most celebrated cultural figure. [aitmatov-britannica]
- 1990: **Akayev chosen to lead the republic** — Amid Gorbachev's reforms and rising ethnic tension in the south, the physicist Askar Akayev was elected head of the Kirghiz SSR, the leader who would guide the republic to independence. [akayev-britannica, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Chingiz Aitmatov (1928 – 2008) — Kyrgyz writer and diplomat. Kyrgyzstan's most celebrated author, who wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian. Works such as Jamila and The Day Lasts More Than a Hundred Years won him the Lenin Prize (1963) and worldwide renown; he later served as a diplomat for the Soviet Union and independent Kyrgyzstan.

## Independence and the Akayev Years — 1991 – 2005

Kyrgyzstan declared independence on 31 August 1991 and, under President Askar Akayev, won a reputation as Central Asia's most open society before sliding toward corruption and revolt.

- 31 Aug 1991: **Kyrgyzstan declares independence** — As the Soviet Union collapsed, the Supreme Soviet of the republic declared Kyrgyzstan's independence; Askar Akayev became the first president of the sovereign state, with its capital at Bishkek. [kyrgyzstan-history-britannica, akayev-britannica]
- 25 Dec 1991: **U.S. recognition and diplomatic relations** — The United States established diplomatic relations with independent Kyrgyzstan on 25 December 1991, opening its embassy in Bishkek in February 1992 as the Soviet Union was dissolved. [state-historian-kyrgyzstan]
- Mar 2005: **The Tulip Revolution** — Allegations of fraud in the parliamentary elections and of pervasive corruption sparked mass demonstrations that forced President Akayev to flee the country, in the first of Kyrgyzstan's post-Soviet revolutions. [tulip-revolution-britannica, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Askar Akayev (born 1944) — First President of Kyrgyzstan. A physicist who led the republic from 1990, Akayev became independent Kyrgyzstan's first president in 1991 and presided over its early reputation as Central Asia's most open society; accusations of corruption and electoral fraud led to his overthrow in the 2005 Tulip Revolution.

## A Turbulent Republic: Revolution, Violence and Competitive Politics — 2005 – present

Two more upheavals, a tragic outbreak of ethnic violence in the south, and a swing back to presidential rule have marked Kyrgyzstan's turbulent but uniquely competitive politics.

- Apr 2010: **The 2010 revolution ousts Bakiyev** — A violent uprising in Bishkek over corruption and rising prices forced President Kurmanbek Bakiyev to flee; an interim government under Roza Otunbayeva took power and prepared a new constitution. [otunbayeva-britannica, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- Jun 2010: **Ethnic violence in the south** — Clashes between ethnic Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in Osh and Jalal-Abad erupted into days of communal violence, killing several hundred people — figures are estimates and remain contested — and displacing hundreds of thousands. [hrw-kyrgyzstan-2010, kyrgyzstan-history-britannica]
- 2010: **Parliamentary republic and the first peaceful transfer** — A new constitution made Kyrgyzstan a parliamentary republic, a rarity in Central Asia; the 2011 election that followed Otunbayeva's interim presidency was the country's first peaceful handover of power. [otunbayeva-britannica]
- 2021: **Japarov and the return to presidential rule** — After 2020 protests over disputed elections, Sadyr Japarov rose from prison to the presidency, winning election in January 2021; constitutional referendums then restored a strong presidential system. [japarov-britannica, japarov-crs]

Figures:

- Roza Otunbayeva (born 1950) — Interim President of Kyrgyzstan. A former diplomat who led the interim government formed after the 2010 ouster of Kurmanbek Bakiyev, overseeing the transition to a parliamentary republic. She stepped down after the 2011 election, in Kyrgyzstan's first peaceful transfer of power.
- Sadyr Japarov (born 1968) — President of Kyrgyzstan. A nationalist politician freed from prison during the 2020 protests, Japarov rose rapidly to become president in January 2021 and oversaw constitutional changes restoring a strong presidential system.
