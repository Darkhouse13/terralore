# The Chronicle of Armenia: From the kingdom of Urartu and the world's first Christian state through Ani and the genocide to a republic reborn in 1991.

Founding: Independence from the Soviet Union — 21 September 1991. Modern Armenia dates its statehood to 21 September 1991, when an overwhelming majority of voters approved independence in a referendum and the Supreme Council declared the country's secession from the dissolving Soviet Union; the United States recognised Armenia's independence on 25 December 1991. But Armenian statehood reaches back millennia. The Iron Age kingdom of Urartu flourished in the Armenian highlands in the 9th and 8th centuries BCE, succeeded by the Armenians by the 6th century BCE; under Tigranes II 'the Great' (95–55 BCE) Armenia became briefly the strongest state in the Roman East. Around 301 CE Armenia became the first nation to adopt Christianity as its state religion, and the Armenian alphabet devised by Mesrop Mashtots around 405 anchored a literary culture that survived the loss of political independence for most of the next 1,500 years. A First Republic existed from 1918 to 1920 before Sovietisation, making 1991 the restoration of an interrupted statehood.

Armenia occupies one of the oldest continuously inhabited corners of the world, the Armenian highlands south of the Caucasus, where the Iron Age kingdom of Urartu and later an Armenian kingdom that briefly stretched from the Caspian to the Mediterranean under Tigranes the Great took shape. Around 301 CE, under King Tiridates III and Gregory the Illuminator, Armenia became the first state to adopt Christianity as its religion, and a century later Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet — twin pillars of a durable national identity preserved across centuries of foreign rule. Medieval Bagratid Armenia centred on the city of Ani, and a separate Armenian kingdom flourished in Cilicia, while monasteries such as Haghpat, Sanahin and Geghard now stand as UNESCO World Heritage Sites. After partition among Persian, Ottoman and Russian empires, Armenians endured the genocide of 1915, a short-lived First Republic (1918), Soviet rule, and finally independence on 21 September 1991 — followed by a long and unresolved conflict with Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh.

## Urartu and the Armenian Kingdom — c. 860 BCE – 428 CE

From the Iron Age kingdom of Urartu to the empire of Tigranes the Great, the Armenian highlands became a centre of power between Rome and Persia.

- 782 BCE: **Founding of Erebuni** — The Urartian king Argishti I founded the fortress of Erebuni on the site of present-day Yerevan, one of the oldest continuously inhabited city sites in the world. [britannica-urartu, britannica-yerevan]
- 8th century BCE: **Urartu at its height** — In the 9th and 8th centuries BCE the kingdom of Urartu, centred on Lake Van, became a major power of the Middle East before being succeeded in the region by the Armenians. [britannica-urartu]
- 95–55 BCE: **Reign of Tigranes the Great** — Tigranes II expanded Armenia until it briefly stretched from the Caspian toward the Mediterranean, taking the title 'king of kings' and making Armenia the strongest state in the Roman East before Roman armies forced his retreat. [britannica-tigranes, whe-tigranes]
- 428 CE: **End of the Armenian monarchy** — The Sasanian Persians abolished the Arsacid kingdom of Armenia and ruled the territory through appointed officials, ending the ancient Armenian monarchy. [britannica-armenia-modern]

Figures:

- Tigranes II 'the Great' (c. 140 – c. 55 BCE) — King of Armenia (95–55 BCE). Under Tigranes the Armenian kingdom reached its greatest extent, briefly stretching from the Caspian toward the Mediterranean; he took the title 'king of kings' and founded the city of Tigranocerta.

## The First Christian Nation and the Alphabet — 301 CE – 885 CE

Armenia became the first state to adopt Christianity, and the invention of its alphabet around 405 forged a literary identity that outlasted political independence.

- c. 301 CE: **Armenia adopts Christianity** — King Tiridates III proclaimed Christianity the religion of his kingdom after his conversion by Gregory the Illuminator, making Armenia the first nation to adopt Christianity as its state religion. [whe-christianization, unesco-echmiatsin, britannica-tiridates]
- 301–303 CE: **Mother Cathedral of Echmiatsin** — The earliest of Armenia's great domed churches, the Mother Cathedral of Echmiatsin, was traditionally built by Tiridates III and Gregory the Illuminator; it remains the seat of the Armenian Apostolic Church. [unesco-echmiatsin]
- c. 405 CE: **Mesrop Mashtots creates the Armenian alphabet** — The scholar Mesrop Mashtots devised the 36-letter Armenian alphabet, enabling Scripture and literature in Armenian and launching a 5th-century golden age of writing and translation. [britannica-alphabet, whe-mashtots]

Figures:

- Tiridates III (reigned c. 298 – c. 330 CE) — King of Armenia; converted to Christianity c. 301. After his conversion by Gregory the Illuminator, Tiridates made Christianity the religion of his kingdom around 301, making Armenia the world's first officially Christian state.
- Mesrop Mashtots (c. 360/370 – c. 440 CE) — Monk, scholar and creator of the Armenian alphabet. Devised the Armenian alphabet around 405, allowing the Bible and liturgy to be written in Armenian and anchoring a literary culture that long outlived political independence.

## Bagratid Ani and Cilician Armenia — 885 – 1375

A revived kingdom flowered at Ani, the 'city of a thousand churches', while a second Armenian kingdom rose far to the south in Cilicia.

- 885: **Bagratid kingdom restored** — Ashot I of the Bagratuni dynasty was recognised as king of Armenia by both the caliph and the Byzantine emperor, reviving an independent Armenian monarchy. [britannica-bagratid, britannica-ani]
- c. 961: **Ani becomes the Bagratid capital** — King Ashot III moved the capital to Ani, which flourished as a major medieval city known as the 'city of a thousand churches' for its cathedral and monastic architecture. [britannica-ani, unesco-haghpat]
- 6 January 1198: **Kingdom of Cilician Armenia** — Leo II was crowned king Leo I, raising the Armenian principality in Cilicia to a kingdom that, allied with the Crusaders, lasted until its conquest by the Mamluks in 1375. [britannica-little-armenia]
- 1375: **Fall of Cilician Armenia** — The Mamluks of Egypt conquered the Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia, extinguishing the last independent medieval Armenian state. [britannica-little-armenia]

Figures:

- Ashot I the Great (died 890) — First Bagratid king of Armenia (r. 885–890). Founder of the Bagratid kingdom, recognised as king by both the Abbasid caliph and the Byzantine emperor, restoring Armenian statehood after centuries of foreign rule.

## Empires, the Genocide and the First Republic — 1375 – 1920

Partitioned among Persia, the Ottomans and Russia, the Armenians of the empire suffered the genocide of 1915 — and then, briefly, won an independent republic.

- 1828: **Russia annexes eastern Armenia** — Following war with Persia, Russia annexed the eastern (Persian) Armenian lands, dividing the Armenian homeland between the Russian and Ottoman empires. [britannica-armenia-modern]
- 1915–1916: **The Armenian Genocide** — From 24 April 1915 the Ottoman Young Turk government deported and massacred the empire's Armenian population in death marches toward the Syrian desert; widely recognised as the first genocide of the 20th century, with deaths estimated from roughly 600,000 to as many as 1.5 million. [ushmm-armenian, britannica-genocide]
- 28 May 1918: **First Republic of Armenia** — As the Russian Empire collapsed, Armenians of the Caucasus declared an independent Republic of Armenia with its capital at Yerevan, the first modern Armenian state. [britannica-armenia-modern]
- 2 December 1920: **Sovietisation of Armenia** — Squeezed between Turkish nationalist forces and the advancing Red Army, the First Republic fell and Armenia was Sovietised, ending its short-lived independence. [britannica-armenia-modern]

Figures:

- Gregory the Illuminator (c. 240 – c. 332 CE) — Patron saint and first head of the Armenian Church. Credited with converting King Tiridates III and establishing Christianity in Armenia around 301; his cathedral at Echmiatsin remains the centre of the Armenian Apostolic Church.

## Soviet Armenia — 1920 – 1991

For seven decades Armenia was a Soviet republic — industrialised, urbanised and rebuilt after disaster, yet always keeping its Church, language and memory of the genocide.

- 1936: **Armenian SSR established** — The Transcaucasian federation was dissolved and Armenia became a separate union republic of the U.S.S.R., the Armenian Soviet Socialist Republic. [britannica-armenia-modern]
- 1988: **Nagorno-Karabakh dispute erupts** — The regional legislature of Nagorno-Karabakh, an Armenian-majority enclave within Soviet Azerbaijan, voted to join Armenia, triggering a conflict that escalated into open war. [britannica-nagorno-karabakh]
- 7 December 1988: **Spitak earthquake** — A catastrophic earthquake struck northern Armenia around Spitak and Gyumri, killing tens of thousands and leaving hundreds of thousands homeless in the final Soviet years. [britannica-armenia-modern]

## Independent Armenia — 1991 – present

Independence in 1991 reopened Armenian statehood — and a long, unresolved conflict with Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh that reshaped the region into the 2020s.

- 21 September 1991: **Independence from the Soviet Union** — Armenians approved independence by referendum and the Supreme Council declared secession from the U.S.S.R.; the United States recognised Armenia on 25 December 1991. [history-state-armenia, britannica-armenia-modern]
- 1994: **First Nagorno-Karabakh War ceasefire** — After fighting that left roughly 30,000 dead (estimate) and hundreds of thousands displaced, a 1994 ceasefire left Armenian forces in de facto control of Nagorno-Karabakh and adjacent districts. [britannica-nagorno-karabakh]
- April–May 2018: **The Velvet Revolution** — A peaceful protest movement led by Nikol Pashinyan brought down the governing elite and installed a new reform government, with Pashinyan elected prime minister. [britannica-armenia-modern]
- Sept–Nov 2020: **Second Nagorno-Karabakh War** — In a 44-day war Azerbaijan retook much of the territory around Nagorno-Karabakh; a Russian-brokered ceasefire on 9 November 2020 ended the fighting, with heavy casualties on both sides (estimates). [britannica-nagorno-karabakh]
- September 2023: **Azerbaijani offensive and the Karabakh exodus** — An Azerbaijani offensive brought Nagorno-Karabakh fully under its control; within about a week well over 100,000 ethnic Armenians fled to Armenia, and the enclave's authorities were dissolved by 1 January 2024. [britannica-nagorno-karabakh]

Figures:

- Levon Ter-Petrosyan (born 1945) — First president of independent Armenia (1991–1998). Led Armenia to independence in 1991 and through the first years of statehood and the first Nagorno-Karabakh War before resigning in 1998.
