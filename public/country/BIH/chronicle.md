Founding: The Banate and Kingdom of Bosnia — c. 1180 / 1377. A distinct Bosnian polity emerges into recorded history around 1180 with Ban Kulin, whose charter of 1189 granting trading rights to the merchants of Dubrovnik (Ragusa) is one of the oldest state documents of the South Slavs. The medieval Banate grew under the Kotromanić dynasty, and in 1377 Tvrtko I was crowned king, raising Bosnia to a kingdom that for a brief period in the late fourteenth century was the most powerful state in the western Balkans, reaching south to the Dalmatian coast. The kingdom fell to the Ottomans in 1463, opening more than four centuries of Ottoman and then Austro-Hungarian rule. The modern state traces its sovereignty to the independence referendum of 1992 and the Dayton settlement of 1995, which preserved Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single country.

Bosnia and Herzegovina occupies the rugged heart of the western Balkans, and its history is the story of a borderland where empires, faiths and peoples have met for a thousand years. A medieval Banate emerged under Ban Kulin around 1180 and rose to a kingdom when Tvrtko I was crowned in 1377; its distinctive Bosnian Church and the monumental stećci tombstones survive as emblems of that age. The Ottoman conquest of 1463 brought centuries of Islamic rule, founding Sarajevo and Mostar and giving Bosnia a uniquely interwoven population of Muslims, Orthodox, Catholics and Jews. Austria-Hungary occupied the land after the Congress of Berlin in 1878 and annexed it in 1908; it was in Sarajevo, on 28 June 1914, that the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand lit the fuse of the First World War. Absorbed into Yugoslavia, Bosnia became a federal republic after the Second World War, then declared independence in 1992. The Bosnian War that followed brought the longest siege of a capital in modern history at Sarajevo, campaigns of ethnic cleansing, and the Srebrenica genocide of July 1995, judged genocide by both the ICTY and the International Court of Justice. The Dayton Agreement ended the war in late 1995, binding two entities into one state under international oversight; in 2022 Bosnia and Herzegovina was granted European Union candidate status.

## Medieval Bosnia: Banate, Kingdom and the Bosnian Church — c. 1180 – 1463

A mountain borderland between Catholic and Orthodox Europe coalesced into a banate and then a kingdom, with a church and a culture of carved tombstones all its own.

- 29 Aug 1189: **Charter of Ban Kulin** — Ban Kulin granted the merchants of Dubrovnik (Ragusa) freedom to trade across Bosnia; the document is one of the oldest state charters of the South Slavs and a landmark of early Bosnian statehood. [kulin-charter-reference, bih-history-britannica]
- 1322–1353: **Stjepan Kotromanić expands Bosnia** — Ban Stjepan Kotromanić of the Kotromanić dynasty enlarged Bosnia southward, incorporating the principality of Hum, the territory later known as Herzegovina. [kotromanic-britannica, bih-history-britannica]
- 1377: **Coronation of Tvrtko I** — Tvrtko I was crowned king, raising Bosnia from a banate to a kingdom; under him it reached the Dalmatian coast and became, briefly, the most powerful state in the western Balkans. [kotromanic-britannica, bih-history-britannica]
- 12th–16th c.: **The stećci tombstones** — Across medieval Bosnia, communities raised monumental carved limestone tombstones known as stećci; sites in Bosnia and neighbouring lands were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in 2016. [stecci-unesco-reference]
- 1463: **Ottoman conquest of Bosnia** — The armies of Sultan Mehmed II conquered most of Bosnia in 1463, ending the medieval kingdom and beginning more than four centuries of Ottoman rule. [bih-history-britannica, ottoman-bosnia-britannica]

Figures:

- Ban Kulin (ruled c. 1180 – 1204) — Ban (ruler) of Bosnia. The first clearly documented ruler of Bosnia, Kulin presided over a prosperous era remembered in later folklore as a golden age; his 1189 charter to Dubrovnik is a foundational text of Bosnian statehood and early Bosnian Cyrillic writing.
- Tvrtko I (ruled 1353 – 1391) — First King of Bosnia. Of the Kotromanić dynasty, Tvrtko I extended Bosnia to the Adriatic and was crowned king in 1377, making the kingdom the leading power of the western Balkans before its post-mortem decline toward Ottoman conquest.

## Ottoman Bosnia and the Making of a Multi-Confessional Land — 1463 – 1878

Four centuries of Ottoman rule founded Sarajevo and Mostar, drew much of the population to Islam, and wove together the Muslim, Orthodox, Catholic and Jewish communities that still define Bosnia.

- 1463: **Bosnia incorporated into the Ottoman Empire** — Following the conquest, Bosnia became an Ottoman province; the settlement of Vrhbosna was developed into Sarajevo, the future provincial capital. [ottoman-bosnia-britannica, bih-history-britannica]
- 16th c.: **Islamization and a multi-confessional society** — Over generations much of Bosnia's Slavic population converted to Islam, creating, alongside Orthodox, Catholic and Jewish communities, the uniquely interwoven multi-confessional society that defines the country. [ottoman-bosnia-britannica]
- 1566–1577: **The Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge, Višegrad** — The eleven-arch Ottoman bridge over the Drina at Višegrad was built to designs by the imperial architect Sinan; it was inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in 2007. [visegrad-bridge-reference]
- 1566: **The Old Bridge at Mostar (Stari Most)** — The single-arch Old Bridge over the Neretva, a masterpiece of Ottoman engineering, gave Mostar its name; the Old City of Mostar was inscribed by UNESCO in 2005 after the bridge's wartime destruction and reconstruction. [mostar-unesco-reference]
- 1875–1878: **Herzegovinian uprising and the Eastern Crisis** — A peasant revolt in Herzegovina widened into a great-power crisis over the 'Eastern Question', ending Ottoman rule and leading to Austro-Hungarian occupation in 1878. [bih-history-britannica, ottoman-bosnia-britannica]

Figures:

- Mehmed Paša Sokolović (c. 1506 – 1579) — Ottoman Grand Vizier. Born in eastern Bosnia and taken into Ottoman service, Sokolović rose to become Grand Vizier of the empire. He endowed the great bridge over the Drina at Višegrad, an enduring monument of Ottoman Bosnia.
- Gazi Husrev-beg (1480 – 1541) — Ottoman governor of Bosnia. The most celebrated Ottoman governor of Bosnia, Gazi Husrev-beg transformed Sarajevo with mosques, a madrasa, baths and a great bazaar, his pious endowments shaping the city for centuries.

## Habsburg Rule, Sarajevo 1914 and the Yugoslav Kingdom — 1878 – 1941

Austria-Hungary occupied and then annexed Bosnia; in Sarajevo a single shot in 1914 set the world at war, and Bosnia was folded into a new Yugoslav kingdom.

- 1878: **Austro-Hungarian occupation** — The Congress of Berlin authorised Austria-Hungary to occupy and administer Bosnia and Herzegovina, ending effective Ottoman rule while leaving nominal sovereignty in Istanbul. [bih-history-britannica, ottoman-bosnia-britannica]
- 1908: **Annexation by Austria-Hungary** — Vienna formally annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina, triggering a European diplomatic crisis and inflaming South Slav nationalist opposition to Habsburg rule. [bih-history-britannica, franz-ferdinand-reference]
- 28 Jun 1914: **Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand** — In Sarajevo, Gavrilo Princip of the Young Bosnia movement shot dead Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie, the act that triggered the outbreak of the First World War. [franz-ferdinand-reference, loc-franz-ferdinand-reference]
- 1918: **Bosnia joins the Yugoslav kingdom** — With Austria-Hungary's collapse, Bosnia was incorporated into the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, later renamed Yugoslavia, losing its separate administrative identity. [bih-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Gavrilo Princip (1894 – 1918) — Assassin, member of Young Bosnia. A young Bosnian Serb radical who sought to free the South Slavs from Habsburg rule, Princip assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. He died in an Austrian prison; the event helped ignite the First World War.
- Archduke Franz Ferdinand (1863 – 1914) — Heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. Heir presumptive of Emperor Franz Joseph, his assassination in Sarajevo with his wife Sophie set off the July Crisis of 1914 and the slide of the great powers into the First World War.

## The Second World War and Socialist Bosnia — 1941 – 1991

Carved into a fascist puppet state and ravaged by occupation and civil war, Bosnia emerged from 1945 as one of six republics of socialist Yugoslavia.

- Apr 1941: **Axis invasion and the NDH** — The Axis dismembered Yugoslavia and incorporated Bosnia into the Ustaša-led Independent State of Croatia, whose terror against Serbs, Jews and Roma helped ignite a brutal multi-sided civil war. [zavnobih-parliament-gov, bih-history-britannica]
- 25 Nov 1943: **First session of ZAVNOBiH** — At Mrkonjić Grad the anti-fascist council ZAVNOBiH proclaimed Bosnia and Herzegovina a federal unit of its equal peoples, ensuring it would be restored as a distinct republic within Yugoslavia. [zavnobih-parliament-gov]
- 1945: **Bosnia a republic of socialist Yugoslavia** — After the Partisan victory, Bosnia and Herzegovina became one of the six federal republics of Tito's socialist Yugoslavia, with its multi-ethnic society constitutionally recognised. [bih-history-britannica]
- 1984: **Sarajevo Winter Olympics** — Sarajevo hosted the 1984 Winter Olympic Games, a high point of confidence for the multi-ethnic socialist republic less than a decade before war engulfed the city. [bih-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Josip Broz Tito (1892 – 1980) — Partisan leader and President of Yugoslavia. Leader of the communist Partisans whose wartime struggle was waged in large part across Bosnia, Tito built and ruled socialist Yugoslavia, within which Bosnia and Herzegovina was restored as a federal republic.
- Ivo Andrić (1892 – 1975) — Writer and Nobel laureate. Born near Travnik, Andrić won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1961; his novel 'The Bridge on the Drina', set at the Ottoman bridge in Višegrad, made Bosnia's layered history known to the world.

## Independence, the Bosnian War and the Srebrenica Genocide — 1992 – 1995

A vote for independence was answered by war, the siege of Sarajevo, ethnic cleansing and the genocide at Srebrenica, until the Dayton Agreement ended the fighting.

- 29 Feb – 1 Mar 1992: **Independence referendum** — Bosnians voted overwhelmingly for independence in a referendum largely boycotted by Serbs; the country was recognised internationally and joined the UN on 22 May 1992. [statedept-yugoslavia-gov, siege-sarajevo-britannica]
- 5 Apr 1992: **Siege of Sarajevo begins** — Bosnian Serb forces encircled and shelled the capital from the surrounding hills; lasting about 1,425 days, it became the longest siege of a capital city in modern history. [siege-sarajevo-britannica]
- 1992–1995: **Ethnic cleansing and the Bosnian War** — Campaigns to expel and destroy rival communities through killings, deportation, detention camps and sexual violence devastated Bosnia, crimes later prosecuted before the ICTY. [statedept-yugoslavia-gov, icty-srebrenica-gov]
- 11 Jul 1995: **The Srebrenica genocide** — After overrunning the UN 'safe area', Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić executed up to 8,000 Bosniak men and boys; the ICTY and ICJ ruled the killings genocide. [un-srebrenica-gov, icj-genocide-case-gov, icty-srebrenica-gov]
- 26 Feb 2007: **ICJ Genocide judgment (Bosnia v. Serbia)** — The International Court of Justice held that genocide was committed at Srebrenica and that Serbia had violated the Genocide Convention by failing to prevent it and to cooperate with the ICTY. [icj-genocide-judgment-gov, icj-genocide-case-gov]

Figures:

- Alija Izetbegović (1925 – 2003) — First President of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Leader of Bosnia's Bosniaks through independence and war, Izetbegović headed the wartime presidency in besieged Sarajevo and signed the Dayton Agreement that ended the conflict in 1995.
- Ratko Mladić (born 1942) — Bosnian Serb military commander. Commander of the Bosnian Serb army during the war, Mladić directed the siege of Sarajevo and the seizure of Srebrenica; the ICTY convicted him of genocide and crimes against humanity and sentenced him to life imprisonment.

## The Dayton State and the European Path — 1995 – present

The Dayton Agreement bound two entities into one state under international oversight; three decades on, Bosnia struggles with its own structure while edging toward the European Union.

- 21 Nov 1995: **Dayton Agreement initialled** — After negotiations led by Richard Holbrooke, the General Framework Agreement for Peace was initialled at Dayton, Ohio, ending the Bosnian War. [dayton-ohr-gov, dayton-accords-britannica]
- 14 Dec 1995: **Dayton Agreement signed in Paris** — The peace agreement was formally signed in Paris, preserving a single Bosnian state divided into two entities, the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska, with an Office of the High Representative. [dayton-ohr-gov, dayton-accords-britannica]
- 1999: **Brčko District established** — Binding arbitration under Annex 2 created the Brčko District, a self-governing unit held in condominium by both entities under the sovereignty of Bosnia and Herzegovina. [brcko-ohr-gov]
- 15 Dec 2022: **EU candidate status granted** — The European Council granted Bosnia and Herzegovina the status of EU candidate country, conditional on reforms to the rule of law and the constitutional and electoral system. [eu-candidate-reference]

Figures:

- Richard Holbrooke (1941 – 2010) — US diplomat and chief negotiator. As US Assistant Secretary of State, Holbrooke led the shuttle diplomacy of 1995 that brought the warring parties to Dayton, brokering the agreement that ended the Bosnian War.
- Carl Bildt (born 1949) — First High Representative for Bosnia and Herzegovina. The former Swedish prime minister became the first High Representative under Dayton, charged with overseeing the civilian implementation of the peace and the building of the post-war state.
