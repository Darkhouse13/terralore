Founding: The founding of the Republic at Þingvellir — 17 June 1944. Iceland's modern statehood is dated to 17 June 1944, when, in a ceremony at the ancient assembly plain of Þingvellir, Icelanders proclaimed a republic and dissolved the personal union with the Danish crown. The act followed an overwhelming referendum, in which more than 95 percent of voters backed the republic, and was timed to the lapse of the 1918 Act of Union while Denmark itself lay under German occupation. Sveinn Björnsson became the first president. The date was deliberately symbolic: Þingvellir had been the seat of the Althing since 930, and the republic was understood as the restoration of an independence that Icelanders traced back to the Commonwealth and that had been lost to the Norwegian crown by the Old Covenant of 1262-64. 17 June remains Iceland's national day.

Iceland's history begins late and in fire. Settled by Norse and Celtic newcomers in the Viking Age, conventionally from 874, this empty north-Atlantic island filled within sixty years and in 930 founded the Althing at Þingvellir, one of the oldest parliaments in the world and the assembly of a stateless Commonwealth that governed without a king. Around the year 1000 the same assembly voted, peacefully, to accept Christianity, and in the thirteenth century Icelanders wrote the sagas and Eddas that preserved the literature of the Norse world. Civil strife in the Age of the Sturlungs ended that independence: by the Old Covenant of 1262-64 Iceland submitted to the Norwegian crown, and with Norway it passed to Denmark, enduring the imposed Reformation, a Danish trade monopoly and the catastrophe of the Laki eruption of 1783-84. A nineteenth-century awakening led by Jón Sigurðsson restored the Althing and won Home Rule in 1904 and sovereignty in 1918; on 17 June 1944, with Denmark under German occupation, Icelanders proclaimed a republic at Þingvellir. The modern republic became a founding member of NATO in 1949, fought and won the Cod Wars over its fishing seas, helped establish the 200-mile exclusive economic zone in international law, and survived a spectacular banking collapse in 2008, all upon a land split by the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

## The Settlement and the Icelandic Commonwealth — c. 874 – 1000

Norse and Celtic newcomers filled an empty island within two generations and, having no king, built a free state governed by an assembly at Þingvellir.

- c. 874: **Ingólfr Arnarson settles Reykjavík** — By tradition the first permanent settler, Ingólfr Arnarson, came from Norway around 874 and established his homestead at the site he named Reykjavík, opening the age of settlement, the Landnám. [ingolfur-britannica, encyclopedia-settlement, iceland-britannica]
- c. 870–930: **The Landnám (Age of Settlement)** — Within roughly sixty years hundreds of Norse settlers, many by way of the Norse colonies in the British Isles, claimed Iceland's habitable land, as recorded in Íslendingabók and Landnámabók. [encyclopedia-settlement, iceland-britannica]
- 930: **Founding of the Althing at Þingvellir** — The settlers established a general assembly, the Althing, on the plain of Þingvellir, where law was recited from the Law Rock; it is one of the oldest parliaments in the world and the centre of the Icelandic Commonwealth. [thingvellir-history, thingvellir-britannica, encyclopedia-settlement]
- 930–1262: **The Icelandic Commonwealth** — A stateless society of chieftains and farmers governed itself for over three centuries through the Althing and its law council, without a king or central executive. [thingvellir-history, iceland-britannica]

Figures:

- Ingólfr Arnarson (9th century) — First permanent settler of Iceland. Norse settler who, by the testimony of the Landnámabók, came from Norway around 874 and made the first lasting home in Iceland at Reykjavík, the site of the future capital. He stands as the symbolic founder of the Icelandic nation.
- Þorgeir Ljósvetningagoði (10th–11th century) — Lawspeaker of the Althing. Lawspeaker who, at the Althing around the year 1000, was entrusted to decide between pagans and Christians and ruled that Iceland should accept Christianity, preserving the unity of the Commonwealth without bloodshed.

## Christianity, the Sagas and the Old Covenant — 1000 – 1262

The Althing voted itself Christian without civil war, the chieftains wrote the sagas and Eddas, and then internal feud delivered the free state into the hands of the Norwegian crown.

- c. 1000: **The Althing adopts Christianity** — Faced with a split between pagans and Christians, the Althing entrusted the decision to the lawspeaker Þorgeir, who ruled that Iceland should become Christian; the conversion was achieved by compromise, without bloodshed. [thingvellir-christianity, iceland-britannica]
- 1179–1241: **Snorri Sturluson and the sagas** — Chieftain and twice lawspeaker, Snorri Sturluson wrote the Prose Edda and the Heimskringla, central works of the saga age that preserved the mythology and history of the Norse world. [snorri-britannica]
- c. 1220–1262: **The Age of the Sturlungs** — Rivalry among a few powerful families, above all the Sturlungs, brought decades of feud, killing and burning, fatally weakening the Commonwealth's institutions of consensus. [medievalists-sturlungs, iceland-britannica]
- 1262–1264: **The Old Covenant (Gamli sáttmáli)** — Worn down by civil strife and pressed by King Haakon IV, the Icelandic chieftains swore allegiance to the Norwegian crown, ending the independent Commonwealth and beginning centuries of foreign rule. [medievalists-sturlungs, iceland-britannica]

Figures:

- Snorri Sturluson (1179 – 1241) — Chieftain, historian and saga writer. Born at Hvammur and twice lawspeaker of the Althing, Snorri was the greatest writer of the Icelandic medieval world. His Prose Edda and Heimskringla preserve the mythology and the royal history of Norse Scandinavia. He was killed in 1241 amid the feuds of the Sturlung Age.
- Haakon IV of Norway (1204 – 1263) — King of Norway. The Norwegian king whose persistent pressure and exploitation of Icelandic feuds brought the chieftains to accept his sovereignty in the Old Covenant of 1262-64, ending the Icelandic Commonwealth.

## Under Norway and Denmark: Reformation, Monopoly and Famine — 1262 – 1783

Passing with Norway under the Danish crown, Iceland endured an imposed Reformation, a crushing trade monopoly and recurring natural disaster on a volcanic land.

- 7 Nov 1550: **Execution of Bishop Jón Arason** — Jón Arason, bishop of Hólar and the last Catholic bishop in Iceland, was beheaded with his two sons at Skálholt for resisting the Danish-imposed Reformation, after which Iceland became fully Lutheran. [jon-arason-britannica, iceland-britannica]
- 1602–1786: **The Danish trade monopoly** — The Danish crown confined all Icelandic trade to a small group of licensed Danish merchants, depressing prices and returns and impoverishing the island for nearly two centuries. [iceland-britannica, nordics-1840s]
- 8 Jun 1783: **The Laki eruption** — The Laki fissure erupted for eight months from June 1783, producing about 14.7 cubic kilometres of lava and immense quantities of toxic gas that poisoned the land and spread a haze across Europe. [laki-eruption, iceland-britannica]
- 1783–1785: **The Móðuharðindin famine** — Fallout from Laki killed most of Iceland's livestock; the ensuing 'famine of the mist' killed roughly a fifth of the population, the worst disaster in the country's history. [laki-eruption]

Figures:

- Jón Arason (1484 – 1550) — Last Catholic bishop of Iceland. Bishop of Hólar and a noted poet, Jón Arason resisted the Reformation imposed by Christian III of Denmark. His execution with his sons at Skálholt in 1550 ended Catholic Iceland and made him a national as well as a religious hero.
- Christian III of Denmark (1503 – 1559) — King of Denmark and Norway. The Danish king who imposed Lutheranism across his realms, including Iceland, breaking the power of the Catholic bishops and bringing the island into the Protestant world.

## National Awakening, Home Rule and Sovereignty — 1783 – 1918

A nineteenth-century revival, led by Jón Sigurðsson and rooted in the memory of the Commonwealth, restored the Althing and carried Iceland to Home Rule and then to sovereign statehood.

- 1843: **The Althing restored** — A royal decree of 1843 revived the Althing, which first met again on 1 July 1845 as an advisory assembly, reviving in modern form the institution founded in 930. [nordics-1840s, thingvellir-independence]
- 1874: **Constitution on the settlement millennium** — On the thousandth anniversary of the settlement, Denmark granted Iceland a constitution and increased control over domestic affairs, though executive power remained with the crown. [nordics-1840s, thingvellir-independence]
- 1904: **Home Rule** — Iceland gained Home Rule, with an Icelandic minister resident in Reykjavík and answerable to the Althing, a major step toward full self-government. [nordics-1840s]
- 1 Dec 1918: **The Act of Union and the Kingdom of Iceland** — The Danish-Icelandic Act of Union made Iceland a sovereign state in personal union with the Danish crown, with Denmark retaining foreign affairs and the union open to termination after twenty-five years. [actofunion-britannica, nordics-1840s]

Figures:

- Jón Sigurðsson (1811 – 1879) — Leader of the independence movement. Scholar and statesman, the central figure of Iceland's nineteenth-century awakening, who campaigned for the end of the Danish trade monopoly and for self-government. He is Iceland's national hero, and his birthday, 17 June, was chosen as the date for founding the republic.
- Hannes Hafstein (1861 – 1922) — First minister for Iceland under Home Rule. Poet and statesman who in 1904 became the first Icelandic minister under Home Rule, resident in Reykjavík and responsible to the Althing, presiding over a period of rapid modernisation.

## Occupation, the Republic and NATO — 1918 – 1949

Wartime occupation by Britain and the United States severed the link to an occupied Denmark; Icelanders proclaimed a republic at Þingvellir in 1944 and abandoned neutrality to help found NATO.

- May 1940: **British occupation of Iceland** — After Germany occupied Denmark, British forces occupied strategically vital Iceland in May 1940 to forestall a German move, severing Iceland's last constitutional ties to its monarch. [nordics-from1944, iceland-britannica]
- 1941: **United States assumes Iceland's defence** — By agreement with the Icelandic government, the United States took over the island's defence in 1941, building a substantial military presence in the North Atlantic. [statedept-nato, iceland-britannica]
- 17 Jun 1944: **Founding of the Republic at Þingvellir** — After an overwhelming referendum, Icelanders proclaimed a republic at Þingvellir, ending the union with Denmark; Sveinn Björnsson became the first president and 17 June became the national day. [ebsco-republic, nordics-from1944, sveinn-britannica]
- 1949: **Iceland a founding member of NATO** — Abandoning neutrality, Iceland joined NATO as a founding member in 1949, a state with no army, on the understanding it would not host foreign troops in peacetime; the decision provoked a riot at the Althing. [statedept-nato, nordics-from1944]

Figures:

- Sveinn Björnsson (1881 – 1952) — First President of the Republic of Iceland. Diplomat and statesman who served as regent during the war and, on 17 June 1944, became the first president of the new republic, a post he held until his death in 1952.
- Ólafur Thors (1892 – 1964) — Prime Minister at the founding of the republic. Leader of the Independence Party and several times prime minister, Ólafur Thors headed the government that proclaimed the republic in 1944 and led the contested decision to join NATO in 1949.

## The Cod Wars and the Modern Republic — 1949 – present

A small, unarmed state defeated the Royal Navy at sea, won control of its fishing grounds and helped write the 200-mile EEZ into international law, then weathered the largest banking collapse in history.

- 1958: **First Cod War (12 nautical miles)** — Iceland extended its fishery limit from four to twelve nautical miles in 1958; after confrontations at sea Britain acknowledged the twelve-mile limit, the first of Iceland's victories over the Royal Navy. [ebsco-codwars, iceland-britannica]
- 1972–1973: **Second Cod War (50 nautical miles)** — Iceland extended its limit to fifty miles from September 1972; its coast guard cut dozens of British trawl nets before a NATO-mediated interim agreement was reached in November 1973. [ebsco-codwars]
- 1975–1976: **Third Cod War (200 nautical miles)** — Iceland claimed a 200-mile zone in 1975; after clashes and a threat to close the Keflavík NATO base, Britain recognised the 200-mile limit in an agreement of 2 June 1976. [ebsco-codwars, statedept-nato]
- 1976: **The 200-mile limit and the EEZ** — Iceland's successful assertion of a 200-mile fishing zone helped establish the Exclusive Economic Zone in international law, later codified in the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. [ebsco-codwars]
- Oct 2008: **Collapse of the banks** — The three big Icelandic banks, whose assets dwarfed the economy, failed within days in October 2008 in the largest banking collapse relative to GDP in history; the state passed emergency laws and turned to the IMF before recovering. [nordics-from1944, iceland-britannica]

Figures:

- Vigdís Finnbogadóttir (born 1930) — President of Iceland. Elected president in 1980, Vigdís Finnbogadóttir was the world's first popularly elected woman head of state, serving until 1996 and becoming an enduring symbol of modern Iceland.
- Geir Hallgrímsson (1925 – 1990) — Prime Minister during the Third Cod War. Prime minister from 1974 to 1978, he led Iceland through the Third Cod War and the brinkmanship over the Keflavík base that secured British recognition of the 200-mile limit in 1976.
