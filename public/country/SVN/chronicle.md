Founding: The Declaration of Independence of the Republic of Slovenia — 25 June 1991. Modern Slovenia dates its statehood to 25 June 1991, when, following a plebiscite held on 23 December 1990 in which 88.5 per cent of all eligible voters chose independence, the Slovenian parliament adopted the Basic Constitutional Charter on the Sovereignty and Independence of the Republic of Slovenia together with a formal Declaration of Independence. The move made Slovenia, with Croatia, the first of Yugoslavia's republics to break away. It provoked the intervention of the Yugoslav People's Army and the Ten-Day War of 27 June to 7 July 1991, which the Slovene Territorial Defence won; the Brioni Declaration of 7 July halted the fighting and led to the withdrawal of federal forces. The deeper roots of Slovene identity reach back to the early Slavic principality of Carantania and to the founding of the literary language by the Protestant reformer Primoz Trubar in 1550.

Slovenia's history is the long story of a small Alpine and Adriatic people who preserved a distinct language and identity through more than a thousand years of foreign rule. Slavic ancestors settled the eastern Alps in the sixth and seventh centuries and organised the early principality of Carantania, remembered for its ritual enthronement of dukes in the Slovene tongue. From the late Middle Ages the Slovene lands were gathered into the Habsburg duchies of Carniola, Styria and Carinthia, and remained under Austrian rule for some six centuries. The Reformation gave the Slovenes their first printed books, when Primoz Trubar published a catechism and a primer in 1550 and founded a literary language. Napoleon's short-lived Illyrian Provinces, with Ljubljana as their capital, and a nineteenth-century cultural awakening led by the poet France Preseren prepared the way for modern nationhood. After the fall of Austria-Hungary in 1918 the Slovenes joined the Yugoslav state, becoming after 1945 the most prosperous republic of socialist Yugoslavia, before declaring independence on 25 June 1991 and winning it in the brief Ten-Day War. Slovenia joined NATO and the European Union in 2004 and adopted the euro in 2007.

## Carantania and the Slavic Settlement of the Eastern Alps — c. 550 – 1000

Slavic peoples settled the eastern Alps and organised Carantania, an early principality whose dukes were enthroned in the Slovene tongue before Frankish power absorbed the region.

- 6th–7th c.: **Slavic settlement of the eastern Alps** — Slavic peoples migrated into the eastern Alpine valleys after the fall of Roman authority, becoming the ancestors of the Slovenes and establishing the linguistic foundation of the later nation. [slovenia-si-past, govsi-history]
- 7th c.: **Founding of Carantania** — The Slavic principality of Carantania took shape in the eastern Alps, centred north of present-day Slovenia, and is remembered for the enthronement of its dukes in the Slovene language at the Prince's Stone. [slovenia-si-past, govsi-history]
- c. 745: **Carantania under Frankish overlordship** — Allied with Bavaria against the Avars, Carantania was drawn into the Frankish realm and gradually Christianised, ending its independence and binding the region to the Holy Roman Empire. [slovenia-si-past, govsi-history]
- 973: **Carniola first attested** — The march of Carniola, in the central part of present-day Slovenia, appears in the records as a frontier zone of the empire, the nucleus of the duchy that would later define the Slovene lands. [govsi-history, slovenia-britannica-middle-ages]

Figures:

- Carantanian dukes — Rulers of the early Slavic principality. The dukes of Carantania were installed in a ritual conducted in the Slovene tongue at the Prince's Stone, a ceremony that survived into the late Middle Ages and became a touchstone of Slovene political memory.
- Samo (fl. 7th c.) — Frankish-era Slavic ruler. A figure associated in tradition with the early organisation of the Alpine and Pannonian Slavs, invoked in Slovenia's national narrative as a precursor of Carantanian self-rule before Frankish dominance.

## The Habsburg Duchies and the Birth of Written Slovene — 1000 – 1789

Gathered into the Habsburg duchies of Carniola, Styria and Carinthia, the Slovene lands endured six centuries of Austrian rule, in which the Reformation gave them their first books and a literary language.

- 1335: **Habsburgs acquire Carniola and Carinthia** — Carinthia and Carniola passed to the Habsburg dynasty, which already held Styria, bringing nearly all the Slovene lands under the rule that would last about six centuries. [slovenia-britannica-middle-ages, govsi-history]
- 1364: **Carniola raised to a duchy** — Carniola was elevated to the status of a duchy, consolidating the core territorial unit of the Slovene lands, with Ljubljana as its principal city. [slovenia-britannica-middle-ages, ljubljana-britannica]
- 1550: **First books printed in Slovene** — Primoz Trubar published the Catechismus and Abecedarium, the first printed books in the Slovene language, founding the literary language as a product of the Protestant Reformation. [trubar-britannica, culture-si-protestantism, stb-reformation]
- 1584: **Dalmatin's Bible and Bohoric's grammar** — Jurij Dalmatin published a complete Slovene translation of the Bible and Adam Bohoric the first Slovene grammar, proving the new written language fit for the most demanding texts. [govsi-history, stb-reformation]
- 1490: **Mercury found at Idrija** — Mercury was first discovered at Idrija, which grew into one of the world's two largest mercury mines; the site is today recognised by UNESCO as part of the Heritage of Mercury. [idrija-unesco]

Figures:

- Primoz Trubar (1508 – 1586) — Protestant reformer and founder of literary Slovene. A Protestant preacher who, in exile, wrote and printed the first books in Slovene in 1550. By rendering the catechism and the alphabet into the vernacular he created a literary language and is honoured as the father of Slovene letters.
- Jurij Dalmatin (c. 1547 – 1589) — Bible translator. A student of the Slovene Reformation who completed a full translation of the Bible into Slovene, published in 1584, demonstrating that the young written language could carry the entire scriptural canon.

## The Illyrian Provinces and the National Awakening — 1789 – 1914

Napoleon's Illyrian Provinces made Ljubljana a capital and stirred Slovene self-awareness; a nineteenth-century cultural awakening, led by the poet France Preseren, turned a literary identity into a political programme.

- 1809–1813: **The Illyrian Provinces** — Napoleon organised the southern Slovene lands as the Illyrian Provinces of the French Empire, with Ljubljana as their capital, introducing reforms and giving the Slovene language a place in public life. [illyrian-britannica-history, govsi-history]
- 1815: **Austria regains the Slovene lands** — After Napoleon's defeat the Congress of Vienna restored Habsburg rule over the Illyrian territories, returning the Slovene lands to the Austrian Empire. [illyrian-britannica-history, ljubljana-britannica]
- 1844: **Preseren writes Zdravljica** — The poet France Preseren composed Zdravljica, a toast to liberty and the brotherhood of nations whose seventh stanza became the lyric of the Slovene national anthem after independence. [preseren-britannica]
- 1848: **The United Slovenia programme** — During the Spring of Nations, Slovene activists demanded a single autonomous unit joining all Slovene districts of the monarchy with the Slovene language in public use, marking the birth of modern Slovene politics. [govsi-history, slovenia-si-past]

Figures:

- France Preseren (1800 – 1849) — National poet of Slovenia. Slovenia's foremost contributor to European Romanticism, Preseren elevated the Slovene language to the highest literary art. His poem Zdravljica, calling for the freedom and equality of nations, became the source of the national anthem.
- Primoz Trubar (1508 – 1586) — Founder of literary Slovene. The Reformation-era writer whose first Slovene books made possible the later literary and national revival; his legacy was repeatedly invoked by the awakeners of the nineteenth century as proof of the language's antiquity and dignity.

## Into Yugoslavia: War, Partition and Contested Borders — 1914 – 1945

The fall of Austria-Hungary drew the Slovenes into a Yugoslav state, but the new frontiers were bitterly contested: Carinthia voted to stay with Austria, Italy seized the western marches, and the Second World War tore the land into three occupation zones.

- 1 Dec 1918: **Birth of the Yugoslav kingdom** — After the brief State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs, the Slovene lands joined the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, proclaimed on 1 December 1918 and renamed the Kingdom of Yugoslavia in 1929. [scs-britannica, slovenia-britannica-since-1918]
- 10 Oct 1920: **The Carinthian Plebiscite** — In the disputed southern Carinthian basin, a majority of about 59 per cent voted to remain with Austria rather than join Yugoslavia, leaving a Slovene minority on the Austrian side of the border. [carinthia-plebiscite-mdpi, slovenia-britannica-since-1918]
- 1920: **Treaty of Rapallo and the Italian border** — Under the Treaty of Rapallo, Italy gained the Slovene Littoral, the Karst and parts of Inner Carniola, subjecting a large Slovene population to Italian and later Fascist rule. [slovenia-britannica-since-1918]
- Apr 1941: **Axis partition of Slovenia** — Following the invasion of Yugoslavia, the Axis powers carved up the Slovene lands among Germany, Italy and Hungary, while a Liberation Front founded in Ljubljana launched armed resistance. [slovenia-britannica-since-1918, govsi-history]

Figures:

- Anton Korosec (1872 – 1940) — Slovene political leader. A priest and statesman who led the Slovene People's Party and presided over the National Council that proclaimed the State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs in 1918, the only Slovene to head a Yugoslav government.
- Edvard Kardelj (1910 – 1979) — Partisan and communist theoretician. A Slovene communist who helped found the Liberation Front in 1941 and became one of the leading theorists of postwar Yugoslavia, shaping its system of self-management and federal structure.

## Socialist Yugoslavia and the Road to Independence — 1945 – 1991

As the most prosperous republic of socialist Yugoslavia, Slovenia settled its western border with Italy, then broke away in 1991 in the first secession from the Yugoslav federation, won in the Ten-Day War.

- 1945: **Slovenia within socialist Yugoslavia** — Slovenia became one of the six republics of Tito's Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, developing into its most prosperous and industrialised member. [slovenia-britannica-since-1918]
- 5 Oct 1954: **London Memorandum on Trieste** — The United States, United Kingdom, Italy and Yugoslavia agreed to divide the Free Territory of Trieste, placing Zone A under Italian and Zone B under Yugoslav administration. [trieste-frus, osimo-reference]
- 10 Nov 1975: **Treaty of Osimo** — Italy and Yugoslavia signed the Treaty of Osimo, making the partition of the former Free Territory of Trieste definitive and settling the long-disputed western border. [osimo-reference]
- 23 Dec 1990: **Plebiscite on independence** — In a plebiscite with 93.2 per cent turnout, some 88.5 per cent of all eligible Slovene voters chose independence from Yugoslavia, providing the mandate for statehood. [govsi-independence, govsi-history]
- 25 Jun – 7 Jul 1991: **Independence and the Ten-Day War** — Slovenia declared independence on 25 June 1991; the Yugoslav army's intervention sparked the Ten-Day War, which the Slovenes won, leading to the Brioni Declaration of 7 July and the withdrawal of federal forces. [govsi-independence, slovenia-britannica-since-1918, brioni-reference]

Figures:

- Milan Kucan (born 1941) — First President of Slovenia. The last head of the Slovene communist party and the country's first president, Kucan steered Slovenia from the loosening of Yugoslavia through the 1990 plebiscite and the 1991 declaration of independence.
- Josip Broz Tito (1892 – 1980) — Leader of socialist Yugoslavia. The Partisan commander and lifelong president of Yugoslavia under whom Slovenia became the federation's most prosperous republic; his death in 1980 began the unravelling that ended in Slovene independence.

## A European Republic and the Border Arbitration — 1991 – present

Independent Slovenia anchored itself in NATO and the European Union, adopted the euro, and pursued a long-running maritime border dispute with Croatia to international arbitration.

- 29 Mar 2004: **Slovenia joins NATO** — After a 2003 referendum approved by some two-thirds of voters, Slovenia became a member of NATO on 29 March 2004, anchoring its security in the Western alliance. [govsi-nato, eu-slovenia]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to the European Union** — Slovenia joined the European Union on 1 May 2004 in the bloc's largest enlargement, the only former Yugoslav republic to accede in that wave. [eu-slovenia]
- 1 Jan 2007: **Adoption of the euro** — Slovenia became the first of the 2004 entrants to adopt the euro, replacing the tolar on 1 January 2007 and completing its integration into the core of the European Union. [eu-slovenia]
- 4 Nov 2009: **Arbitration Agreement with Croatia** — Slovenia and Croatia signed an Arbitration Agreement submitting their land and maritime border dispute, including Piran Bay, to the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague. [govsi-arbitration, pca-case]
- 29 Jun 2017: **PCA award on the Piran Bay border** — The Permanent Court of Arbitration rendered its Final Award, fixing the land and maritime boundary through Piran Bay and granting Slovenia a junction to the high seas; Slovenia accepted the award, Croatia rejected it. [pca-case, govsi-arbitration]

Figures:

- Janez Drnovsek (1950 – 2008) — Prime Minister and President. The dominant statesman of Slovenia's first independent decade, Drnovsek led the governments that stabilised the economy and negotiated entry into NATO and the European Union, and later served as president.
- Borut Pahor (born 1963) — Prime Minister and President. Prime minister when the 2009 Arbitration Agreement with Croatia was signed and later president, he was a central figure in Slovenia's pursuit of a settled border and its place in the European Union.
