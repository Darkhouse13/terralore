Founding: Legendary founding of the imperial line — 660 BCE (traditional). Japanese tradition, recorded in the 8th-century chronicles Kojiki and Nihon shoki, dates the founding of the realm to the accession of the legendary Emperor Jimmu in 660 BCE, a descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu. No historical evidence supports Jimmu's existence; modern scholarship treats him as a mythical figure and locates the real emergence of organised Japanese statehood in the Yamato polity of central Honshu around the 3rd to 4th centuries CE.

Japanese history unfolds across more than two millennia on an archipelago off the eastern edge of Asia. From the rice-farming Yayoi settlements emerged the Yamato state, whose chieftains claimed descent from the sun goddess and whose imperial line, by tradition, has never been broken. Successive ages absorbed Chinese script, Buddhism, and statecraft, then refined them into something unmistakably Japanese: the courtly elegance of Heian Kyoto, the warrior ethos of the samurai, the iron stability of the Tokugawa peace. After more than two centuries of self-imposed isolation, Japan responded to Western pressure not with collapse but with the Meiji Restoration, racing to build a modern industrial empire. Imperial overreach ended in the catastrophe of 1945, after which a reconstructed, constitutionally pacifist Japan rose to become a democratic, economic power while its emperor endured as a symbol of the state.

## Yamato and Asuka: The Birth of the State — c. 300 - 710 CE

Out of the rice paddies and burial mounds of central Honshu rose the Yamato court, which imported Buddhism, the Chinese writing system, and continental statecraft to forge Japan's first centralised government.

- c. 3rd-4th century CE: **Rise of the Yamato state** — Clan leaders on the Yamato plain consolidate power, build monumental kofun tombs, and establish the imperial lineage claiming descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu. [jimmu, japan-place]
- c. 538-552 CE: **Introduction of Buddhism** — Buddhism reaches Japan from the Korean kingdom of Baekje, traditionally dated to 552; it is championed by the Soga clan over conservative opposition. [asuka-period, japan-place]
- 604 CE: **Prince Shotoku's Seventeen-Article Constitution** — As regent, Prince Shotoku promulgates a set of Confucian and Buddhist moral precepts for government and intensifies the adoption of Chinese institutions. [shotoku, asuka-period]
- 645 CE: **The Taika Reform** — A court coup destroys Soga dominance and inaugurates reforms reorganizing land and administration along centralized Chinese lines, the basis of the ritsuryo state. [asuka-period, japan-place]

Figures:

- Prince Shotoku (Shotoku Taishi) (574-622 CE) — Imperial regent and reformer. Regent for Empress Suiko, he gave Buddhism official patronage, resumed embassies to China, and is traditionally credited with the Seventeen-Article Constitution and the founding of temples such as Horyu-ji, helping to define Japan's early state.

## Nara and Heian: The Classical Age — 710 - 1185 CE

With permanent capitals at Nara and then Heian-kyo (Kyoto), an aristocratic court presided over a golden age of Buddhist art, vernacular literature, and Fujiwara political dominance.

- 710 CE: **Capital established at Nara** — The court founds Japan's first permanent capital, Heijo-kyo (Nara), modeled on the Tang Chinese capital, beginning the Nara period. [nara-period, japan-place]
- 794 CE: **Capital moved to Heian-kyo (Kyoto)** — Emperor Kanmu relocates the capital to Heian-kyo, the site of Kyoto, opening the Heian period and a long era of aristocratic court culture. [heian-period]
- c. 1010 CE: **The Tale of Genji** — The court lady Murasaki Shikibu completes The Tale of Genji, widely regarded as the world's first full-length novel and the summit of Heian literature. [tale-of-genji, murasaki]
- 1180-1185 CE: **Genpei War** — Civil war between the Taira and Minamoto warrior clans ends with Minamoto victory at Dan-no-ura in 1185, shattering court supremacy and ushering in warrior rule. [minamoto-yoritomo, kamakura-period]

Figures:

- Murasaki Shikibu (c. 978 - c. 1014 CE) — Court lady-in-waiting and novelist. A member of a lesser branch of the Fujiwara and lady-in-waiting at the Heian court, she wrote The Tale of Genji, a psychologically rich portrait of aristocratic life generally considered Japan's greatest literary work.

## Kamakura and Muromachi: The Age of the Shogun — 1185 - 1573 CE

Power passed from courtiers to warriors as Minamoto Yoritomo founded the shogunate, a system of military government that, with the Ashikaga that followed, would dominate Japan for centuries.

- 1192 CE: **Minamoto Yoritomo becomes shogun** — Yoritomo is appointed shogun, formalizing the Kamakura shogunate and a system of warrior government that would last, in varying forms, until 1868. [minamoto-yoritomo, kamakura-period]
- 1274 and 1281 CE: **Mongol invasions repelled** — Kublai Khan's Yuan forces twice attempt to invade Japan; both fleets are wrecked by typhoons later mythologized as the kamikaze, or divine winds. [kamakura-period, japan-place]
- 1338 CE: **Ashikaga Takauji founds the Muromachi shogunate** — Takauji establishes the second shogunate, ruling from Kyoto's Muromachi district and presiding over a brilliant if unstable cultural era. [muromachi-period]
- 1467-1477 CE: **Onin War** — A succession dispute ignites a decade of warfare that devastates Kyoto, cripples shogunal authority, and tips Japan into the fragmented Sengoku era. [muromachi-period, sengoku-period]

Figures:

- Minamoto Yoritomo (1147-1199 CE) — First Kamakura shogun. After leading the Minamoto to victory in the Genpei War, he founded the bakufu at Kamakura and took the title of shogun, establishing the warrior government that would rule Japan for roughly seven centuries.

## Sengoku and Edo: Unification and the Long Peace — 1467 - 1868 CE

A century of civil war ended when three great unifiers reassembled the realm; the Tokugawa then imposed more than two and a half centuries of internal peace, social order, and self-imposed isolation from the wider world.

- c. 1543 CE: **Arrival of Europeans and firearms** — Portuguese traders reach Japan, introducing firearms and, soon after, Christianity, adding new pressures to the Sengoku wars. [sengoku-period, japan-place]
- 1590 CE: **Hideyoshi completes unification** — Toyotomi Hideyoshi brings the last domains under central control, finishing the reunification of Japan begun by Oda Nobunaga. [toyotomi-hideyoshi, azuchi-momoyama-period]
- 1600 CE: **Battle of Sekigahara** — Tokugawa Ieyasu defeats a rival coalition in the decisive battle that makes him master of Japan and clears the path to the shogunate. [battle-of-sekigahara, tokugawa-ieyasu]
- 1603 CE: **Tokugawa shogunate founded** — Ieyasu is appointed shogun and establishes his government at Edo, beginning the Tokugawa (Edo) period of long internal peace. [tokugawa-ieyasu, sakoku]
- c. 1633-1639 CE: **Sakoku: national seclusion** — A series of edicts seals Japan from most foreign contact, banning Christianity and overseas travel and restricting trade to Nagasaki for over two centuries. [sakoku]

Figures:

- Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543-1616 CE) — Founder of the Tokugawa shogunate. The last of the three great unifiers, he won supremacy at Sekigahara in 1600 and became shogun in 1603, founding a dynasty that ruled Japan in peace from Edo for more than 250 years.
- Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537-1598 CE) — Unifier and imperial minister. Rising from peasant origins to chief minister, he completed Japan's unification by 1590, conducted sweeping land surveys and disarmament of the peasantry, and launched two costly invasions of Korea.

## Meiji and Empire: Revolution from Above — 1868 - 1945 CE

Confronted by Western power, Japan toppled the shogunate, restored the emperor, and remade itself into a modern industrial nation-state, then a colonial empire whose ambitions led to catastrophic war.

- 1868 CE: **Meiji Restoration** — A coup ends the Tokugawa shogunate and restores direct imperial rule under Emperor Meiji, launching rapid modernization and Westernization. [meiji-restoration]
- 1889 CE: **Meiji Constitution promulgated** — Japan adopts a Western-influenced constitution establishing a constitutional monarchy with an imperial parliament, the Diet. [meiji-restoration, japan-place]
- 1904-1905 CE: **Russo-Japanese War** — Japan's victory over Russia marks the first modern defeat of a European power by an Asian state and confirms Japan as a major power. [japan-place]
- 1941 CE: **Attack on Pearl Harbor** — Japan attacks the U.S. fleet at Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States into World War II and opening the Pacific War. [pacific-war]
- 1945 CE: **Atomic bombings and surrender** — The United States drops atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki on August 6 and 9; Japan announces surrender, formalized on September 2, ending World War II. [atomic-bombings, pacific-war]

Figures:

- Emperor Meiji (Mutsuhito) (1852-1912 CE) — Emperor of Japan. In whose name the 1868 restoration was carried out, he reigned over Japan's transformation from a secluded feudal society into a modern industrial and military power and the framing of its first constitution.

## Postwar Japan: Reconstruction and the Modern Nation — 1945 - present

Defeated and occupied, Japan adopted a pacifist democratic constitution, regained sovereignty, and rose through an extraordinary economic miracle to become one of the world's leading industrial democracies.

- 1947 CE: **Pacifist constitution takes effect** — Japan's postwar constitution establishes democracy and popular sovereignty, makes the emperor a symbol of the state, and renounces war under Article 9. [state-occupation]
- 1951-1952 CE: **Treaty of San Francisco and end of occupation** — Signed in 1951 and effective in 1952, the peace treaty restores Japanese sovereignty and ends the Allied occupation. [state-occupation]
- 1964 CE: **Postwar economic miracle** — Hosting the Tokyo Olympics symbolizes Japan's recovery as it becomes an industrial powerhouse during decades of rapid, sustained growth. [state-occupation, japan-place]
- 2011 CE: **Tohoku earthquake and Fukushima disaster** — A magnitude-9.0 earthquake and tsunami devastate northeastern Japan and trigger meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant. [japan-place]

Figures:

- Douglas MacArthur (1880-1964) — Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers. As head of the Allied occupation from 1945, the American general directed the demilitarization and democratic reform of Japan, including the drafting of its 1947 constitution.
