# The Chronicle of Gabon: From the rainforest hunter-gatherers and the Bantu migrations of the Ogooué basin, through the Atlantic and slave trades of the Myènè coast, the founding of Libreville and French Equatorial Africa, to independence in 1960 and the long Bongo era ended by the coup of 2023.

Founding: Independence from France — 17 August 1960. Gabon became an independent republic on 17 August 1960, ending decades as a French colony within French Equatorial Africa. Léon M'ba, who had led the territory's government in the final colonial years, became the country's first head of state and was formally elected president in February 1961. Independence came at the close of a long history: the rainforest hunter-gatherers and Bantu farmers of the Ogooué basin; the Myènè and Fang peoples of the coast and interior; the Portuguese naming of the estuary as Gabão in 1472 and the centuries of Atlantic and slave trades that followed; the founding of Libreville in 1849 as a settlement for liberated captives; Brazza's exploration of the Ogooué; and incorporation into French Equatorial Africa in 1910. Gabon retained close political, military and economic ties to France after independence, and from 1967 entered the long presidency of Omar Bongo.

Gabon straddles the equator on the Atlantic coast of Central Africa, a heavily forested country drained by the Ogooué River. Its earliest inhabitants were rainforest hunter-gatherers — the Babongo and related peoples — followed over the first millennium BCE and after by Bantu-speaking farmers and ironworkers whose movements left some 1,800 rock engravings and dense archaeological remains in the Lopé-Okanda landscape, inscribed by UNESCO in 2007. Coastal Myènè peoples such as the Mpongwe and Orungu, and later the Fang, settled the estuary and rivers and became middlemen in the Atlantic trade. Portuguese navigators reached the estuary in 1472 and named it Gabão; over the following centuries European traders dealt in ivory, dyewoods and enslaved people through coastal kingdoms linked to Orungu and Loango. France signed treaties with Mpongwe rulers from 1839, and in 1849 settled freed captives from a seized slave ship at a site named Libreville. Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza's expeditions opened the Ogooué interior, Gabon became part of French Equatorial Africa in 1910, and Albert Schweitzer founded his hospital at Lambaréné in 1913. Gabon became independent on 17 August 1960 under Léon M'ba; from 1967 it was ruled for over four decades by Omar Bongo, sustained by oil wealth, and then by his son Ali Bongo, until a military coup on 30 August 2023 ended the Bongo family's 56-year hold on power.

## Forest peoples and the Bantu migrations — Prehistory – c. 1000 CE

The equatorial forest of the Ogooué basin was home to hunter-gatherers such as the Babongo, then to Bantu-speaking farmers and ironworkers whose passage left rock engravings and settlement sites preserved in the Lopé-Okanda landscape.

- deep prehistory: **Forest hunter-gatherers of the Ogooué** — Rainforest hunter-gatherers, ancestors of peoples such as the Babongo and Baka, inhabited the equatorial forest of present-day Gabon, living by hunting, fishing and gathering and trading with later farming communities. [britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki, globalsecurity-gabon-people]
- c. 1st millennium BCE: **Bantu farmers and ironworkers arrive** — Bantu-speaking peoples moved into the region from the north, bringing farming, pottery and iron-working; the Ogooué valley served as a corridor of the wider Bantu expansion across Central Africa. [britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki, unesco-lope-okanda]
- early settlement sites: **Settlement and iron-working at Lopé-Okanda** — The Lopé-Okanda landscape along the Ogooué preserves dense habitation sites, iron-working remains and about 1,800 rock engravings, recording the successive passage of peoples through the river corridor. [unesco-lope-okanda, world-heritage-datasheet-lope]

## The Myènè coast, the Fang, and the Atlantic trade — c. 1000 – 1840

Myènè-speaking peoples such as the Mpongwe and Orungu settled the estuary and rivers and, after Portuguese ships named the coast Gabão in 1472, became middlemen in a trade in ivory, dyewoods and enslaved people linked to Orungu and Loango.

- 1472: **Portuguese reach the estuary and name it Gabão** — Portuguese navigators arrived at the estuary of the Komo River in 1472 and named it Gabão, the Portuguese word for a hooded cloak, after the estuary's shape; the country's name derives from it. [britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki]
- c. 11th–13th centuries: **Myènè peoples settle the coast** — Myènè-speaking peoples including the Mpongwe at the estuary, the Orungu at Cape Lopez and the Nkomi at Fernan Vaz settled the Gabon coast, organised in clans well placed to trade with the interior and the sea. [britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki, globalsecurity-gabon-people]
- c. 1760s–1840s: **Peak of the Atlantic slave trade** — The Atlantic slave trade on the Gabon coast reached its height, driven by Brazilian and Cuban demand; the Orungu kingdom and the Loango coast served as middlemen channelling captives from the interior to European slavers. [britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki, wisc-slave-trade-gabon]
- 1788: **Captives embarked at Gabon and Cape Lopez** — Records describe roughly 500 captives a year being embarked at points called Gabon and Cape Lopez in the late eighteenth century, an index of the trade run through the coastal Myènè kingdoms. [britannica-history-gabon, wisc-slave-trade-gabon]

## Libreville, Brazza, and French conquest — 1839 – 1910

France signed treaties with Mpongwe rulers from 1839, settled freed captives at Libreville in 1849, and through Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza's Ogooué expeditions extended its rule, folding Gabon into French Equatorial Africa in 1910.

- 1839: **France treats with King Denis** — The French officer Bouët-Willaumez signed a treaty in 1839 with the Mpongwe ruler Antchouwe Kowe Rapontchombo ("King Denis"), securing French trading and basing rights on the Gabon Estuary. [libreville-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]
- 1849: **Libreville founded for freed slaves** — Freed captives taken from a slave ship seized by the French navy were settled on the estuary in 1849; the settlement was named Libreville, "free town," and became the colonial and modern capital. [libreville-wiki, britannica-history-gabon, history-gabon-wiki]
- 1875–1882: **Brazza explores the Ogooué** — Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza led expeditions up the Ogooué and on to the Congo River, founding Franceville in 1880 and signing treaties that opened the interior to French rule. [brazza-wiki, britannica-brazza, fea-wiki]
- 1910: **Gabon joins French Equatorial Africa** — France reorganised its Central African colonies into French Equatorial Africa in 1910 — Gabon, the Middle Congo, Ubangi-Shari and Chad, governed from Brazzaville — with Gabon a separate colony. [fea-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]

Figures:

- Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza (1852–1905) — Explorer of the Ogooué and Congo. Italian-born French explorer whose expeditions up the Ogooué from 1875 and treaties on the Congo opened the interior to French rule; founder of Franceville (1880) and namesake of Brazzaville.

## Colonial Gabon and the road to independence — 1910 – 1960

Within French Equatorial Africa, Gabon's forests fed a timber economy, Albert Schweitzer opened his hospital at Lambaréné in 1913, and a new African political class emerged that carried the colony to independence in 1960.

- 1913: **Schweitzer founds the Lambaréné hospital** — Albert Schweitzer and his wife Hélène arrived at Lambaréné on the Ogooué in 1913 and founded a hospital near a mission; it treated thousands and made Lambaréné internationally known. Schweitzer received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1952. [schweitzer-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]
- 1946: **Overseas territory of the French Union** — After the Second World War Gabon became an overseas territory within the French Union, gaining elected representation and laying the groundwork for African political movements led by figures such as Léon M'ba and Jean-Hilaire Aubame. [britannica-history-gabon, nationsencyclopedia-gabon]
- 1956: **Oil discovered near Port-Gentil** — Oil was discovered offshore at the Ozouri field near Port-Gentil in 1956; petroleum would become the foundation of independent Gabon's economy and a key source of state revenue. [britannica-economy-gabon, imf-gabon-oil]
- 1958: **Internal autonomy as the Gabonese Republic** — Under the 1958 French Community Gabon became an autonomous republic with Léon M'ba as head of government, a step short of full sovereignty within the French constitutional framework. [britannica-history-gabon, nationsencyclopedia-gabon]

Figures:

- Albert Schweitzer (1875–1965) — Physician, theologian, founder of the Lambaréné hospital. Alsatian doctor and thinker who in 1913 founded a hospital at Lambaréné on the Ogooué, working there for much of his life; his ethic of "reverence for life" and his medical mission earned the 1952 Nobel Peace Prize.

## Independence, oil, and the Bongo era — 1960 – 2023

Independent in 1960 under Léon M'ba, Gabon fell after his death to Omar Bongo, who ruled for over four decades on oil wealth and a one-party state; his son Ali Bongo followed, until a military coup in 2023 ended the family's 56-year rule.

- 17 August 1960: **Independence under Léon M'ba** — Gabon became an independent republic on 17 August 1960, with Léon M'ba as its first leader; he was formally elected president in February 1961, maintaining close ties to France. [mba-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]
- February 1964: **Coup attempt reversed by France** — Military officers briefly ousted M'ba in February 1964, but French troops intervened within a day to restore him, illustrating the depth of post-independence Franco-Gabonese ties. [mba-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]
- 1967: **Omar Bongo takes power** — On M'ba's death in November 1967 his vice-president Albert-Bernard (later Omar) Bongo became president; in 1968 he made the Gabonese Democratic Party the sole legal party, beginning a four-decade rule. [omar-bongo-wiki, britannica-history-gabon]
- 2009: **Death of Omar Bongo; Ali Bongo succeeds** — Omar Bongo died in June 2009 after ruling for over forty years; his son Ali Bongo Ondimba won the ensuing presidential election, continuing the family's hold on power. [omar-bongo-wiki, coup-2023-wiki]
- 30 August 2023: **Military coup ends Bongo rule** — After a disputed 26 August 2023 election, officers led by General Brice Oligui Nguema seized power on 30 August 2023, annulling the result and detaining Ali Bongo — ending the Bongo family's 56-year hold on the presidency. [coup-2023-wiki, britannica-gabon]

Figures:

- Léon M'ba (1902–1967) — First president of Gabon. Former colonial official and Libreville mayor who led Gabon to independence and became its first president in 1961; pro-French and authoritarian, he was restored by French troops after a 1964 coup and died in office in 1967.
- Omar Bongo (1935–2009) — President of Gabon, 1967–2009. Succeeded M'ba in 1967 and ruled for over four decades through a one-party then dominant-party state funded by oil; converted to Islam in 1973, taking the name Omar. One of Africa's longest-serving leaders.
