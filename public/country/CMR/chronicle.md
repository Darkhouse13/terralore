# The Chronicle of Cameroon: From the Sao of Lake Chad and the Bantu cradle of the Grassfields to a German colony, a divided trusteeship, and a bilingual republic still seeking its unity.

Founding: Independence of French Cameroun and reunification — 1 January 1960. Modern Cameroon dates its sovereignty to 1 January 1960, when the United Nations trusteeship administered by France ended and French Cameroun became an independent republic under Ahmadou Ahidjo. The state took its present shape on 1 October 1961, when the formerly British-administered Southern Cameroons, having voted in a UN plebiscite to join rather than enter Nigeria, was reunified with the republic to form a federation. That founding rested on a far older world: the Sao towns of the Lake Chad basin, the Grassfields kingdoms such as Bamum and the Bamileke fondoms, the Fulani Adamawa Emirate of the north, and the Douala trading polities of the coast, all of which had been bound into the single colonial territory of German Kamerun from 1884 before its wartime partition between France and Britain.

Cameroon's land holds one of Africa's deepest pasts. Near Lake Chad the Sao culture raised walled towns and cast striking terracotta and bronze, while the western Grassfields are widely held to be near the cradle from which Bantu-speaking peoples spread across central and southern Africa. Over centuries the region grew dense with states and societies: the trading Douala of the coast, the Grassfields kingdoms and fondoms — among them Bamum, whose King Njoya invented an original script — and, in the north, the Adamawa Emirate forged by the Fulani jihad of Modibo Adama. Portuguese sailors named the Wouri estuary the Rio dos Camaroes, the river of prawns, giving the country its name. From 1884 Germany ruled the protectorate of Kamerun until Allied forces conquered it in the First World War, after which the League of Nations split the territory between France and Britain. French Cameroun won independence on 1 January 1960 under Ahmadou Ahidjo, and in 1961 the British Southern Cameroons voted to join it, forming a federation reunified on 1 October 1961, while the British Northern Cameroons joined Nigeria. Since 1982 the country has been led by Paul Biya, and since 2016 the Anglophone regions have been gripped by a grave separatist crisis.

## The Sao, the Bantu cradle and Cameroon's early peoples — c. 1000 BCE – 1600 CE

Near Lake Chad the Sao raised walled towns and cast terracotta and bronze, while the western Grassfields are widely held to lie near the homeland from which Bantu-speaking peoples spread across a continent.

- c. 6th century BCE: **Rise of the Sao culture** — South of Lake Chad the Sao began building walled towns and producing distinctive terracotta and bronze artwork, forming one of the earliest known complex societies in the region. [sao-wiki, britannica-cameroon]
- c. 1000 BCE: **The Bantu expansion begins** — The Grassfields of the Cameroon-Nigeria borderlands are widely held to lie near the homeland of the Bantu languages, whose speakers spread from this region across central and southern Africa. [history-cameroon-wiki, britannica-cameroon]
- c. 11th–15th centuries: **Height of the Sao city-states** — The Sao settlements of the Lake Chad basin reached their apex between roughly the ninth and fifteenth centuries before being absorbed by neighbouring states and peoples such as the Kotoko. [sao-wiki, history-cameroon-wiki]

## Grassfields kingdoms, the Douala coast and the Fulani north — c. 1400 – 1884

Portuguese sailors named the river of prawns; the Douala grew rich as coastal middlemen; the Grassfields fondoms and the kingdom of Bamum took shape; and the Fulani jihad forged the Adamawa Emirate in the north.

- 1470s: **Portuguese name the river of prawns** — Portuguese sailors reaching the Wouri estuary named it Rio dos Camaroes, the river of prawns, after its abundant crustaceans; the name later evolved into Kamerun, Cameroun, and Cameroon. [britannica-wouri, history-cameroon-wiki]
- late 14th century: **Founding of the Bamum kingdom** — The Bamum dynasty was founded by Nshare Yen, who established Foumban as the capital of a Grassfields kingdom that grew through conquest and diplomacy. [bamum-kingdom-wiki, britannica-bamum]
- c. 1809: **Modibo Adama founds the Adamawa Emirate** — Acting under Usman dan Fodio, the Fulani leader Modibo Adama launched a jihad into Fombina and established the Adamawa Emirate, spreading Islam and Fulani rule across the north as part of the Sokoto Caliphate. [modibo-adama-wiki, britannica-adamawa]
- c. 1895–1900s: **King Njoya devises the Bamum script** — King Ibrahim Njoya of Bamum and his palace circle created an original writing system, the Bamum script, and the Shumom language, used to record the kingdom's history, laws, and knowledge. [loc-bamum-njoya, britannica-bamum]

Figures:

- Modibo Adama (c. 1786–1847) — Founder of the Adamawa Emirate. Fulani scholar and military leader who, under the banner of Usman dan Fodio's jihad, conquered the region of Fombina from about 1809 and founded the Adamawa Emirate, the dominant power of northern Cameroon.
- Ibrahim Njoya (c. 1876–1933) — King (mfon) of Bamum. Scholar-king of Bamum who modernised his court, devised the original Bamum script and the Shumom language around the turn of the twentieth century, and built the celebrated palace at Foumban.

## German Kamerun — 1884 – 1916

A treaty with the Douala kings in 1884 opened three decades of German colonial rule, expansion into the interior, and forced labour, ended by Allied conquest in the First World War.

- 12 July 1884: **Douala treaty and German protectorate** — The Douala kings Bell and Akwa signed a treaty of protection with German agents; Gustav Nachtigal then proclaimed a German protectorate over Kamerun, beginning colonial rule. [history-cameroon-wiki, britannica-cameroon, statedept-cameroon]
- 1911: **France cedes Neukamerun** — Under the Treaty of Fez ending the Agadir crisis, France ceded the large territory of Neukamerun to Germany, expanding the colony eastward. [kamerun-wiki, history-cameroon-wiki]
- 1914–1916: **The Kamerun campaign** — British, French, and Belgian forces invaded German Kamerun from August 1914; after a prolonged campaign the last German garrison surrendered in early 1916. [kamerun-campaign-wiki, britannica-cameroon]

Figures:

- Gustav Nachtigal (1834–1885) — German explorer and imperial commissioner. Explorer who, acting for the German Empire, proclaimed the protectorate over Kamerun in July 1884 after treaties were signed with the Douala kings, inaugurating German colonial rule.
- Rudolf Duala Manga Bell (1873–1914) — Douala king and resistance leader. Paramount king of the Douala who protested German land expropriations around the estuary and was tried and hanged by the colonial authorities in 1914, becoming a symbol of anti-colonial resistance.

## Partition: French Cameroun and British Cameroons — 1916 – 1959

The League of Nations split the territory between France and Britain; under their mandates and later UN trusteeships, a nationalist movement rose, and the suppression of the UPC turned into open war.

- 1919–1922: **Partition into French and British mandates** — The League of Nations divided the former German colony between France, which took the larger French Cameroun, and Britain, which administered the Northern and Southern Cameroons alongside Nigeria. [britannica-cameroon, statedept-cameroon, history-cameroon-wiki]
- 1948: **Founding of the UPC** — Ruben Um Nyobe and others founded the Union des Populations du Cameroun, a nationalist party demanding immediate independence and the reunification of French and British Cameroon. [blackpast-umnyobe, britannica-cameroon]
- July 1955: **The UPC is banned** — After waves of unrest, the French administration outlawed the UPC, pushing the movement underground and into an armed insurgency. [cameroon-war-wiki, blackpast-umnyobe]
- 13 September 1958: **Killing of Ruben Um Nyobe** — The UPC leader Ruben Um Nyobe was tracked down and shot by a French-led military unit; repression of the insurgency continued in the Bassa and Bamileke regions with heavy loss of life. [blackpast-umnyobe, cameroon-war-wiki]

Figures:

- Ruben Um Nyobe (1913–1958) — Nationalist leader, founder of the UPC. Trade unionist and co-founder of the UPC who became the foremost voice for immediate independence and reunification; driven underground after the party's 1955 ban, he was killed by French-led forces in 1958.

## Independence, reunification and the Ahidjo years — 1960 – 1982

French Cameroun became independent on 1 January 1960; in 1961 the British Southern Cameroons voted to join it while the north chose Nigeria, forming a federation under Ahmadou Ahidjo that hardened into a one-party state.

- 1 January 1960: **Independence of French Cameroun** — The UN trusteeship administered by France ended and French Cameroun became an independent republic, with Ahmadou Ahidjo as its first president. [statedept-cameroon, britannica-ahidjo, britannica-cameroon]
- 11 February 1961: **The British Cameroons plebiscite** — In a UN-supervised vote the Northern Cameroons chose to join Nigeria while the Southern Cameroons chose to reunite with the Republic of Cameroon. [britcam-referendum-wiki, britannica-cameroon]
- 1 October 1961: **Reunification and federation** — The Southern Cameroons joined the republic to form the Federal Republic of Cameroon, uniting francophone East Cameroon and anglophone West Cameroon following the Foumban Conference. [britcam-referendum-wiki, britannica-cameroon, statedept-cameroon]
- 1972: **From federation to unitary state** — A referendum abolished the two-state federation in favour of a centralised United Republic of Cameroon, ending West Cameroon's separate autonomy. [britannica-cameroon, britannica-ahidjo]
- 4 November 1982: **Ahidjo resigns; Biya succeeds** — Ahmadou Ahidjo unexpectedly resigned the presidency and was succeeded by his prime minister, Paul Biya. [britannica-ahidjo, britannica-biya]

Figures:

- Ahmadou Ahidjo (1924–1989) — First President of Cameroon. Northern politician who led French Cameroun to independence in 1960 and oversaw reunification in 1961, then built a centralised one-party state before resigning in 1982; he died in exile in Senegal.
- John Ngu Foncha (1916–1999) — Anglophone leader and Prime Minister of Southern Cameroons. Leader who championed the Southern Cameroons' reunification with the republic and negotiated the federal terms at the Foumban Conference of 1961, later serving as vice-president of the federation.

## The Biya era and the Anglophone crisis — 1982 – present

Paul Biya has held power since 1982 through one-party rule and tightly managed multiparty elections, while grievances over anglophone marginalisation hardened into an armed separatist conflict from 2016.

- April 1984: **Failed coup against Biya** — An attempted coup by elements loyal to former president Ahidjo was suppressed within days, after which Biya tightened his hold on power. [britannica-biya, britannica-cameroon]
- 1990–1992: **Return to multiparty politics** — Biya legalised opposition parties in 1990 and held the first multiparty presidential election in 1992, which he won amid allegations of irregularities. [britannica-biya, britannica-cameroon]
- late 2016: **Anglophone protests begin** — Lawyers and teachers in the North-West and South-West regions launched protests against the marginalisation of English-speaking Cameroonians; a security crackdown followed. [crisisgroup-cameroon, globalr2p-cameroon]
- 2017: **Escalation into armed conflict** — The protests gave way to an armed insurgency as separatists proclaimed the independent state of Ambazonia; the conflict has since killed thousands and displaced hundreds of thousands. [globalr2p-cameroon, crisisgroup-cameroon]
- October 2025: **Biya re-elected at 92** — Paul Biya won a further presidential term, extending a tenure begun in 1982; opposition figures disputed the official results. [britannica-biya, crisisgroup-cameroon]

Figures:

- Paul Biya (born 1933) — President of Cameroon since 1982. Cameroon's second president, in power since 1982; he survived a 1984 coup attempt, presided over a managed transition to multiparty politics, removed term limits in 2008, and remains one of the world's longest-serving heads of state.
