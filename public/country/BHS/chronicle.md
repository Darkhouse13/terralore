Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 10 July 1973. The Commonwealth of The Bahamas became a sovereign state on 10 July 1973, ending more than three centuries of British colonial rule. Independence came under Prime Minister Lynden Pindling and his Progressive Liberal Party, which had carried the islands to majority rule in 1967 and to internal self-government, and it followed a constitutional conference in London in December 1972. The Bahamas chose to remain within the Commonwealth as a constitutional monarchy, retaining the British sovereign as head of state represented by a governor-general, with a Westminster-style parliament at Nassau. The new nation joined the United Nations the same year. Independence crowned the "Quiet Revolution" by which the Black majority, long excluded from power by a white merchant elite, took control of their own affairs without violence.

The Bahamas is an archipelago of some seven hundred low coral islands and cays strung across the western Atlantic between Florida and Hispaniola. Its recorded history opens dramatically: it was on a Bahamian island the Lucayan people called Guanahani — usually identified with San Salvador — that Christopher Columbus made his first landfall in the Americas on 12 October 1492, after which Spanish slaving emptied the islands of their original inhabitants within a few decades. Left largely abandoned, the Bahamas drew English settlers from 1648, became a notorious pirate haven until Woodes Rogers restored Crown control in 1718, and was transformed after 1783 by American Loyalists who brought enslaved Africans to short-lived cotton plantations. Out of emancipation in the 1830s grew a majority-Black society that won majority rule in 1967 under Lynden Pindling and independence from Britain on 10 July 1973, sustaining itself today on tourism and offshore finance while contending with the ever-present threat of hurricanes such as Dorian in 2019.

## The Lucayans and Columbus's landfall — c. 700 – 1520

A Taíno people, the Lucayans, settled the islands and met Columbus at Guanahani in 1492 — only to be carried off into Spanish slavery and gone within a generation.

- by c. 700 CE: **Lucayan settlement of the islands** — The Lucayans, a Taíno (Arawakan) people, migrated from Hispaniola and Cuba and settled the Bahamian archipelago, eventually numbering perhaps 40,000 across dozens of islands. [smithsonian-lucayans, loc-bahamas, britannica-history]
- 12 October 1492: **Columbus's first landfall at Guanahani** — Christopher Columbus made his first landfall in the Americas at the Lucayan island of Guanahani, which he renamed San Salvador and claimed for Castile, opening the European encounter with the New World. [natgeo-landfall, britannica-history, loc-bahamas]
- from c. 1509: **Spanish enslavement of the Lucayans** — Spanish raiders began systematically seizing Lucayans as forced labour for the mines and pearl fisheries of the Greater Antilles and Venezuela, devastating the islands' population. [smithsonian-lucayans, britannica-history]
- c. 1520: **Depopulation of the Bahamas** — By about 1520 enslavement and disease had all but exterminated the Lucayans; the islands were left effectively abandoned for more than a century. [smithsonian-lucayans, loc-bahamas, britannica-history]

## English settlers and the Republic of Pirates — 1648 – 1718

Puritan Eleutheran Adventurers brought the first lasting English settlement in 1648; by the 1700s Nassau had become a pirate haven, until Woodes Rogers seized it for the Crown in 1718.

- 1648: **The Eleutheran Adventurers settle Eleuthera** — English Puritans led by William Sayle left Bermuda seeking religious freedom and founded the first lasting English settlement in the Bahamas on the island they named Eleuthera ("freedom"). [loc-bahamas, britannica-history]
- 1670: **Grant to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina** — The Bahamas were granted to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina, though their rule proved weak and largely nominal, leaving the scattered islands poorly governed. [loc-bahamas, britannica-history]
- c. 1706–1718: **Nassau becomes a pirate haven** — Amid a collapse of royal authority, Nassau became the base of a loose pirate republic, home to Blackbeard, Charles Vane, Anne Bonny and others who preyed on Atlantic shipping. [britannica-history, loc-bahamas]
- 1718: **Woodes Rogers ends the pirate republic** — Royal governor Woodes Rogers arrived at Nassau with the King's Pardon, accepted the surrender of many pirates, drove out the rest and restored Crown authority under the motto "Pirates Expelled, Commerce Restored". [britannica-history, loc-bahamas]

Figures:

- Woodes Rogers (c. 1679–1732) — First royal Governor of the Bahamas. Former privateer and circumnavigator appointed governor in 1717, who arrived at Nassau in 1718 with the King's Pardon, broke the pirate republic and restored royal government; he served two terms as governor and gave the colony its motto of commerce restored.

## Loyalists, slavery and emancipation — 1718 – 1838

American Loyalists arrived after 1783 with thousands of enslaved Africans and tried to plant cotton; the experiment failed, and emancipation from the 1830s created a majority of African descent.

- 1783: **Spain cedes the Bahamas to Britain** — Under the Treaty of Paris of 1783 Spain ceded the Bahamas to Britain, securing the colony's future under the Crown after years of contest and a brief Spanish occupation. [britannica-history, loc-bahamas]
- from 1783: **American Loyalists and enslaved Africans arrive** — Loyalist refugees from the new United States emigrated to the Bahamas after the American Revolution, bringing thousands of enslaved Africans and roughly trebling the colony's population. [britannica-history, loc-bahamas]
- 1780s–1800s: **The failed cotton plantations** — Loyalists planted cotton on the "Cotton Islands", but exhausted soils and pests ruined the crop within a couple of decades, collapsing the plantation economy. [britannica-history]
- 1 August 1834: **Slavery Abolition Act takes effect** — The British Slavery Abolition Act came into force in the Bahamas on 1 August 1834, beginning emancipation; after an apprenticeship period, full freedom followed in 1838. [loc-bahamas, britannica-history]

## Wrecking, blockade-running and rum — 1838 – 1934

A poor colony living off salvage and sponging found sudden riches twice — running the Union blockade in the American Civil War, and smuggling liquor to Prohibition-era America.

- 19th century: **An economy of wrecking and sponging** — The poor post-emancipation colony lived largely on salvaging shipwrecks, sponge-fishing and salt, with wrecking for a time its single largest source of income. [loc-bahamas, britannica-history]
- 1861–1865: **Civil War blockade-running boom** — During the American Civil War, Nassau became the chief hub for running the Union blockade, shipping arms to the Confederacy and cotton out, and growing fabulously wealthy in the process. [historytoday-blockade, britannica-history]
- 1865: **Collapse of the blockade boom** — The defeat of the Confederacy in 1865 ended blockade-running overnight and plunged Nassau back into economic depression. [historytoday-blockade, britannica-history]
- 1920–1933: **Prohibition-era rum-running** — American Prohibition turned the Bahamas into a base for smuggling liquor across the Gulf Stream, enriching the colony until repeal in 1933 ended the trade. [britannica-history, loc-bahamas]

## Majority rule and independence — 1934 – 1973

Tourism revived the islands while a Black majority, long shut out by the "Bay Street Boys", organised through the PLP to win majority rule in 1967 and independence in 1973.

- 1953: **Founding of the Progressive Liberal Party** — Bahamians opposed to UBP and "Bay Street" dominance founded the Progressive Liberal Party, which became the vehicle of the movement for majority rule. [britannica-independence, statedept-bgn, blackpast-pindling]
- 7 January 1964: **Internal self-government** — A new constitution granted the Bahamas internal self-government with a cabinet and a first premier, while Britain retained defence and external affairs. [statedept-bgn, britannica-independence]
- 10 January 1967: **Majority rule — the Quiet Revolution** — Lynden Pindling's PLP gained power after the 1967 election, making him the first Black premier and ending the long political dominance of the white "Bay Street" elite. [britannica-pindling, blackpast-pindling, statedept-bgn]
- 10 July 1973: **Independence of The Bahamas** — The Commonwealth of The Bahamas became independent of the United Kingdom on 10 July 1973 under Prime Minister Lynden Pindling, remaining a Commonwealth realm and joining the United Nations. [britannica-independence, britannica-pindling, statedept-bgn]

Figures:

- Sir Lynden Pindling (1930–2000) — First Prime Minister of The Bahamas. Lawyer and leader of the Progressive Liberal Party who led the Bahamas to majority rule in 1967 as its first Black premier and to independence in 1973, then governed as prime minister until 1992; knighted in 1983, he is widely regarded as the "Father of the Nation".

## Tourism, finance and the age of hurricanes — 1973 – present

An independent Bahamas built a prosperous economy on tourism and offshore finance, alternated power peacefully between rival parties, and confronted catastrophic storms such as Hurricane Dorian in 2019.

- 1992: **First peaceful change of government** — Hubert Ingraham's Free National Movement defeated the PLP in 1992, ending Lynden Pindling's twenty-five years in power in the country's first democratic alternation of government. [britannica-independence, statedept-bgn]
- September 2019: **Hurricane Dorian devastates Abaco and Grand Bahama** — Category 5 Hurricane Dorian stalled over the northern Bahamas, killing scores, leaving hundreds missing and causing about US$3.4 billion in damage — roughly a quarter of GDP. [idb-dorian, cnn-dorian]
- September 2021: **Philip Davis and the PLP return to power** — Philip Davis led the Progressive Liberal Party to victory in the 2021 general election, becoming prime minister, in another peaceful transfer of power. [bahamasgov-davis]

Figures:

- Hubert Ingraham (born 1947) — Prime Minister of The Bahamas (1992–2002, 2007–2012). Lawyer and Free National Movement leader who in 1992 ended Lynden Pindling's twenty-five-year rule in the first democratic change of government, and served two further terms as prime minister, presiding over continued growth in tourism and finance.
