# The Chronicle of Greece: Birthplace of democracy and philosophy, reborn as a modern nation through the War of Independence.

Founding: Greek War of Independence and recognition of an independent Greece — 1821 (uprising); recognized 1830 / 1832. The modern Greek state traces its political founding to the War of Independence, which broke out against Ottoman rule in 1821 (conventionally dated to 25 March 1821). After years of fighting and the intervention of Britain, France and Russia, the Great Powers recognized Greece as an independent state in the London Protocol of 3 February 1830, and the Ottoman sultan formally acknowledged Greek independence and the new kingdom's frontiers in the Treaty of Constantinople of July 1832. The roots of Greek civic identity run far deeper: the Athenian statesman Cleisthenes founded what is regarded as the world's first democracy around 508/507 BCE, an inheritance the founders of the modern nation consciously claimed.

Greece's history stretches from the Bronze Age palaces of Minoan Crete and Mycenaean kings, through the Archaic and Classical city-states that gave the world democracy, drama and philosophy, to the world-spanning Hellenistic empire of Alexander the Great. Absorbed by Rome and reborn as the Greek-speaking heart of the Byzantine Empire, the Greek lands then passed under nearly four centuries of Ottoman rule. The modern Greek state was forged in the War of Independence that began in 1821; the Great Powers recognized an independent Greece by the London Protocol of February 1830, and the Ottoman sultan confirmed it by the Treaty of Constantinople in 1832.

## Minoan, Mycenaean and the Bronze Age Aegean — c. 3000 BCE – c. 1100 BCE

On Crete and the mainland, the first European palace civilizations rose, wrote in scripts, traded across the Mediterranean, and then collapsed into a dark age.

- c. 2000–1450 BCE: **Rise of the Minoan palaces on Crete** — The Minoan civilization built great palace complexes at Knossos and elsewhere on Crete, Europe's first advanced civilization, sustained by trade and a sophisticated artistic and administrative culture. [whe-minoan, britannica-minoan]
- c. 1600 BCE: **Flourishing of Mycenaean civilization** — Fortified palace-citadels such as Mycenae and Pylos rose on the mainland, ruled by warrior elites who were the first attested Greek-speakers and traded across the eastern Mediterranean. [whe-mycenaean, britannica-mycenaean]
- c. 1450 BCE: **Linear B and Mycenaean control of Crete** — Mycenaeans adapted Minoan script into Linear B to write an early form of Greek and extended their authority to Knossos, as Minoan power on Crete waned. [whe-mycenaean, britannica-minoan]
- c. 1200–1100 BCE: **Bronze Age collapse and the Greek Dark Ages** — The Mycenaean palaces were destroyed and writing was lost in the wider Bronze Age collapse, ushering in centuries of decline known as the Greek Dark Ages. [whe-bronze-age-aegean, britannica-mycenaean]

Figures:

- Sir Arthur Evans (1851 – 1941) — Archaeologist who excavated Knossos. British archaeologist who excavated the palace of Knossos from 1900, naming the 'Minoan' civilization after the legendary King Minos and bringing Bronze Age Crete to the modern world.

## Archaic and Classical Greece — c. 800 BCE – 323 BCE

In a world of independent city-states, Greeks invented democracy, repelled the Persian Empire, and created the philosophy, drama and art at the root of Western civilization.

- 776 BCE (traditional): **Traditional first Olympic Games** — Greek tradition dated the first Olympic Games to 776 BCE at Olympia, a panhellenic religious festival that became a shared marker of Greek identity and a basis for dating. [whe-ancient-greece]
- c. 508/507 BCE: **Cleisthenes founds Athenian democracy** — The Athenian statesman Cleisthenes reorganized the citizens into ten tribes and established popular institutions of self-rule, the system later called democracy and regarded as the first in the world. [britannica-cleisthenes, history-greek-democracy, ebsco-cleisthenes]
- 490 BCE: **Battle of Marathon** — Athenian hoplites defeated the first Persian invasion on the plain of Marathon, a landmark victory that became central to Athenian self-confidence and identity. [britannica-marathon, whe-marathon]
- 480 BCE: **Battle of Salamis** — A Greek coalition fleet destroyed the navy of the Persian king Xerxes in the straits of Salamis, the decisive naval victory of the second Persian invasion; victory at Plataea in 479 BCE ended the threat. [britannica-marathon, whe-ancient-greece]
- 431–404 BCE: **The Peloponnesian War** — A protracted war between Athens and Sparta and their allies ended in Athens' defeat, exhausting the leading city-states and chronicled by the historian Thucydides. [whe-ancient-greece]

Figures:

- Cleisthenes (c. 570 – c. 508 BCE) — Athenian reformer, 'father of democracy'. Athenian statesman who reformed the constitution around 508/507 BCE, reorganizing the citizen body and creating institutions of popular government that historians regard as the first democracy.
- Pericles (c. 495 – 429 BCE) — Leading Athenian statesman of the golden age. The dominant politician of democratic Athens at its height, Pericles championed the empire and the building of the Parthenon, giving his name to the cultural golden age of 5th-century Athens.

## The Hellenistic Age: Alexander and His Successors — 338 BCE – 31/30 BCE

Macedon united Greece by force, and Alexander the Great carried Greek culture from Egypt to India, creating a vast cosmopolitan world that endured until Rome.

- 338 BCE: **Battle of Chaeronea** — Philip II of Macedon defeated an alliance of Greek city-states led by Athens and Thebes, ending the era of fully independent poleis and establishing Macedonian dominance over Greece. [britannica-chaeronea, whe-chaeronea]
- 334 BCE: **Alexander invades the Persian Empire** — Alexander the Great crossed into Asia and began the campaign that, over a decade, toppled the Persian Empire and carried Greek arms and culture as far as India. [britannica-alexander, whe-alexander]
- 331 BCE: **Foundation of Alexandria** — Alexander founded Alexandria in Egypt, which became the greatest city of the Hellenistic world and a centre of Greek learning, home to its famous Library and Museum. [whe-alexander]
- 323 BCE: **Death of Alexander and the Hellenistic kingdoms** — Alexander died at Babylon, and his generals divided his empire into the Ptolemaic, Seleucid and Antigonid kingdoms, beginning the Hellenistic age of Greek-ruled states across the East. [britannica-alexander, britannica-hellenistic]

Figures:

- Philip II of Macedon (382 – 336 BCE) — King of Macedon, unifier of Greece. King who built Macedon into the dominant Greek power, reforming its army and defeating the southern city-states at Chaeronea in 338 BCE before his assassination left his conquests to his son Alexander.
- Alexander the Great (356 – 323 BCE) — King of Macedon and conqueror (r. 336–323 BCE). One of history's greatest conquerors, Alexander overthrew the Persian Empire and extended Greek rule from Egypt to India, spreading Hellenistic culture across the ancient world before dying at 32.

## Roman and Byzantine Greece — 146 BCE – 1453 CE

Conquered by Rome yet conquering it culturally, the Greek world became the heart of the long-lived, Christian, Greek-speaking Byzantine Empire centred on Constantinople.

- 146 BCE: **Rome sacks Corinth and conquers Greece** — The Roman general Lucius Mummius destroyed Corinth and crushed the Achaean League, bringing Greece under Roman domination; it was later organized as the province of Achaea. [whe-roman-greece, britannica-greece]
- 330 CE: **Foundation of Constantinople** — Constantine refounded Byzantium as Constantinople, the new capital that shifted the Roman Empire's centre eastward and became the heart of the Greek-speaking Byzantine world. [britannica-byzantine, britannica-greece]
- 1204 CE: **Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople** — Crusaders captured and plundered Constantinople, fragmenting Byzantine territory, including parts of Greece, among Latin and Greek rulers and gravely weakening the empire. [britannica-byzantine, britannica-greece]
- 29 May 1453: **Fall of Constantinople** — Ottoman forces under Sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire and clearing the way for Ottoman rule over the Greek lands. [britannica-fall-constantinople, britannica-byzantine]

Figures:

- Constantine the Great (c. 272 – 337 CE) — Roman emperor, founder of Constantinople. Emperor who legalized Christianity and in 330 CE founded Constantinople as a new imperial capital, shifting the Roman world eastward and laying the foundation of the Byzantine Empire.

## Ottoman Rule and the War of Independence — 1453 – 1832

Under nearly four centuries of Ottoman rule, the Greek Orthodox Church preserved Greek identity, until a revolution in 1821 won a recognized, independent Greek state.

- 1460: **Ottoman conquest of the Morea** — The Ottomans completed their conquest of the Greek mainland with the fall of the Byzantine Despotate of the Morea, including Mystras, bringing the Greek lands under Ottoman rule. [britannica-greece-ottoman, wiki-ottoman-morea]
- 25 March 1821 (traditional): **Outbreak of the Greek War of Independence** — Revolution against Ottoman rule erupted across the Peloponnese and beyond, beginning the war that would create an independent Greek state and inspiring philhellenes across Europe. [britannica-war-independence, ebsco-greek-independence]
- 20 October 1827: **Battle of Navarino** — A combined British, French and Russian fleet destroyed the Ottoman-Egyptian navy at Navarino, a decisive intervention by the Great Powers that turned the war in Greece's favour. [britannica-war-independence, ebsco-greek-independence]
- 3 February 1830: **London Protocol recognizes Greek independence** — Britain, France and Russia signed the London Protocol declaring Greece an independent state, the first international act recognizing Greek sovereignty. [greeknewsagenda-london-protocol, ebsco-greek-independence]
- July 1832: **Treaty of Constantinople and the Kingdom of Greece** — The Ottoman sultan recognized Greek independence and the new state's frontiers in the Treaty of Constantinople; the Bavarian prince Otto was chosen as the first king of Greece. [britannica-war-independence, britannica-otto]

Figures:

- Theodoros Kolokotronis (1770 – 1843) — Military leader of the Greek War of Independence. The most celebrated general of the Greek revolution, whose victories in the Peloponnese, notably against Ottoman armies, made him a national hero of the War of Independence.
- Otto of Greece (1815 – 1867) — First king of modern Greece (r. 1832–1862). Bavarian prince selected by the Great Powers as the first king of the new Greek kingdom; he reached Greece in 1833 and ruled until he was deposed in 1862.

## The Modern Greek State — 1832 – present

From a small kingdom the modern nation expanded, endured catastrophe in Asia Minor, occupation and civil war, then became a stable democracy at the heart of Europe.

- 1919–1922: **Asia Minor Catastrophe** — The Greco-Turkish War ended in the rout of Greek forces and the destruction of Smyrna in 1922; the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne mandated a population exchange that brought over a million refugees into Greece. [britannica-greece, globalsecurity-greco-turkish]
- 1941–1944: **Axis occupation of Greece** — After resisting Italy in 1940, Greece was occupied by Germany, Italy and Bulgaria from 1941 to 1944, enduring a devastating famine and the deaths of hundreds of thousands of civilians. [wiki-axis-occupation, britannica-greece]
- 1946–1949: **Greek Civil War** — Following liberation, a civil war between communist and government forces tore the country apart until 1949, an early conflict of the Cold War that left lasting political divisions. [ww2museum-greek-civil-war, britannica-greece]
- 8 December 1974: **Fall of the junta and the Third Hellenic Republic** — After the collapse of the 1967–1974 military dictatorship, a referendum abolished the monarchy and established the current Greek parliamentary republic. [britannica-greece, bbc-greece-timeline]
- 1981: **Greece joins the European Communities** — Greece became a member of the European Communities (later the European Union), anchoring its post-war democracy in European integration; it adopted the euro in 2001. [britannica-greece, bbc-greece-timeline]

Figures:

- Eleftherios Venizelos (1864 – 1936) — Statesman and seven-time Prime Minister. The dominant Greek statesman of the early 20th century, who led territorial expansion in the Balkan Wars and modernization at home, and whose career was bound up with the era of the Great Idea and the Asia Minor campaign.
- Konstantinos Karamanlis (1907 – 1998) — Prime Minister and President of the Republic. Statesman who led Greece's return to democracy after the fall of the junta in 1974, oversaw the abolition of the monarchy, and guided the country into the European Communities.
