Founding: Independence from France — 15 August 1960. The Republic of the Congo became an independent state on 15 August 1960, ending its status as the colony of Middle Congo within French Equatorial Africa. The Abbé Fulbert Youlou, a Catholic priest turned nationalist politician who had led the territory's government in the final colonial years, became the new republic's first president. Independence followed a long history: the Bantu settlement of the region; the Vili Kingdom of Loango and the inland Teke kingdom; the Atlantic slave trade through Loango's coast; Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza's treaty with the Teke ruler Makoko and his 1880 founding of Brazzaville; and the colonial era of forced labour and the Congo-Ocean Railway. Brazzaville, the colonial federal capital of French Equatorial Africa and wartime seat of Free France, became the capital of the new state. The country kept close ties to France but, within a few years of independence, would turn sharply toward the political left.

The Republic of the Congo — Congo-Brazzaville, distinct from the larger Democratic Republic of the Congo to its south and east — lies on the Atlantic coast of Central Africa, straddling the equator and drained by the Congo and Ubangi river systems. Its territory was settled in the first millennium CE by Bantu-speaking peoples, and from around the sixteenth century its coast was dominated by the Kingdom of Loango, a Vili state whose ports became major channels of the Atlantic slave and ivory trades; the northern reaches of the Kongo and the inland Teke (Anziku or Tio) kingdom also fell partly within the modern country. In 1880 the Italian-born French explorer Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza signed a treaty with the Teke ruler Makoko and founded a post at Nkuna on the north bank of the Congo River, the future Brazzaville. The territory became the colony of Middle Congo (Moyen-Congo) and, from 1910, part of French Equatorial Africa, with Brazzaville as the federal capital; during the Second World War the city served as the capital of Free France, hosting the 1944 Brazzaville Conference. Colonial rule rested heavily on forced labour, most notoriously in building the Congo-Ocean Railway (1921–1934), which cost the lives of many thousands of conscripted workers. The country became independent on 15 August 1960 under Fulbert Youlou. After his fall in 1963 it moved leftward, and from 1969 to 1991 it was the People's Republic of the Congo, a one-party Marxist-Leninist state led by Marien Ngouabi and, from 1979, Denis Sassou Nguesso. A 1991 national conference opened a brief democratic period under Pascal Lissouba, but a civil war in 1997 returned Sassou Nguesso to power, and he has ruled since.

## Bantu peoples, Loango, and the Teke kingdom — 1st millennium CE – c. 1600

Bantu-speaking farmers settled the equatorial forests and savannas; on the coast the Vili built the Kingdom of Loango, while inland the Teke (Anziku) kingdom and the northern reaches of Kongo took shape.

- 1st millennium CE: **Bantu peoples settle the region** — Bantu-speaking farmers and ironworkers settled the forests and savannas of the modern Congo over the first millennium CE, founding the Kongo, Teke, Vili, Mboshi and other communities that make up the country's peoples. [globalsecurity-cog-early, britannica-rep-congo, history-rep-congo-wiki]
- c. 16th century: **Rise of the Kingdom of Loango** — The Vili built the Kingdom of Loango on the Atlantic coast, with its capital at Buali; ruled by the maloango, it became a producer of cloth and a centre of trade in copper, ivory and enslaved people. [loango-wiki, britannica-loango, africanhistoryextra-loango]
- c. 15th–16th centuries: **The Teke (Anziku) kingdom inland** — North of the Congo River the Teke (Bateke), organised as the Anziku or Tio kingdom under a sacred king titled the makoko, controlled the interior plateaus and trade routes; the northern reaches of Kongo also touched the region. [globalsecurity-cog-early, history-rep-congo-wiki, iloo-wiki]

## Loango ports and the Atlantic slave trade — c. 1600 – 1880

Loango's coast became one of the great markets of the Atlantic trade, exporting ivory, copper and tens of thousands of enslaved people; the kingdom declined through the nineteenth century as the slave trade was suppressed.

- 17th–18th centuries: **Loango becomes a hub of the Atlantic trade** — The Loango coast, with markets at Loango Bay, Malemba and Cabinda, grew into a major export point for ivory, copper and enslaved people; Vili middlemen linked interior caravan networks to European ships offshore. [britannica-loango, loango-wiki, vili-wiki]
- 18th century: **Peak of the slave trade through Loango** — By the eighteenth century the Loango ports were embarking large numbers of enslaved Africans each year, bound mainly for the Caribbean and the Americas, drawing the coastal kingdoms ever deeper into the Atlantic economy. [britannica-loango, loango-wiki, vili-wiki]
- late 18th century: **Succession crisis weakens Loango** — A succession crisis after the death of the king recorded as Buatu, together with the growing power of provincial governors and traders, undermined the maloango's central authority and began Loango's long decline. [loango-wiki, africanhistoryextra-loango]
- 19th century: **Decline as the slave trade is suppressed** — The European suppression of the Atlantic slave trade undercut the Loango coast's economy; the kingdom fragmented into small chieftaincies and had effectively ceased to exist as a unified state by the early 1880s. [britannica-loango, loango-wiki, globalsecurity-cog-early]

## Brazza, Brazzaville, and the colony of Middle Congo — 1875 – 1910

Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza signed a treaty with the Teke ruler Makoko and founded a post at Nkuna in 1880, the future Brazzaville; the territory became the colony of Middle Congo, folded in 1910 into French Equatorial Africa.

- September 1880: **Brazza's treaty with Makoko** — In September 1880 Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza signed a treaty with the Teke ruler Makoko Iloo I, placing his lands under French protection and securing France's claim to the north bank of the Congo River. [brazza-wiki, globalsecurity-brazza, iloo-wiki]
- 10 September 1880: **Founding of Brazzaville at Nkuna** — Brazza founded a French post at the village of Nkuna (Mfoa) on the north bank of Stanley Pool on 10 September 1880; held by Sergeant Malamine Camara, it was named Brazzaville and became the centre of French rule. [brazzaville-wiki, blackpast-brazzaville, malamine-wiki]
- 1884–1885: **Berlin Conference divides the two Congos** — The Berlin Conference recognised French claims north of the Congo River and assigned the south bank to Leopold II's Congo Free State, fixing the lasting division between the Republic of the Congo and the future Democratic Republic. [britannica-rep-congo, history-rep-congo-wiki]
- 1910: **Middle Congo joins French Equatorial Africa** — France reorganised its Central African colonies in 1910 into French Equatorial Africa — Gabon, Middle Congo, Ubangi-Shari and Chad — governed from Brazzaville, which became the federation's federal capital. [fea-wiki, britannica-rep-congo, globalsecurity-moyen-congo]

Figures:

- Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza (1852–1905) — Explorer and founder of Brazzaville. Italian-born French explorer whose 1880 treaty with the Teke ruler Makoko secured the north bank of the Congo for France; founder of the post at Nkuna that became Brazzaville and namesake of the city and the country's capital.

## French Equatorial Africa, forced labour, and the Congo-Ocean Railway — 1910 – 1958

Brazzaville governed French Equatorial Africa and, from 1940, served as the capital of Free France; the colonial economy rested on forced labour, above all on the Congo-Ocean Railway, which cost many thousands of lives.

- 1921–1934: **Building the Congo-Ocean Railway** — The Congo-Ocean Railway was built from 1921 to 1934 by the Société de Construction des Batignolles to link Brazzaville to Pointe-Noire; forced labourers conscripted across French Equatorial Africa died in the thousands, an estimate commonly placed above 17,000. [congo-ocean-wiki, stanford-congo-ocean, cambridge-forced-labour]
- 1934: **Railway completed; Pointe-Noire opens as port** — The roughly 512-kilometre railway was completed in 1934, establishing Pointe-Noire as the colony's Atlantic ocean terminal and the backbone of the export economy that bypassed the lower Congo rapids. [congo-ocean-wiki, pointe-noire-station-wiki]
- 1940: **Brazzaville, capital of Free France** — After French Equatorial Africa rallied to General de Gaulle in 1940, Brazzaville became the effective capital of Free France, a base for organising resistance against Vichy and the Axis powers. [fea-wiki, brazzaville-conference-blackpast, britannica-rep-congo]
- 30 January – 8 February 1944: **The Brazzaville Conference** — De Gaulle convened the Brazzaville Conference in early 1944, which promised post-war colonial reforms — ending the harshest forced labour, granting African representation and citizenship — while explicitly rejecting independence. [brazzaville-conference-wiki, brazzaville-conference-blackpast]
- 1958: **Autonomy within the French Community** — Following the September 1958 referendum, Middle Congo became the autonomous Republic of the Congo within the French Community, a self-governing step short of full sovereignty. [britannica-rep-congo, history-rep-congo-wiki]

## Independence, Youlou, and the Marxist People's Republic — 1960 – 1991

Independent in 1960 under Fulbert Youlou, the Congo turned sharply left after his 1963 fall; from 1969 to 1991 it was the People's Republic of the Congo, a Marxist-Leninist state led by Marien Ngouabi and then Denis Sassou Nguesso.

- 15 August 1960: **Independence under Fulbert Youlou** — The Republic of the Congo became independent on 15 August 1960 with the Abbé Fulbert Youlou as its first president, a pro-Western leader who kept close ties to France. [youlou-wiki, britannica-rep-congo, aaregistry-congo-independence]
- August 1963: **The Trois Glorieuses topple Youlou** — A three-day popular uprising in August 1963, led by trade unions and youth, forced Youlou's resignation; Alphonse Massamba-Débat took power and steered the country toward one-party "scientific socialism." [youlou-wiki, britannica-rep-congo, uca-congo-1960]
- 1969: **People's Republic of the Congo proclaimed** — Marien Ngouabi, who had seized power in 1968, proclaimed the People's Republic of the Congo in 1969 — Africa's first Marxist-Leninist state — with the Congolese Labour Party (PCT) as the sole legal party. [ngouabi-britannica, britannica-rep-congo, uca-congo-1960]
- 18 March 1977: **Assassination of Marien Ngouabi** — President Marien Ngouabi was assassinated in Brazzaville on 18 March 1977; a military committee took power, and after a brief interim under General Yhombi-Opango leadership passed to Denis Sassou Nguesso in 1979. [ngouabi-wiki, ngouabi-britannica, sassou-wiki]
- 1979: **Sassou Nguesso takes power** — Colonel Denis Sassou Nguesso became president in 1979, leading the PCT one-party state through the 1980s while petroleum became the mainstay of the economy and ties to France and Western oil firms deepened. [sassou-wiki, sassou-britannica, britannica-rep-congo]
- 1991: **National conference opens democracy** — After the PCT dropped Marxism-Leninism in 1990, a sovereign national conference in 1991 stripped Sassou Nguesso of executive power, made him a ceremonial head of state and set the Congo on a path to multiparty elections. [britannica-rep-congo, sassou-wiki, uca-congo-1960]

Figures:

- Fulbert Youlou (1917–1972) — First president of the Congo, 1960–1963. A Catholic priest turned nationalist politician, Youlou led Middle Congo to independence in 1960 and became the republic's first president; pro-Western and anti-communist, he was forced out by the Trois Glorieuses uprising in August 1963.
- Marien Ngouabi (1938–1977) — President and founder of the People's Republic, 1969–1977. Army officer who seized power in 1968 and in 1969 proclaimed the People's Republic of the Congo, Africa's first Marxist-Leninist state, under the PCT; he was assassinated in Brazzaville in March 1977.

## Democracy, civil war, and the return of Sassou Nguesso — 1992 – present

Multiparty elections in 1992 brought Pascal Lissouba to power, but militia conflict culminated in the 1997 civil war, which returned Denis Sassou Nguesso to the presidency — a position he has held since.

- August 1992: **Lissouba wins multiparty election** — Pascal Lissouba of UPADS won the Congo's first multiparty presidential election in August 1992, ending the PCT's monopoly and inaugurating a brief democratic period. [lissouba-wiki, britannica-rep-congo, uca-congo-1960]
- 1993–1994: **Militia clashes over disputed elections** — Disputes over the 1993 legislative elections sparked armed clashes between political militias in Brazzaville, fracturing the new democracy along factional and regional lines. [congo-civilwar-1993-wiki, uca-congo-1960]
- June–October 1997: **Civil war returns Sassou Nguesso to power** — Civil war erupted in June 1997 between forces of President Lissouba and former president Sassou Nguesso; backed by Angolan troops, Sassou Nguesso's forces prevailed by October, Lissouba fled into exile, and Sassou Nguesso declared himself president. [sassou-wiki, sassou-britannica, state-1997-congo]
- 2002: **New constitution; Sassou Nguesso elected** — A new constitution adopted in 2002 entrenched Sassou Nguesso's presidency; he won the presidential election that year and was re-elected in 2009 amid opposition complaints. [sassou-wiki, sassou-britannica]
- 25 October 2015: **Referendum removes term limits** — A constitutional referendum in October 2015, reported as overwhelmingly approved on a disputed turnout, scrapped presidential term limits and the age ceiling, clearing the way for Sassou Nguesso to seek further terms; he was re-elected in 2016 and 2021. [referendum-2015-wiki, loc-congo-referendum, sassou-wiki]

Figures:

- Pascal Lissouba (1931–2020) — President of the Congo, 1992–1997. Agronomist and politician of UPADS who won the Congo's first multiparty presidential election in 1992; his presidency collapsed in the 1997 civil war, after which he went into exile.
- Denis Sassou Nguesso (born 1943) — President of the Congo, 1979–1992 and since 1997. Army officer who led the Marxist People's Republic from 1979, lost power at the 1991 national conference, and returned through the 1997 civil war; he has ruled since, among the longest-serving leaders in Africa.
