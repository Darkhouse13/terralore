Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 26 May 1966. Guyana became an independent state within the Commonwealth at midnight on 26 May 1966, when British Guiana shed its colonial name and took the name Guyana, with Forbes Burnham as its first prime minister and Cheddi Jagan as leader of the opposition. The United States recognised the new nation the same day and opened its embassy at Georgetown. On 23 February 1970 — the anniversary of the 1763 Berbice slave rebellion — Guyana was proclaimed a cooperative republic, severing the last constitutional ties to the British crown while remaining in the Commonwealth.

Guyana, the 'land of many waters' on the north-eastern shoulder of South America, was home to Arawak, Carib and Warrau peoples long before Dutch traders planted the colonies of Essequibo, Berbice and Demerara and engineered a coastal world of dams, dykes and sluice-gates below the high-tide line. A sugar economy built on enslaved Africans gave rise to the great Berbice rebellion of 1763 under Cuffy, today the nation's first national hero, and after emancipation in 1838 planters imported indentured labourers from India, Portugal and China, producing Guyana's distinctive Indo- and Afro-Guyanese society. The three colonies, ceded to Britain and united as British Guiana in 1831, moved through the rivalry of Cheddi Jagan and Forbes Burnham to independence on 26 May 1966 and a cooperative republic in 1970. Since the first major offshore oil discovery in 2015, Guyana has become one of the world's fastest-growing economies, while the long-running Essequibo territorial controversy with Venezuela is now before the International Court of Justice.

## The First Peoples and the Land of Many Waters — Pre-1600

Long before any European sail appeared off the Wild Coast, the rivers, rainforests and table-top highlands of present-day Guyana were home to Amerindian peoples — Arawak, Carib and Warrau — whose worlds gave the country its name and its first place-names.

- 1st millennium BCE: **Amerindian settlement of the Guianas** — Arawak, Carib and Warrau peoples settle the rivers, coast and highlands of present-day Guyana, developing cassava farming, fishing and riverine trade. [britannica-guyana-history, loc-guyana]
- 16th century: **The Wild Coast and El Dorado** — Spanish and other navigators probe the rivers of the 'Wild Coast' in search of the golden kingdom of El Dorado, but find no empire to conquer. [britannica-guyana-history]

## The Dutch Colonies and the Engineered Coast — c. 1580 - 1814

Dutch traders and planters created three colonies — Essequibo, Berbice and Demerara — and built an extraordinary coastal landscape of dams, dykes, canals and sluice-gates that wrested sugar lands from the sea, all worked by enslaved Africans.

- c. 1616: **Founding of Essequibo** — The Dutch establish the colony of Essequibo, the oldest of the three, followed by Berbice (1627) and Demerara (split from Essequibo in 1745). [britannica-guyana-history, history-state-guyana]
- 17th-18th c.: **The engineered sugar coast** — Dutch planters drain the coastal plain with polders, dams, canals and koker sluice-gates, building a below-sea-level sugar landscape worked by enslaved Africans. [britannica-guyana-history, loc-guyana]
- Feb 1763: **The Berbice slave rebellion** — Enslaved Africans led by Cuffy rise in Berbice, holding much of the colony for almost a year in one of the largest slave revolts in the Americas before being suppressed. [britannica-guyana-history, ntg-1763]
- 1781: **Founding of Stabroek (Georgetown)** — A British occupying force lays out the town of Stabroek at the mouth of the Demerara River; renamed Georgetown in 1812, it becomes the colony's capital. [britannica-georgetown]

Figures:

- Cuffy (Kofi) (died 1763) — Leader of the 1763 Berbice rebellion. Akan-born enslaved man who led the great Berbice rebellion of 1763, declared himself governor of Berbice, and held much of the colony for nearly a year. Guyana's first national hero, he is commemorated by the 1763 Monument in Georgetown and on Republic Day, 23 February.

## British Guiana: Emancipation and Indenture — 1814 - 1928

United as British Guiana in 1831, the sugar colony passed from slavery to freedom through the Demerara rising of 1823 and emancipation in 1838, after which planters imported indentured labourers from India, Portugal and China — peopling the multi-ethnic society of modern Guyana.

- Aug 1823: **The Demerara rising** — Thousands of enslaved people in Demerara rebel, believing emancipation has been withheld; the revolt is brutally suppressed, and the death of missionary John Smith galvanises British abolitionists. [britannica-guyana-history, loc-guyana]
- 1831: **Creation of British Guiana** — Britain unites the colonies of Essequibo, Demerara and Berbice into a single Crown colony, British Guiana, with Georgetown as its capital. [history-state-guyana, britannica-guyana-history]
- 1838: **Emancipation and Indian indenture** — Full emancipation takes effect on 1 August 1838; the first indentured labourers from India arrive on 5 May 1838, beginning decades of immigration that reshape the colony's population. [britannica-guyana-history, loc-guyana]
- 1917: **End of Indian indenture** — Indentured immigration from India is brought to a close after some 250,000 people have come to British Guiana, leaving Indo-Guyanese the colony's largest ethnic group. [loc-guyana, britannica-guyana-history]

## Jagan, Burnham and the Road to Independence — 1928 - 1970

The mid-twentieth century brought mass politics, the rivalry of Cheddi Jagan and Forbes Burnham, Cold War intervention, and a split along ethnic lines that carried British Guiana to independence in 1966 and a cooperative republic in 1970.

- 1953: **PPP victory and suspended constitution** — The People's Progressive Party wins the first elections under universal suffrage and Jagan becomes chief minister, but Britain suspends the constitution and sends troops after 133 days. [britannica-jagan, loc-guyana]
- 1955-1957: **The PPP splits along ethnic lines** — Forbes Burnham breaks with Jagan and forms the People's National Congress; the two parties harden into Afro- and Indo-Guyanese blocs that dominate national politics. [britannica-guyana-independence, britannica-burnham]
- 26 May 1966: **Independence of Guyana** — British Guiana becomes the independent nation of Guyana within the Commonwealth, with Forbes Burnham as first prime minister and Cheddi Jagan as opposition leader. [britannica-guyana-independence, history-state-guyana]
- 23 Feb 1970: **The Cooperative Republic** — Guyana is proclaimed a cooperative republic, marking the date of Cuffy's 1763 rebellion as Republic Day while remaining within the Commonwealth. [britannica-guyana-independence, history-state-guyana]

Figures:

- Cheddi Jagan (1918-1997) — Premier and later President of Guyana. American-trained dentist and Marxist leader of the People's Progressive Party, the first popularly elected premier of British Guiana and the foremost Indo-Guyanese statesman. Kept from power for decades, he was finally elected president in 1992 and is widely regarded as a father of the nation.
- Forbes Burnham (1923-1985) — First Prime Minister and President of Guyana. London-trained barrister and Afro-Guyanese leader of the People's National Congress who led the colony to independence in 1966 and ruled until his death in 1985, declaring the Cooperative Republic and nationalising the economy.

## The Burnham Era and the Shadow of Jonestown — 1970 - 1992

Under Forbes Burnham the cooperative republic swung left and nationalised the commanding heights of the economy, while the country drew the world's horrified attention in 1978 as the site of the Jonestown tragedy.

- 1971-1976: **Nationalisation of the economy** — Burnham's government nationalises the foreign-owned bauxite and sugar industries, bringing most of the economy under state control in a 'cooperative socialist' programme. [britannica-burnham, britannica-guyana-independence]
- 18 Nov 1978: **The Jonestown tragedy** — At the Peoples Temple settlement of Jonestown, more than 900 followers of the American preacher Jim Jones die in a mass murder-suicide after the killing of a visiting U.S. congressman. [britannica-jonestown]
- 1985: **Death of Burnham; Hoyte succeeds** — Forbes Burnham dies in office and is succeeded by Desmond Hoyte, who begins to liberalise the economy and ease political controls. [britannica-burnham, loc-guyana]
- Oct 1992: **Return of Jagan and free elections** — Internationally observed elections return Cheddi Jagan and the PPP to power after 28 years, restoring multiparty democracy; his widow Janet Jagan later becomes Guyana's first woman president. [britannica-jagan, britannica-janet-jagan]

Figures:

- Janet Jagan (1920-2009) — President of Guyana. American-born activist and co-founder of the People's Progressive Party who, after a lifetime in Guyanese politics alongside her husband Cheddi Jagan, was elected president in 1997 — the first woman to lead Guyana and the first U.S.-born woman elected to lead any nation.

## Oil, the Essequibo Controversy and the Present — 1992 - present

A poor, debt-burdened nation at the turn of the century, Guyana has been transformed since 2015 by one of the world's great offshore oil discoveries, even as the century-old Essequibo territorial controversy with Venezuela reaches the International Court of Justice.

- 2015: **First offshore oil discovery** — ExxonMobil and partners make the first major oil discovery in the offshore Stabroek block, beginning Guyana's transformation into a significant petroleum producer. [eia-guyana, exxon-guyana]
- 2018: **Guyana takes Essequibo case to the ICJ** — Guyana files proceedings at the International Court of Justice seeking to confirm the 1899 arbitral award fixing its boundary with Venezuela. [icj-guyana-venezuela]
- Dec 2019: **First oil production** — Production begins in the Stabroek block; within a few years Guyana exceeds 600,000 barrels a day and becomes one of the world's fastest-growing economies. [eia-guyana]
- May 2026: **ICJ hearings on the merits** — Public hearings on the merits of Arbitral Award of 3 October 1899 (Guyana v. Venezuela) are held at the International Court of Justice, a decisive stage in the long-running Essequibo controversy. [icj-guyana-venezuela]
