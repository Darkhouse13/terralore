# The Chronicle of Lebanon: From the Phoenician harbours of Tyre, Sidon and Byblos to the confessional republic of the cedars.

Founding: Independence from France — 22 November 1943. Modern Lebanon dates its sovereignty to the autumn of 1943. After elections, the new government of President Bechara El Khoury and Prime Minister Riad al-Solh amended the constitution to remove the articles enshrining the French Mandate. The French authorities responded on 11 November 1943 by arresting the president, prime minister and several ministers, but mass protests and British and international pressure forced their release on 22 November 1943—now celebrated as Independence Day. Power was shared under the National Pact, an unwritten understanding reached the same year by El Khoury, a Maronite, and al-Solh, a Sunni, which balanced the country's confessional communities. The United States formally recognised Lebanese independence on 8 September 1944, and the last French troops withdrew at the end of 1946.

Lebanon is a narrow, mountainous land on the eastern Mediterranean whose coastal cities—Byblos, Sidon and Tyre—were the great harbours of Phoenicia, seafaring traders who carried the alphabet across the ancient world. Its rugged interior long sheltered marginalised communities, above all the Maronite Christians and the Druze, and under the Maan and Shihab emirs Mount Lebanon developed a distinct autonomy within the Ottoman Empire. After the 1860 sectarian conflict and the collapse of the Ottoman order, France created the State of Greater Lebanon in 1920, and the country won full independence on 22 November 1943 under an unwritten National Pact that distributed power among its religious communities. Prosperity made mid-century Beirut a regional financial and cultural capital, but a devastating civil war (1975–1990), foreign interventions, and a catastrophic economic collapse and port explosion after 2019 have severely tested the republic into the present.

## Phoenicia: Byblos, Sidon and Tyre — c. 3000 BCE – 332 BCE

The Canaanite harbour cities of the Lebanese coast became the seafaring Phoenicians, who spread the alphabet, Tyrian purple and Mediterranean trade.

- c. 3000 BCE: **Byblos trades cedar with Egypt** — Byblos flourishes as a port exporting Lebanese cedar to Egypt, beginning a record of urban habitation that makes it one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. [unesco-byblos, britannica-byblos]
- early 1st millennium BCE: **The Phoenician alphabet at Byblos** — A twenty-two letter alphabetic script comes into use at Byblos; adopted by the Greeks, it becomes the ancestor of the Greek and Latin alphabets and the writing of much of the world. [britannica-byblos, britannica-phoenicia, ebsco-phoenicia]
- trad. 814 BCE: **Tyre founds Carthage** — Tyrian colonists are traditionally credited with founding Carthage, the most powerful of the Phoenician colonies in the western Mediterranean. [britannica-phoenicia, unesco-tyre]
- 332 BCE: **Alexander the Great takes Tyre** — After a long siege and the building of a causeway to the island city, Alexander captures Tyre, ending the independence of the Phoenician city-states. [unesco-tyre, britannica-phoenicia]

## Roman Heliopolis and the Rise of Christianity — 332 BCE – 7th century CE

Under Greek and then Roman rule the coast prospered, Baalbek became a colossal sanctuary, and Christianity took root—giving rise to the Maronite community of Mount Lebanon.

- 1st–3rd century CE: **Baalbek becomes Roman Heliopolis** — Refounded as a Roman colony, Baalbek is endowed over more than two centuries with colossal temples to Jupiter, Bacchus and Venus, becoming one of the most famous sanctuaries of the Roman world. [unesco-baalbek, britannica-baalbek]
- late 4th–early 5th century: **Saint Maron and the Maronite origins** — The Maronite community traces its origin to Saint Maron, a Syrian hermit whose ascetic following grows into a distinct church that later takes refuge in the mountains of Lebanon. [britannica-maronite]
- 636: **Arab Muslim conquest of the Levant** — Arab armies defeat Byzantium and bring the Levant under Muslim rule, while the Christian communities of Mount Lebanon retain their distinct character in the highlands. [britannica-history-lebanon, britannica-lebanon]

Figures:

- Saint Maron (died c. 410) — Patron of the Maronite Church. A Syrian hermit of the late fourth and early fifth centuries whose ascetic disciples grew into the Maronite community, which later established itself in the mountains of Lebanon.

## The Emirate of Mount Lebanon — 1516 – 1840

Under Ottoman suzerainty the Maan and Shihab emirs forged a semi-autonomous Mount Lebanon; Fakhr al-Din II, often called the father of modern Lebanon, first united its Druze and Maronite districts.

- 1516: **Ottoman conquest of Syria** — The Ottomans defeat the Mamluks and take Syria and Lebanon, but leave Mount Lebanon largely to local emirs, allowing a semi-autonomous emirate to develop. [britannica-history-lebanon]
- c. 1590s–1633: **Fakhr al-Din II unites the mountain** — Fakhr al-Din II of the Druze Maan dynasty unites the Druze and Maronite districts of Mount Lebanon, expands his domains across the Levant and cultivates ties with Tuscany. [britannica-fakhr-al-din, britannica-history-lebanon]
- 1635: **Execution of Fakhr al-Din II** — Defeated by Ottoman forces wary of his power, Fakhr al-Din is captured and executed in Constantinople, but the union of Druze and Maronite districts he created endures. [britannica-fakhr-al-din]
- 1840: **Fall of Bashir Shihab II** — After siding with Egypt's Ibrahim Pasha, the long-ruling emir Bashir Shihab II is deposed and exiled when Egyptian rule in Syria collapses, ending the autonomous emirate. [britannica-bashir-shihab, britannica-history-lebanon]

Figures:

- Fakhr al-Din II (c. 1572–1635) — Maan emir of Mount Lebanon. Druze prince who first united the Druze and Maronite districts of Mount Lebanon and is often called the father of modern Lebanon; executed by the Ottomans in 1635.
- Bashir Shihab II (1767–1850) — Shihab emir of Mount Lebanon. The only Maronite ruler of the emirate, he governed Mount Lebanon from Beiteddine for some fifty years before being deposed and exiled in 1840.

## 1860, the Mutasarrifate and the French Mandate — 1840 – 1943

Sectarian violence in 1860 brought European intervention and an autonomous province; after the Ottoman collapse France created Greater Lebanon in 1920, leading to independence in 1943.

- 1860: **Sectarian conflict in Mount Lebanon** — Violence between Druze and Maronites spreads to Damascus and kills many thousands of Christians (figures are disputed estimates), prompting a French-led European intervention. [britannica-history-lebanon, loc-lebanon-pact]
- 1861: **The Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate** — Under European pressure the Ottomans grant Mount Lebanon autonomy as the Mutasarrifate, governed by a Christian official and a council of the religious communities. [britannica-history-lebanon]
- 1 September 1920: **Creation of Greater Lebanon** — French High Commissioner Henri Gouraud proclaims the State of Greater Lebanon, enlarging the old province with the coastal cities and the Bekaa under a French mandate. [britannica-history-lebanon, britannica-lebanon]
- 1926: **The Lebanese Republic and constitution** — Greater Lebanon is reorganised as the Lebanese Republic with a written constitution, though France retains ultimate authority under the mandate. [britannica-history-lebanon]

Figures:

- Henri Gouraud (1867–1946) — French High Commissioner for Syria and Lebanon. French general who proclaimed the State of Greater Lebanon on 1 September 1920, defining the borders of the modern country under the French mandate.

## Independence and the Golden Age — 1943 – 1975

An independent Lebanon governed by the National Pact prospered as the financial and cultural hub of the region, with Beirut hailed as the Paris of the Middle East.

- 1943: **The National Pact** — Bechara El Khoury and Riad al-Solh agree the unwritten National Pact distributing the presidency, premiership and parliamentary speakership among Maronites, Sunnis and Shi'a. [loc-lebanon-pact, britannica-history-lebanon]
- 8 September 1944: **U.S. recognition of Lebanon** — The United States formally recognises Lebanon as an independent state, and establishes diplomatic relations later that year. [state-lebanon]
- 31 December 1946: **French withdrawal completed** — The last French troops leave Lebanon, completing the country's transition to full sovereignty. [britannica-history-lebanon, state-lebanon]
- c. 1955–1975: **Beirut, the Paris of the Middle East** — Lebanon's golden age makes Beirut the banking, commercial and cultural capital of the region, renowned for its cosmopolitan prosperity. [britannica-lebanon, britannica-history-lebanon]

Figures:

- Bechara El Khoury (1890–1964) — First president of independent Lebanon. Maronite statesman and the republic's first president, who with Riad al-Solh forged the 1943 National Pact and led Lebanon to independence from France.
- Riad al-Solh (1894–1951) — First prime minister of independent Lebanon. Sunni nationalist and the republic's first prime minister, co-author of the National Pact, who anchored Lebanon's independence in an Arab orientation; assassinated in 1951.

## Civil War, Foreign Intervention and Collapse — 1975 – present

A fifteen-year civil war and successive interventions devastated the country; the Cedar Revolution, the rise of Hezbollah, and an economic collapse and port explosion have defined a fraught present.

- 13 April 1975: **The Lebanese Civil War** — Fighting erupts in Beirut and escalates into a fifteen-year civil war that draws in Syria, Israel and many militias; deaths are estimated, very approximately, in the range of 120,000–150,000. [britannica-civil-war, loc-lebanon-pact]
- 1982: **Israeli invasion and siege of Beirut** — Israel invades Lebanon and besieges Beirut, forcing out the PLO; the assassination of president-elect Bashir Gemayel is followed by the Sabra and Shatila massacre. [britannica-civil-war]
- 1989: **The Taif Agreement** — Brokered with Arab support, the Taif Agreement winds down the war and rebalances the confessional system, shifting authority from the Maronite president to the Sunni prime minister and cabinet. [britannica-civil-war, loc-lebanon-pact]
- 14 February 2005: **Hariri assassination and the Cedar Revolution** — A car bomb kills former prime minister Rafic Hariri and others; mass protests known as the Cedar Revolution lead Syria to withdraw its forces after nearly thirty years. [britannica-hariri, ebsco-hariri, britannica-second-republic]
- 2019–present: **Economic collapse and the Beirut port explosion** — From 2019 Lebanon suffers one of the world's worst economic crises; on 4 August 2020 an ammonium-nitrate explosion at the port of Beirut kills more than 200 people and devastates the capital. [cfr-lebanon, britannica-second-republic]

Figures:

- Rafic Hariri (1944–2005) — Prime minister of Lebanon. Businessman and statesman who led the post-war reconstruction of Beirut as prime minister; his 2005 assassination triggered the Cedar Revolution and the Syrian withdrawal.
