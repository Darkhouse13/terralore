# The Chronicle of Nigeria: From the Nok artisans and the great Sahelian and forest kingdoms to a colonial amalgamation, a hard-won independence, and Africa's most populous nation.

Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 1 October 1960. Modern Nigeria became a sovereign state on 1 October 1960, when British rule ended and Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa took office as the country's first prime minister; it became a republic in 1963. That date, however, sits atop a vast precolonial past. The central Nigerian Nok culture was producing terracotta sculpture and smelting iron from the first millennium BCE; the empire of Kanem-Bornu dominated the Lake Chad basin and the trans-Saharan trade for centuries; the Hausa city-states such as Kano and Katsina grew rich on commerce and Islamic learning; the Oyo Empire and the Kingdom of Benin built powerful states in the forest south; and in 1804 the jihad of Usman dan Fodio founded the Sokoto Caliphate across the north. The colonial entity itself was assembled by Britain, which on 1 January 1914 amalgamated the Northern and Southern Protectorates into a single Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria under Governor-General Frederick Lugard.

The land that became Nigeria holds one of Africa's deepest and richest pasts. As early as the first millennium BCE, the Nok culture of the central plateau was casting striking terracotta figures and smelting iron. Over the following centuries, powerful states arose across the region: the trans-Saharan empire of Kanem-Bornu around Lake Chad, the trading Hausa city-states of the north, the cavalry empire of Oyo and the artistic kingdom of Benin in the forest south, and the Igbo communities of the southeast whose Igbo-Ukwu bronzes astonish to this day. From the sixteenth century the Atlantic slave trade drew the coastal regions into a brutal commerce that deported enormous numbers of people, while in 1804 the jihad of Usman dan Fodio created the vast Sokoto Caliphate across the north. Britain conquered these lands piece by piece during the nineteenth century, joining the Northern and Southern Protectorates into a single colony in 1914. Nigeria won independence on 1 October 1960, but its early decades were scarred by the Biafran civil war of 1967 to 1970 and long stretches of military rule, before the return to civilian government in 1999 inaugurated the Fourth Republic and the modern, oil-rich, intensely diverse federation of more than 200 million people.

## The Nok culture and Nigeria's early societies — c. 1500 BCE – 1000 CE

Long before recorded kingdoms, the central Nigerian Nok culture was casting terracotta sculpture and smelting iron, while later communities such as Igbo-Ukwu reveal a sophisticated metalworking world in the forest south.

- c. 1500 BCE: **Rise of the Nok culture** — On the central Nigerian plateau the Nok culture emerged, producing distinctive terracotta sculpture and, in its later phases, smelting iron — among the earliest such traditions in sub-Saharan Africa. [met-nok, britannica-nigeria, breunig-nok]
- 1928: **Nok terracottas first recognised** — Terracotta heads unearthed near the village of Nok during tin-mining drew scholarly attention, giving the ancient culture its name and revealing the antiquity of Nigerian art. [met-nok, britannica-nigeria]
- 9th century CE: **The bronzes of Igbo-Ukwu** — An Igbo community at Igbo-Ukwu produced sophisticated cast-bronze ritual objects and vast quantities of trade beads, evidence of advanced metalworking and long-distance trade in the southeastern forests. [britannica-nigeria, blackpast-hausa]

Figures:

- Thurstan Shaw (1914–2013) — Archaeologist. British archaeologist whose excavations at Igbo-Ukwu around 1959–1960 revealed a ninth-century Igbo society of extraordinary bronze-casting skill, reshaping understanding of early Nigerian history.

## The great precolonial kingdoms: Kanem-Bornu, Hausa, Oyo and Benin — c. 800 – 1700

Across savanna and forest, powerful states took shape — the trans-Saharan empire of Kanem-Bornu, the trading Hausa city-states, the cavalry empire of Oyo, and the artistic kingdom of Benin.

- c. 9th century: **Rise of the Kanem empire** — Around Lake Chad the empire of Kanem emerged under the Sayfawa dynasty, growing wealthy as the southern terminus of the trans-Saharan trade and gradually adopting Islam. [lumen-bornu, britannica-nigeria]
- c. 1400: **Shift from Kanem to Bornu** — After conflict with the Bulala forced the dynasty to abandon its eastern heartland, the state re-centred west of Lake Chad at Bornu, beginning the era of the Bornu Empire. [lumen-bornu, britannica-nigeria]
- c. 1570–1603: **Peak of Bornu under Idris Alooma** — Mai Idris Alooma reformed Bornu's army and administration, extended its control over central Saharan trade routes, and deepened the role of Islamic law at its height of power. [idris-alooma-wiki, lumen-bornu]
- c. 13th–17th centuries: **Flourishing of Benin and Oyo** — The Edo kingdom of Benin and the Yoruba empire of Oyo rose as major forest powers, Benin renowned for its court art and Oyo for the cavalry empire of its Alaafin. [britannica-oyo, britannica-benin-bronzes, britannica-nigeria]

Figures:

- Mai Idris Alooma (reigned c. 1564–1596) — Ruler of the Bornu Empire. Among the greatest rulers of Kanem-Bornu, he reformed its army and governance, secured control of central Saharan trade routes, and strengthened Islamic law at the empire's height.
- Alaafin of Oyo (title; empire at its height 17th–18th c.) — Sovereign of the Oyo Empire. Title of the Yoruba monarch who ruled the Oyo Empire from Oyo-Ile, commanding a cavalry-based state whose power reached the Atlantic coast, while held in check by the Oyo Mesi council.

## The Atlantic slave trade and the Sokoto Caliphate — c. 1500 – 1850

From the sixteenth century the coastal regions were drawn into the transatlantic slave trade, which deported vast numbers of people, while in 1804 the jihad of Usman dan Fodio forged the great Sokoto Caliphate across the north.

- 16th century onward: **The Atlantic slave trade** — European demand drew the Bights of Benin and Biafra into the transatlantic slave trade; ports such as Bonny and Old Calabar became major export points, and vast numbers of people were deported in conditions of extreme violence. [slavevoyages, slaveryremembrance-biafra, britannica-nigeria]
- 1804: **Usman dan Fodio launches his jihad** — The Fulani scholar Usman dan Fodio began a jihad in the Hausa lands, rallying followers against the existing rulers and igniting a religious and political revolution across the north. [blackpast-sokoto, usman-wiki]
- c. 1808–1815: **Founding of the Sokoto Caliphate** — Dan Fodio's victories consolidated into the Sokoto Caliphate, a vast confederation of emirates under Islamic law that became one of nineteenth-century Africa's largest states. [blackpast-sokoto, usman-wiki]
- 1807: **Britain abolishes its slave trade** — Britain outlawed its own transatlantic slave trade and began naval patrols of the West African coast, though illegal slaving from the region continued for decades. [britannica-nigeria, slaveryremembrance-biafra]

Figures:

- Usman dan Fodio (1754–1817) — Scholar and founder of the Sokoto Caliphate. Fulani Islamic scholar, reformer, and revolutionary whose 1804 jihad overthrew the Hausa rulers and established the Sokoto Caliphate, reshaping the religion and politics of northern Nigeria.

## British conquest, amalgamation and colonial Nigeria — 1851 – 1960

Through gunboats, treaties, and force, Britain conquered the lands of Nigeria piece by piece, joined them into a single colony in 1914, and ruled until nationalist pressure brought independence in 1960.

- 1861: **Britain annexes Lagos** — After occupying Lagos in 1851, Britain annexed it as a crown colony in 1861, establishing a coastal foothold from which colonial power spread into the interior. [britannica-nigeria, statedept-nigeria]
- February 1897: **British sack of Benin City** — A British punitive expedition destroyed Benin City, exiled the Oba, and looted thousands of the Benin Bronzes, dispersing centuries of court art to foreign museums and collectors. [britannica-benin-bronzes, britishmuseum-benin, nms-benin]
- 1903: **Fall of the Sokoto Caliphate** — Frederick Lugard's forces defeated the Sokoto army in March 1903; the last independent sultan, Attahiru I, was killed at Burmi in July, ending the caliphate's independence and bringing the north under British control. [attahiru-wiki, britannica-nigeria]
- 1 January 1914: **Amalgamation of Nigeria** — Lord Lugard joined the Northern and Southern Protectorates into a single Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria, creating the territorial shape of the modern country under indirect rule. [britannica-nigeria, statedept-nigeria]
- 1 October 1960: **Nigeria gains independence** — Nigeria became an independent state within the Commonwealth on 1 October 1960, with Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as its first prime minister; it became a republic in 1963. [britannica-nigeria, statedept-nigeria, sahistory-independence]

Figures:

- Frederick Lugard (1858–1945) — Colonial administrator and Governor-General. Architect of British rule in Nigeria, he conquered the Sokoto Caliphate, championed indirect rule through traditional authorities, and amalgamated the Northern and Southern Protectorates in 1914.
- Nnamdi Azikiwe (1904–1996) — Nationalist leader and first President of Nigeria. Influential nationalist and newspaperman who helped lead the independence movement and became Nigeria's first president when the country became a republic in 1963.

## Independence, the Biafran civil war and military rule — 1960 – 1999

Independence soon gave way to coups, a catastrophic civil war over the secession of Biafra, and decades dominated by military governments and an oil-fuelled politics of patronage and instability.

- 1966: **Military coups and unrest** — A January 1966 coup killed Prime Minister Balewa; a July counter-coup and ensuing violence against Igbo communities deepened the crisis that would lead to civil war. [britannica-nigeria, britannica-civil-war]
- 30 May 1967: **Secession of Biafra** — Colonel Odumegwu Ojukwu declared the Eastern Region an independent Republic of Biafra, precipitating the Nigerian Civil War. [britannica-civil-war, history-civil-war]
- 1967–1970: **The Nigerian Civil War** — Federal forces under General Gowon fought to reunify the country and blockaded Biafra; the resulting famine and fighting killed an estimated one to two million people, mostly civilians, before Biafra surrendered in January 1970. [britannica-civil-war, history-civil-war]
- 1976: **Abuja chosen as new capital** — Amid an oil-fuelled boom, the federal government decided to build a new, centrally located capital at Abuja to replace Lagos, a project realised over the following decades. [britannica-nigeria]
- 1993: **Annulled election and Abacha's rule** — The military annulled the 1993 presidential election, widely believed won by Moshood Abiola, ushering in the repressive regime of General Sani Abacha until his death in 1998. [cfr-transition, britannica-nigeria]

Figures:

- Odumegwu Ojukwu (1933–2011) — Military governor and Biafran leader. Military governor of Nigeria's Eastern Region who declared the secession of Biafra in 1967 and led it through the civil war until its collapse in 1970.
- Yakubu Gowon (born 1934) — Head of state during the civil war. Led Nigeria's federal military government from 1966, prosecuted the war to keep the country united, and promoted reconciliation afterward under the slogan 'no victor, no vanquished'.

## The Fourth Republic and modern Nigeria — 1999 – present

Since the return to civilian rule in 1999, Nigeria has sustained an unbroken democracy, grown into Africa's most populous nation and largest oil producer, and grappled with insecurity, inequality, and the demands of its immense diversity.

- 29 May 1999: **Return to civilian rule and the Fourth Republic** — The military handed power to the elected president Olusegun Obasanjo, ending sixteen years of military government and inaugurating the Fourth Republic, Nigeria's longest period of continuous civilian rule. [cfr-transition, britannica-nigeria]
- 2014: **Africa's largest economy** — After rebasing its GDP, Nigeria was confirmed as the largest economy in Africa, underscoring its weight as the continent's most populous nation and a major oil producer. [aljazeera-economy, britannica-nigeria]
- 2015: **First opposition election victory** — Muhammadu Buhari defeated the sitting president Goodluck Jonathan, the first time an opposition candidate won Nigeria's presidency through the ballot box, marking a democratic milestone. [sais-democracy, britannica-nigeria]

Figures:

- Olusegun Obasanjo (born 1937) — President of Nigeria (1999–2007). Former military ruler who returned as the first elected president of the Fourth Republic, overseeing the consolidation of civilian rule and the creation of anti-corruption institutions.
