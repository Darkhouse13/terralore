Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 31 August 1962. On 31 August 1962 the British colony of Trinidad and Tobago became an independent state within the Commonwealth, with the Oxford-trained historian Dr Eric Williams — leader of the People's National Movement he had founded in 1956 — as its first prime minister. The United States recognised the new nation the same day by establishing its embassy in Port of Spain, and judge William Henry Hastie headed the American delegation at the independence ceremonies. Independence was the headline of a far longer story: the indigenous Carib and Arawak peoples, more than two centuries of Spanish claim and neglect over Trinidad, the French planters drawn by the Cédula de Población of 1783, the British capture of 1797 and the much-contested island of Tobago, emancipation, and the great wave of indentured Indian labour that had remade the society. Trinidad and Tobago went on to adopt a republican constitution in 1976, replacing the British monarch with a president as head of state, while remaining one of the Caribbean's most prosperous and culturally influential nations.

Trinidad and Tobago is a twin-island nation off the coast of Venezuela whose layered history made it one of the most cosmopolitan societies in the Caribbean. Trinidad, sighted by Columbus in 1498, was claimed by Spain but largely neglected until the Cédula de Población of 1783 drew French Catholic planters and enslaved Africans, before Britain captured it in 1797; Tobago, fought over by the Dutch, Courlanders, French, and British more often than any other Caribbean island, was finally ceded to Britain in 1814. Emancipation in 1834–38 and the importation of indentured Indian labourers from 1845 forged the distinctive Afro- and Indo-Trinidadian society, the two islands were united into a single colony in 1889, and oil transformed the economy after 1908. Led by the historian Dr Eric Williams and his People's National Movement, the colony won independence from Britain on 31 August 1962 and became a republic in 1976 — a nation that gave the world Carnival, calypso, soca, and the steelpan.

## The first peoples — c. 5000 BCE – 1498 CE

For thousands of years before Europeans arrived, the islands were home to Arawak- and Carib-speaking peoples who had migrated north from the South American mainland.

- c. 5000 BCE: **The first settlers reach the islands** — Archaic Ortoiroid peoples migrated from the South American mainland into Trinidad, the earliest of several waves of indigenous settlement that populated the twin islands over millennia. [national-trust-timeline, britannica-tt-history]
- c. 1000 CE: **Arawak and Carib peoples established** — By the time of European contact the islands were home to Arawakan-speaking groups such as the Nepoya and Suppoya and Cariban speakers such as the Yao, peoples whose roots lay in the Orinoco region. [britannica-tt, national-trust-timeline]
- 1498: **Columbus sights Trinidad and Tobago** — On his third voyage Christopher Columbus reached Trinidad, naming it after the Holy Trinity, and sighted Tobago — the first European contact with islands then inhabited by Arawak and Carib peoples. [britannica-tt-history, national-trust-timeline, statedept-tt]

## Spanish neglect and the Cédula de Población — 1498 – 1797

Claimed by Spain but left a backwater for nearly three centuries, Trinidad was transformed only after the Cédula de Población of 1783 opened it to French Catholic planters and enslaved Africans.

- 1592: **Spain founds San José de Oruña** — Antonio de Berrio took possession of Trinidad and founded San José de Oruña (St Joseph), establishing the first effective Spanish settlement on an island the crown had claimed but long neglected. [britannica-tt-history, national-trust-timeline]
- 1595: **Raleigh raids the Spanish settlement** — Sir Walter Raleigh attacked the Spanish settlement while searching for El Dorado, underscoring how weak and exposed the Spanish colony on Trinidad remained. [national-trust-timeline]
- 24 November 1783: **The Cédula de Población** — King Charles III's Cédula de Población offered free land and tax breaks to Roman Catholic settlers, drawing French planters with their enslaved Africans and free coloureds and remaking Trinidad as a plantation society. [britannica-tt-history, national-trust-timeline]

Figures:

- José María Chacón (served 1784–1797) — Last Spanish Governor of Trinidad. Governor who implemented the Cédula de Población, presiding over the rapid French and African settlement of Trinidad before surrendering the island to a British fleet in February 1797.

## British conquest and the much-contested Tobago — 1797 – 1838

Britain seized Trinidad in 1797 and confirmed it by treaty in 1802, while Tobago — captured and recaptured by Europeans more often than any other Caribbean island — was finally ceded to Britain in 1814.

- 18 February 1797: **Britain captures Trinidad** — A British expedition under Sir Ralph Abercromby took Trinidad from Spain almost without a fight; the last Spanish governor, Chacón, signed the capitulation at the Valsayn estate. [national-trust-timeline, britannica-tt-history]
- 1802: **Trinidad ceded to Britain by treaty** — The Treaty of Amiens confirmed Trinidad as a British colony, beginning more than a century and a half of British rule over the island. [britannica-tt-history, national-trust-timeline]
- 1814: **Tobago ceded to Britain** — After centuries of being fought over by the Dutch, Courlanders, French, and British, Tobago was finally awarded to Britain by the Treaty of Paris of 1814. [visit-tobago-timeline, britannica-tt-history]
- 1 August 1834: **Abolition of slavery** — Under Britain's Slavery Abolition Act, slavery ended in Trinidad and Tobago on 1 August 1834, though a transitional 'apprenticeship' kept the freed people tied to the estates. [britannica-tt-history, national-trust-timeline]
- 1 August 1838: **Full emancipation** — The apprenticeship system ended on 1 August 1838, granting the formerly enslaved full freedom and leaving the colony's planters scrambling for new sources of labour. [britannica-tt-history, nalis-arrival-day]

## Indenture, oil, and one colony — 1838 – 1937

Indentured Indian labourers arriving from 1845 remade the population, the two islands were joined into a single colony in 1889, oil transformed the economy from 1908, and labour unrest exploded in 1937.

- 30 May 1845: **Arrival of the Fatel Razack** — The ship Fatel Razack landed the first indentured Indian labourers in Trinidad; over 147,000 Indians would arrive under indenture by 1917, transforming the colony's population and culture. [nalis-arrival-day, national-trust-indenture]
- 1 January 1889: **Tobago united with Trinidad** — Britain amalgamated Tobago with the wealthier colony of Trinidad to cut administrative costs; in 1899 Tobago became a ward, creating the single colony of Trinidad and Tobago. [national-archives-unification, britannica-tt-history]
- 1908: **Commercial oil production begins** — The first commercial oil flowed near the Pitch Lake at La Brea, beginning the petroleum industry that would dominate Trinidad and Tobago's economy for over a century. [energy-petroleum-history, britannica-tt-history]
- 1917: **End of Indian indentureship** — The indentured labour system was abolished in 1917, by which time roughly 147,000 Indians had come to Trinidad, the majority remaining to build the Indo-Trinidadian community. [national-trust-indenture, nalis-arrival-day]
- June 1937: **The 1937 labour revolution** — An oilbelt strike led by Tubal Uriah 'Buzz' Butler spread into riots across the colony, leaving at least fourteen dead and giving birth to Trinidad and Tobago's modern trade-union and nationalist movements. [nalis-labour-day, national-trust-1937, britannica-tt-history]

Figures:

- Tubal Uriah 'Buzz' Butler (1897–1977) — Labour leader and trade unionist. Grenadian-born preacher and oilfield worker who led the 1937 labour revolution in the southern oilbelt, helping found Trinidad's trade-union movement and laying the groundwork for the demand for self-government.

## Eric Williams, independence, and the republic — 1937 – 1976

The historian Dr Eric Williams and his People's National Movement led the colony from self-government to independence on 31 August 1962, and to a republican constitution in 1976.

- 1956: **Eric Williams founds the PNM** — The historian Dr Eric Williams founded the People's National Movement and won office, beginning a dominance of Trinidad and Tobago's politics that would carry the colony to independence. [britannica-eric-williams, britannica-tt-history]
- 31 August 1962: **Independence from Britain** — Trinidad and Tobago became an independent state within the Commonwealth, with Eric Williams as first prime minister; the United States recognised the nation the same day. [statedept-tt, britannica-eric-williams, britannica-tt-history]
- 1970: **The Black Power uprising** — Mass marches and protests over racial and economic inequality, with a mutiny in the army, forced Eric Williams to declare a state of emergency in the most serious challenge to his government. [britannica-tt-history]
- 1 August 1976: **Trinidad and Tobago becomes a republic** — A new constitution replaced the British monarch with a president as head of state; the last governor-general, Sir Ellis Clarke, became the republic's first president. [britannica-tt-history, statedept-tt]

Figures:

- Dr Eric Williams (1911–1981) — First Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago. Oxford-trained historian, author of Capitalism and Slavery, who founded the People's National Movement in 1956 and led Trinidad and Tobago to independence in 1962, governing as prime minister until his death in 1981 — the 'Father of the Nation'.

## A modern energy nation — 1976 – present

From the 1990 coup attempt to the rise of natural gas and LNG, Trinidad and Tobago became the Caribbean's energy powerhouse and the cultural home of Carnival, calypso, soca, and the steelpan.

- 27 July 1990: **The Jamaat al-Muslimeen coup attempt** — The Islamist group Jamaat al-Muslimeen seized Parliament and the state television station, taking the prime minister and others hostage for six days in a failed coup that left more than twenty people dead. [jamestown-jam, britannica-tt-history]
- 1999: **Atlantic LNG begins exports** — The Atlantic LNG plant at Point Fortin started production, confirming Trinidad and Tobago's shift from oil to natural gas and making it a major global LNG exporter. [energy-petroleum-history]
- c. 1940: **Birth of the steelpan** — Musicians in Port of Spain fashioned the steelpan from oil drums after colonial bans on African drumming — the only acoustic instrument invented in the 20th century and now the national instrument. [nalis-steelband]
- 2025: **Persad-Bissessar returns as prime minister** — In the April 2025 general election the United National Congress defeated the PNM and Kamla Persad-Bissessar was sworn in as prime minister, another peaceful transfer of power in the republic. [finance-economy-2025]

Figures:

- A. N. R. Robinson (1926–2014) — Prime Minister and later President. Tobago-born statesman who was prime minister during the 1990 Jamaat al-Muslimeen coup attempt, when he was taken hostage and reportedly defied his captors, and who later served as president of the republic.
