Founding: Restoration of independence from the Soviet Union — 9 April 1991. Modern Georgia dates its statehood to 9 April 1991, when the Supreme Council of the Republic of Georgia adopted an Act restoring the country's independence, building on a referendum held on 31 March in which an overwhelming majority voted for independence. The date was chosen deliberately: it fell on the second anniversary of 9 April 1989, when Soviet troops dispersed a peaceful pro-independence demonstration in Tbilisi, killing some twenty people and galvanising the national movement. Independence restored a sovereign Georgian state after seven decades of Soviet rule and a longer century of incorporation into the Russian Empire from 1801. Its deeper roots run back through the short-lived Democratic Republic of Georgia of 1918 to 1921, the medieval kingdom unified in the golden age of David the Builder and Queen Tamar, and the ancient realms of Colchis and Iberia. Zviad Gamsakhurdia, leader of the national movement, became the first president of the newly independent state.

Georgia's story begins in the ancient kingdoms of Colchis, the land to which Greek myth sent Jason and the Argonauts in search of the Golden Fleece, and Iberia, the eastern realm whose king Mirian III adopted Christianity through the preaching of St Nino in the fourth century, making Georgia one of the world's oldest Christian states. A unified kingdom reached its golden age under David IV 'the Builder' and Queen Tamar in the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, a flowering of architecture, learning and the verse of Shota Rustaveli now embodied in the UNESCO monuments of Mtskheta, Gelati and Upper Svaneti. Mongol, Persian and Ottoman pressure fragmented the country before the Russian Empire annexed it from 1801; a Democratic Republic of Georgia survived from 1918 until the Soviet conquest of 1921, and Soviet Georgia, birthplace of Joseph Stalin, lasted until independence was restored on 9 April 1991. Since then Georgia has weathered conflicts in Abkhazia and South Ossetia, the 2003 Rose Revolution and the 2008 war with Russia, while orienting itself firmly toward Europe and the West.

## Colchis, Iberia and the Land of the Golden Fleece — c. 600 BCE – 330 CE

Two ancient kingdoms, the Black Sea realm of Colchis and the eastern land of Iberia, gave Georgia its first statehood and a place in Greek myth as the goal of Jason and the Argonauts.

- c. 6th century BCE: **Kingdom of Colchis flourishes** — On the Black Sea coast of western Georgia, the wealthy kingdom of Colchis, the land of the Golden Fleece in Greek myth, traded with the Greek world and developed a society famed for its metalwork. [colchis-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- c. late 4th century BCE: **Kingdom of Iberia founded** — The eastern Georgian kingdom of Iberia emerged in the lands of Kartli, centred on Mtskheta, forming a distinct monarchy that would become the core of the Georgian state. [iberia-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- 65 BCE: **Pompey campaigns in the Caucasus** — The Roman general Pompey campaigned in Colchis and Iberia, drawing the Georgian lands into the long contest between Rome and the Iranian empires over the Caucasus. [history-georgia-britannica, colchis-britannica]

## The Conversion of Iberia and an Early Christian State — 337 – 1008

Through the preaching of St Nino, King Mirian III made Christianity the state religion of Iberia in the fourth century, binding Georgia to Christendom long before most of Europe and shaping its church, script and identity.

- c. 337: **Iberia adopts Christianity** — Through the preaching of St Nino, King Mirian III of Iberia adopted Christianity as the state religion, making Georgia one of the world's oldest Christian states. [nino-britannica, mtskheta-unesco, history-georgia-britannica]
- 7th century: **Arab conquest and the Emirate of Tbilisi** — Arab armies brought Islam to the Caucasus and established an emirate at Tbilisi, beginning centuries in which Georgian princely houses contested Muslim and Byzantine power. [history-georgia-britannica]
- 1008: **Bagrat III unites Georgia** — Bagrat III of the Bagrationi dynasty united the eastern and western Georgian lands into a single kingdom, laying the foundation for the medieval golden age. [history-georgia-britannica, gelati-unesco]

Figures:

- St Nino (early 4th century) — Evangeliser of Georgia. A Cappadocian woman whose preaching brought Christianity to the kingdom of Iberia. Credited with converting King Mirian III and Queen Nana, she is venerated as 'Equal to the Apostles' and the Enlightener of Georgia, the nation's most cherished saint.

## The Golden Age of David the Builder and Queen Tamar — 1008 – 1230

Under David IV 'the Builder' and his great-granddaughter Queen Tamar, a unified Georgia reached the zenith of its power, learning and art, the era of the Gelati Academy and Rustaveli's national epic.

- 1121: **Battle of Didgori** — David IV the Builder defeated a much larger Seljuk Turkish coalition at Didgori west of Tbilisi, a decisive victory that broke Seljuk power in the Caucasus. [david-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- 1122: **Tbilisi recovered and made capital** — David IV recaptured Tbilisi from Muslim rule, ending centuries of the emirate and establishing the city as the royal capital of a reunited Georgia. [history-georgia-britannica, david-britannica]
- 1184–1213: **Reign of Queen Tamar** — Tamar, titled mepe ('king'), brought Georgia to the height of its power and prestige, ruling a pan-Caucasian realm and presiding over a cultural golden age. [tamar-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- c. 1200: **Rustaveli's national epic** — Shota Rustaveli composed 'The Knight in the Panther's Skin', the Georgian national epic, the supreme literary achievement of the golden age and a classic of world literature. [rustaveli-britannica]

Figures:

- David IV the Builder (1073 – 1125) — King of Georgia. Reigning from 1089, David reformed the army and state, defeated the Seljuks at Didgori in 1121 and recaptured Tbilisi, reuniting Georgia and inaugurating its golden age. Founder of the Gelati monastery and academy, he is regarded as the greatest of Georgian rulers and a saint of the Georgian Orthodox Church.
- Queen Tamar (c. 1160 – 1213) — Queen (mepe) of Georgia. The first woman to rule Georgia in her own right, styled mepe ('king'), Tamar brought the kingdom to the zenith of its power and prestige, ruling a pan-Caucasian empire and presiding over the cultural flowering of the golden age. She was canonised by the Georgian Orthodox Church.
- Shota Rustaveli (fl. c. 1200) — Poet. Georgian poet of Queen Tamar's age, author of 'The Knight in the Panther's Skin', the Georgian national epic. His verse of chivalry, love and friendship is the defining literary work of the golden age and a monument of medieval world literature.

## Fragmentation, Foreign Pressure and Russian Annexation — 1230 – 1878

Mongol, Persian and Ottoman invasions broke the kingdom into rival principalities, and an eastern Georgian state seeking protection against Persia was annexed by the Russian Empire from 1801.

- 1386–1403: **Invasions of Timur** — The armies of Timur (Tamerlane) repeatedly devastated Georgia, accelerating the breakup of the kingdom into rival principalities amid Mongol and Turkic pressure. [history-georgia-britannica, turkish-persian-britannica]
- 24 July 1783: **Treaty of Georgievsk** — King Erekle II of Kartli-Kakheti placed his kingdom under Russian protection, ceding control of foreign affairs in exchange for guarantees of its integrity and dynasty against Persia and the Ottomans. [georgievsk-britannica, turkish-persian-britannica]
- 1801: **Russia annexes Kartli-Kakheti** — The Russian Empire unilaterally annexed eastern Georgia and abolished the Bagrationi monarchy, beginning the absorption of the Georgian lands that was completed over the following decades. [history-georgia-britannica, georgievsk-britannica]

Figures:

- Erekle II (1720 – 1798) — King of Kartli-Kakheti. Ruler of eastern Georgia who sought Russian protection against Persia and the Ottomans, concluding the 1783 Treaty of Georgievsk. He saw Tbilisi sacked by the Persians in 1795, and after his death his kingdom was annexed by Russia.

## Republic, Soviet Conquest and the Georgian SSR — 1878 – 1991

A short-lived Democratic Republic of Georgia was overrun by the Red Army in 1921, and Soviet Georgia, birthplace of Stalin, endured until the national movement restored independence in 1991.

- 26 May 1918: **Democratic Republic of Georgia proclaimed** — Amid the collapse of the Russian Empire, Georgia proclaimed an independent democratic republic governed by Mensheviks, with a democratic constitution and land reform. [independent-georgia-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- Feb–Mar 1921: **Red Army conquest** — The Soviet Red Army invaded, took Tbilisi after heavy fighting and proclaimed the Georgian SSR on 25 February 1921, incorporating Georgia into the Soviet Union. [independent-georgia-britannica, history-georgia-britannica]
- 9 April 1989: **The April 9 tragedy in Tbilisi** — Soviet troops violently broke up a peaceful pro-independence demonstration in Tbilisi, killing about twenty people and galvanising the Georgian national movement. [independent-georgia-britannica]
- 9 April 1991: **Independence restored** — Following a referendum on 31 March, the Supreme Council adopted the Act restoring Georgia's independence on 9 April 1991, two years after the April 9 tragedy. [independent-georgia-britannica, us-state-georgia]

Figures:

- Joseph Stalin (1878 – 1953) — Soviet leader, born in Georgia. Born Ioseb Jughashvili in Gori, Georgia, Stalin rose through the Bolshevik movement to rule the Soviet Union from the late 1920s until his death in 1953. The most powerful Georgian in history, his rule brought industrialisation alongside mass repression, an ambivalent legacy for his homeland.
- Zviad Gamsakhurdia (1939 – 1993) — First President of independent Georgia. Dissident and leader of the Georgian national movement who became the first president of independent Georgia in 1991. His authoritarian style and the ensuing turmoil led to his overthrow in a coup little more than a year later.

## Independent Georgia: Conflicts, the Rose Revolution and the Western Turn — 1991 – present

Independence brought conflict in Abkhazia and South Ossetia, the peaceful Rose Revolution of 2003, the 2008 war with Russia, and a sustained turn toward Europe and the West.

- 1992–93: **War in Abkhazia** — Fighting between Georgian forces and Abkhaz separatists, with outside support, ended in Georgian defeat and the displacement of much of the ethnic Georgian population; casualty figures are disputed estimates. [abkhazia-britannica, independent-georgia-britannica]
- Nov 2003: **The Rose Revolution** — Peaceful mass protests over disputed elections, in which demonstrators carried roses, forced the resignation of President Shevardnadze and brought the reformer Mikheil Saakashvili to power. [independent-georgia-britannica]
- Aug 2008: **Russia–Georgia war over South Ossetia** — A five-day war around South Ossetia drew in the Russian military; estimates put the combined dead at about 850 with over 100,000 displaced. Russia then recognised Abkhazia and South Ossetia, a step most states rejected. [independent-georgia-britannica, south-ossetia-britannica]
- 2014: **EU Association Agreement** — Georgia signed an Association Agreement with the European Union, deepening a Western orientation that later led to its 2022 EU membership application and 2023 candidate status. [us-state-georgia, independent-georgia-britannica]

Figures:

- Eduard Shevardnadze (1928 – 2014) — President of Georgia. Former Soviet foreign minister who led Georgia through the turbulent 1990s after Gamsakhurdia's overthrow, presiding over the wars in Abkhazia and South Ossetia before resigning in the 2003 Rose Revolution.
- Mikheil Saakashvili (born 1967) — President of Georgia. Reformer brought to power by the 2003 Rose Revolution, Saakashvili pursued anti-corruption reforms and a strong pro-Western course; his presidency saw the 2008 war with Russia over South Ossetia.
