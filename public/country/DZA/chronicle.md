Founding: Independence from France — 5 July 1962. After a war of independence lasting from November 1954, Algeria became a sovereign state in the summer of 1962. The Évian Accords of 18 March 1962 set out a ceasefire, and a self-determination referendum on 1 July 1962 returned an overwhelming vote for independence; France recognised Algeria as an independent state on 3 July, and Algerian independence was proclaimed on 5 July 1962—the date deliberately chosen to fall on the anniversary of the French capture of Algiers in 1830, marking the end of 132 years of colonial rule. Ahmed Ben Bella, a leader of the FLN, became the first head of government and, in 1963, the first president of the People's Democratic Republic of Algeria.

Algeria, the largest country in Africa, is an ancient land of the indigenous Amazigh (Berber) peoples whose recorded history begins with the Numidian kingdom of Massinissa and the Roman cities of the coast, among them Hippo Regius, home of Saint Augustine. After Vandal and Byzantine interludes, the Arab-Islamic conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries reshaped the Maghreb, and a succession of Berber dynasties—Zirids, Hammadids and others—ruled before the Ottoman Regency of Algiers made the city a feared corsair power for three centuries. France invaded in 1830 and, against the long resistance of Abd al-Qadir, imposed a settler colonialism that lasted 132 years. The brutal War of Independence (1954–1962) led by the National Liberation Front (FLN) ended with Algerian independence on 5 July 1962; the decades since have seen the rule of Ben Bella and Boumédiène, the civil war of the 1990s known as the "black decade," and the Hirak protests of 2019 that ended the presidency of Abdelaziz Bouteflika.

## Numidia, Carthage and Roman Africa — c. 300 BCE – 430 CE

The Amazigh kingdom of Numidia rose beside Carthage, was absorbed into the Roman world, and gave the Church one of its greatest fathers in Augustine of Hippo.

- c. 202 BCE: **Massinissa unites Numidia** — Allied with Rome in the Second Punic War, the Massylian king Massinissa defeats Syphax and unifies the Amazigh kingdom of Numidia, ruling until 148 BCE. [britannica-masinissa, ebsco-numidia-mauretania]
- 112–105 BCE: **The Jugurthine War** — Massinissa's grandson Jugurtha wars with Rome over control of Numidia; captured by treachery, he is led in Marius's triumph and executed at Rome in 104 BCE. [britannica-masinissa, ebsco-numidia-mauretania]
- c. 100 CE: **Trajan founds Timgad** — The emperor Trajan establishes Timgad (Thamugadi) as a Roman colony; its grid plan, forum, theatre and arch survive as one of the best-preserved Roman towns. [unesco-timgad, worldhistory-roman-algeria]
- c. 395–430 CE: **Augustine, bishop of Hippo** — Augustine serves as bishop of Hippo Regius from about 395 until his death in 430, writing the Confessions and City of God and shaping Western Christian thought. [britannica-history-algeria, worldhistory-roman-algeria]

Figures:

- Massinissa (c. 238–148 BCE) — King of Numidia. Amazigh ruler who allied with Rome in the Second Punic War and united Numidia into a single kingdom, promoting settled agriculture and urban life.
- Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE) — Bishop and Church Father. Born at Thagaste in Roman Numidia, he became bishop of Hippo Regius and one of the most influential theologians of the Latin Church, author of the Confessions and City of God.

## Vandals, Byzantines and the Coming of Islam — 430 – 1152

Vandal and Byzantine rule gave way to the Arab-Islamic conquests, fierce Amazigh resistance under Kusaila and the Kahina, and the rise of Berber Muslim dynasties.

- 439: **Vandals take Carthage** — After seizing Hippo Regius, Genseric's Vandals capture Carthage in 439 and rule a kingdom commanding the western Mediterranean. [britannica-north-africa-vandals, worldhistory-tricamarum]
- 533–534: **Byzantine reconquest** — Justinian's general Belisarius lands in 533 and within a year destroys the Vandal kingdom, restoring Byzantine imperial rule over coastal North Africa. [worldhistory-tricamarum, britannica-north-africa-vandals]
- c. 683: **Kusaila defeats Uqba ibn Nafi** — The Awraba chief Kusaila ambushes and kills the Arab general Uqba ibn Nafi near Biskra, a high point of Amazigh resistance to the Islamic conquest. [britannica-uqba, wiki-muslim-conquest-maghreb]
- c. 698: **The Kahina and the fall of Carthage** — The Amazigh queen known as the Kahina leads resistance from the Aurès before her defeat around 698, the year the Arabs take Carthage and end Byzantine rule in Africa. [wiki-kahina, wiki-muslim-conquest-maghreb]
- 1011: **Hammadid dynasty and Beni Hammad** — The Berber Hammadid dynasty is established and founds the Qal'a of Beni Hammad; Bejaïa becomes one of the foremost ports of North Africa. [unesco-beni-hammad, wiki-history-algeria]

Figures:

- The Kahina (Dihya) (died c. 698) — Amazigh warrior-queen. Berber queen of the Aurès who led the tribes against the Arab conquest of the Maghreb until her defeat at the close of the seventh century; a lasting symbol of Amazigh resistance.

## The Ottoman Regency of Algiers — 1516 – 1830

Under the Barbarossa brothers Algiers became an Ottoman regency and a corsair power whose raids and captives troubled the whole Mediterranean for three centuries.

- c. 1516: **Barbarossa brothers seize Algiers** — The corsair Aruj takes Algiers; his brother Hayreddin places it under Ottoman suzerainty, founding the Regency of Algiers. [britannica-barbarossa, wiki-regency-algiers]
- 1518: **Ottoman suzerainty established** — After Aruj's death, Hayreddin Barbarossa submits Algiers to the Ottoman sultan and is made beylerbey, binding the regency to Istanbul. [britannica-barbarossa, wiki-regency-algiers]
- 1671: **Rule of the deys** — Power in the regency passes to elected military rulers titled dey, heading a janissary state financed by corsairing and ransom. [wiki-regency-algiers, britannica-history-algeria]
- 1827: **The "fly-whisk" incident** — A dispute over French debts ends with the dey of Algiers striking the French consul with a fly-whisk, a slight France later uses as a pretext for invasion. [ebsco-france-conquers-algeria, britannica-history-algeria]

Figures:

- Hayreddin Barbarossa (c. 1466/83–1546) — Corsair and Ottoman beylerbey of Algiers. Corsair captain who placed Algiers under Ottoman suzerainty and rose to command the Ottoman fleet, anchoring three centuries of the Regency of Algiers.

## French Conquest and Settler Colony — 1830 – 1954

France seized Algiers in 1830 and, against the resistance of Abd al-Qadir, built a settler colony in which European pieds-noirs ruled over a disenfranchised Muslim majority.

- 5 July 1830: **France captures Algiers** — A French expeditionary force takes Algiers, ending the Ottoman regency and beginning 132 years of French rule in Algeria. [ebsco-france-conquers-algeria, britannica-history-algeria]
- 1832–1847: **Abd al-Qadir's resistance** — Proclaimed amir in 1832, Abd al-Qadir builds a state across much of Algeria and leads armed resistance to the French until his surrender in 1847. [britannica-abdelkader, ebsco-france-conquers-algeria]
- 1848: **Algeria annexed to France** — Algeria is organised into French départements and declared an integral part of France, opening the way to large-scale European settlement and land expropriation. [britannica-history-algeria, ebsco-french-occupation-algeria]
- 1871: **The Mokrani (Kabyle) revolt** — A major uprising in Kabylia is crushed, followed by further confiscations of land that deepen the dispossession of the Muslim population. [britannica-history-algeria]
- 8 May 1945: **Sétif massacre** — A nationalist demonstration at Sétif on VE Day is met with mass killing of thousands of Algerians, radicalising the independence movement. [britannica-history-algeria, ebsco-algerian-war]

Figures:

- Abd al-Qadir (Abdelkader) (1808–1883) — Amir and leader of the resistance. Religious and military leader, amir of Mascara from 1832, who founded an Algerian state and led the long resistance to the French conquest before surrendering in 1847.

## The War of Independence — 1954 – 1962

The FLN's insurrection of November 1954 grew into a long and brutal war that ended 132 years of French rule with independence on 5 July 1962.

- 1 November 1954: **Toussaint Rouge: the war begins** — The FLN launches coordinated attacks across Algeria on 1 November 1954, opening the war of independence against French rule. [ebsco-algerian-war, britannica-history-algeria]
- 1956–1957: **Battle of Algiers** — A fierce urban campaign in the capital pits FLN cells against French paratroopers; the army's use of torture turns world opinion against the war. [ebsco-algerian-war, lumen-algerian-war]
- 1958: **The war topples the Fourth Republic** — The Algerian crisis brings down France's Fourth Republic and returns Charles de Gaulle to power, opening the way to eventual negotiations. [britannica-history-algeria]
- 18 March 1962: **Évian Accords** — France and the FLN sign the Évian Accords, agreeing a ceasefire and a self-determination referendum that will lead to Algerian independence. [ebsco-algerian-war, lumen-algerian-war]
- 5 July 1962: **Independence proclaimed** — After a referendum on 1 July and French recognition on 3 July, Algeria proclaims its independence on 5 July 1962, ending 132 years of French rule. [state-algeria, britannica-ben-bella]

Figures:

- Ahmed Ben Bella (1916–2012) — FLN leader and first president of Algeria. A founder and leader of the FLN imprisoned by France during the war, he became independent Algeria's first head of government in 1962 and its first president in 1963.

## Independent Algeria: Revolution, Civil War and Hirak — 1962 – present

From the socialist state of Ben Bella and Boumédiène through the "black decade" of civil war to the Hirak protests of 2019, independent Algeria has wrestled with one-party power and popular demands for change.

- 19 June 1965: **Boumédiène seizes power** — Colonel Houari Boumédiène overthrows Ahmed Ben Bella and installs himself as president, beginning thirteen years of centralised socialist rule. [britannica-boumediene, ebsco-boumedienne-coup]
- 1971: **Nationalisation of oil and gas** — Boumédiène nationalises the hydrocarbon industry, using oil and gas revenues to fund state-led industrialisation and a socialist development model. [britannica-boumediene, britannica-history-algeria]
- January 1992: **Elections cancelled; civil war begins** — After the Islamic Salvation Front leads the December 1991 vote, the army cancels the elections and ousts the president, triggering the civil war of the 1990s. [britannica-civil-war, wiki-1992-coup]
- 1999–2005: **Bouteflika and national reconciliation** — Elected in 1999, Abdelaziz Bouteflika ends the civil war through a Civil Concord and the 2005 Charter for Peace and National Reconciliation. [britannica-bouteflika, britannica-civil-war]
- 22 February – 2 April 2019: **The Hirak and Bouteflika's fall** — Mass peaceful protests known as the Hirak erupt against Bouteflika's bid for a fifth term; he resigns on 2 April 2019, and Abdelmadjid Tebboune is elected president that December. [britannica-bouteflika, wiki-hirak]

Figures:

- Houari Boumédiène (1932–1978; r. 1965–1978) — President of Algeria. Army commander who overthrew Ben Bella in 1965 and ruled until his death, nationalising the hydrocarbon industry and building a socialist, non-aligned Algerian state.
- Abdelaziz Bouteflika (1937–2021; r. 1999–2019) — President of Algeria. Veteran of the independence war who as president ended the civil war through national reconciliation but, seeking a fifth term, was forced from office by the 2019 Hirak protests.
