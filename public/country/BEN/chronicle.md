# The Chronicle of Benin: From the Aja kingdoms of Allada and Porto-Novo and the Fon Kingdom of Dahomey at Abomey, through conquest and the slave trade, to a pioneering African democracy.

Founding: Independence from France — 1 August 1960. The modern state became independent on 1 August 1960 as the Republic of Dahomey, when French colonial rule ended and Hubert Maga became its first president. The name 'Dahomey' was inherited from the precolonial Fon kingdom centred on Abomey; in 1975 the country was renamed the People's Republic of Benin, after the Bight of Benin, and in 1990 it became simply the Republic of Benin. That independence date sits atop a deep past: the Aja kingdoms of Allada and Porto-Novo took shape from the late sixteenth century, and the Fon Kingdom of Dahomey rose on the Abomey plateau from around the early seventeenth century, becoming a major power after King Agaja conquered Allada in 1724 and the slaving port of Ouidah in 1727. France conquered the kingdom in 1892–1894 and incorporated it into French West Africa.

The Republic of Benin — known until 1975 as Dahomey — occupies a narrow strip of West Africa between Nigeria and Togo. Its precolonial history was shaped by Aja-speaking peoples who, by tradition, migrated from Tado on the Mono River and founded the coastal kingdoms of Allada and, in the late sixteenth century, Porto-Novo (a town of mixed Aja and Yoruba character). Inland on the Abomey plateau, the Fon built the Kingdom of Dahomey, a centralised military state whose kings — among them Agaja, Ghezo and the last independent ruler Behanzin — were guarded by the famous all-female regiment Europeans called the Dahomey 'Amazons' (the Agojie or Mino). Dahomey grew rich and powerful through the Atlantic slave trade conducted from the port of Ouidah on the 'Slave Coast', through which more than a million enslaved Africans were deported. France conquered Dahomey in two wars between 1890 and 1894, exiling King Behanzin, and ruled it as a colony within French West Africa until independence on 1 August 1960. The Republic of Dahomey then endured more than a decade of coups, until Mathieu Kérékou seized power in 1972, adopted Marxism-Leninism and in 1975 renamed the country Benin. After his regime collapsed, Benin's landmark 1990 National Conference produced one of Africa's earliest peaceful transitions to multiparty democracy. This is the modern Republic of Benin, and is distinct from the historic Kingdom of Benin, an Edo state in present-day Nigeria.

## The Aja migrations and the kingdoms of Allada and Porto-Novo — c. 1100 – 1600

By tradition the Aja people migrated from Tado on the Mono River and founded coastal kingdoms — Allada, and the later port-state of Porto-Novo — that drew Yoruba and European trade to the lagoons of the Slave Coast.

- c. 12th–15th centuries: **Aja migrations from Tado** — Aja-speaking peoples spread, by oral tradition, eastward from Tado on the Mono River across the coastal plain of southern Benin, founding a cluster of kin-based kingdoms including Allada. [britannica-benin, wiki-porto-novo, wiki-dahomey-history]
- 16th century: **Rise of the Kingdom of Allada** — Allada (Great Ardra) emerged as the leading Aja kingdom of the coastal region and the dynastic source from which the kingdoms of Dahomey and Porto-Novo would branch. [britannica-benin, wiki-dahomey-history]
- c. 1600: **Founding of Porto-Novo** — The prince Te-Agbanlin, of the Allada royal house, founded the lagoon settlement of Hogbonu (Ajashe), later named Porto-Novo, which grew into a coastal port-kingdom of mixed Aja and Yoruba population. [wiki-porto-novo, britannica-benin]

## The Fon Kingdom of Dahomey and the palaces of Abomey — c. 1620 – 1818

On the Abomey plateau the Fon built one of West Africa's most formidable kingdoms — centralised, militarised, and ruled from earthen palaces decorated with bas-reliefs, whose kings conquered the coast and seized the slaving port of Ouidah.

- early 17th century: **Foundation of Dahomey at Abomey** — Fon rulers established the Kingdom of Dahomey on the Abomey plateau; King Houegbadja is credited with beginning the Royal Palaces of Abomey and building a strongly centralised monarchy. [britannica-dahomey, wiki-dahomey-history, unesco-abomey]
- 1724: **Dahomey conquers Allada** — King Agaja's army overran Allada, the parent kingdom of the Fon dynasty, beginning Dahomey's drive toward the coast and the Atlantic trade. [britannica-dahomey, wiki-dahomey-history]
- 1727: **Conquest of Ouidah (Whydah)** — Agaja seized the major slaving port of Whydah (Ouidah), giving Dahomey direct control of one of the busiest points of embarkation on the Slave Coast. [britannica-dahomey, cambridge-ouidah, wiki-ouidah]
- c. 1730: **Tributary to the Oyo Empire** — Dahomey's expansion brought it into conflict with the Yoruba empire of Oyo to the east, to which it became tributary for much of the eighteenth century. [wiki-dahomey-history, britannica-dahomey]
- c. 1625–1900: **The Royal Palaces of Abomey** — A line of kings built earthen palaces decorated with polychrome bas-reliefs within a single great enclosure at Abomey; the complex was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985. [unesco-abomey, unesco-japan-abomey]

Figures:

- Agaja (reigned c. 1708–1740) — King of Dahomey. Warrior-king who carried Dahomey to the sea, conquering Allada in 1724 and the slaving port of Ouidah in 1727 and establishing the kingdom as the dominant power of the Slave Coast.

## Ouidah, the slave trade, King Ghezo and the Agojie — c. 1670 – 1889

Through the port of Ouidah, Dahomey deported vast numbers of enslaved people across the Atlantic; King Ghezo expanded the kingdom and its all-female Agojie regiment, even as British pressure and the rise of palm oil forced a slow, contested change.

- c. 1670s–1860s: **Ouidah and the Atlantic slave trade** — The port of Ouidah served for nearly two centuries as a leading point of embarkation on the Slave Coast, through which more than a million enslaved Africans are estimated to have been deported across the Atlantic. [cambridge-ouidah, wiki-ouidah, slavevoyages]
- 1818: **Ghezo seizes the throne** — Ghezo overthrew his brother Adandozan with the support of the Brazilian slave trader Francisco Félix de Sousa, beginning a reign that would expand Dahomey's army and its female Agojie regiment. [wiki-ghezo, blackpast-dahomey-women]
- 1823: **Dahomey breaks free of Oyo** — Ghezo defeated the weakened Yoruba empire of Oyo, ending the tributary status Dahomey had borne since the early eighteenth century and asserting full independence. [wiki-ghezo, britannica-dahomey]
- 1851–1852: **British blockade of Dahomey's ports** — Britain imposed a naval blockade to force Dahomey to end the slave trade; Ghezo agreed in 1852 but soon resumed slaving, as palm oil rose to become the kingdom's chief legitimate export. [cambridge-palm-oil, wiki-ghezo]
- 1729: **First written record of the women warriors** — European observers recorded Dahomey's distinctive all-female troops — the Agojie or Mino, later called the 'Amazons' — who would become an elite, high-status corps of the royal army. [britannica-dahomey-women, blackpast-dahomey-women]

Figures:

- Ghezo (reigned 1818–c.1858) — King of Dahomey. One of Dahomey's most powerful kings, he ended the kingdom's tribute to Oyo, expanded and formalised the all-female Agojie regiment, and navigated the wrenching transition from slave trading to the palm-oil economy under British pressure.
- The Agojie (Mino) (c. 18th–19th centuries) — All-female military regiment of Dahomey. Known to Europeans as the Dahomey 'Amazons', this elite corps of women soldiers guarded the king, fought in the kingdom's wars, and by the mid-nineteenth century formed a substantial and high-status part of the Dahomean army.

## Behanzin, the French conquest, and colonial Dahomey — 1889 – 1960

King Behanzin's resistance could not withstand French firepower; after two wars France conquered Dahomey, exiled its last independent king, and ruled the territory as a colony of French West Africa until independence in 1960.

- 1890: **First Franco-Dahomean War** — King Behanzin's forces clashed with the French around Cotonou; though repelled by superior French weaponry, the war marked the opening of Dahomey's struggle against French expansion. [britannica-behanzin, wiki-behanzin, shd-conquest]
- 1892–1894: **French conquest of Dahomey** — A French expedition under Colonel Alfred-Amédée Dodds advanced on Abomey; Behanzin burned the capital rather than surrender it, and after further resistance capitulated in January 1894. [britannica-behanzin, wiki-behanzin, shd-conquest]
- 1894: **Behanzin deposed and exiled** — France deposed the last independent king of Dahomey and exiled him to Martinique and later Algeria, where he died in 1906; the kingdom passed under French control. [britannica-behanzin, wiki-behanzin]
- c. 1899–1904: **Colony of French Dahomey** — Dahomey was organised as a French colony with its capital at Porto-Novo and incorporated into the federation of French West Africa, where it remained for six decades. [wiki-french-dahomey, britannica-benin]
- 1 August 1960: **Independence as the Republic of Dahomey** — Dahomey became a fully independent state on 1 August 1960, with Hubert Maga as its first president, ending French colonial rule. [britannica-benin, blackpast-maga]

Figures:

- Behanzin (c. 1845–1906) — Last independent King of Dahomey. Eleventh king of Dahomey, who resisted French encroachment in two wars, burned his own capital of Abomey rather than yield it, and was deposed and exiled after his surrender in 1894, dying in Algeria in 1906.
- Alfred-Amédée Dodds (1842–1922) — French colonial general. Senegalese-born French officer who led the 1892–1894 expedition that conquered the Kingdom of Dahomey, captured Abomey and forced the surrender of King Behanzin.

## Coups, Kérékou, and the People's Republic of Benin — 1960 – 1990

Independent Dahomey became notorious for chronic instability and military coups, until Mathieu Kérékou seized power in 1972, embraced Marxism-Leninism, and in 1975 renamed the country Benin.

- 1963: **The cycle of coups begins** — General Christophe Soglo deposed President Hubert Maga in Dahomey's first military coup, opening more than a decade of chronic instability and repeated seizures of power. [britannica-benin-coups, wiki-1972-coup]
- 26 October 1972: **Kérékou seizes power** — Major Mathieu Kérékou overthrew the Presidential Council in the last of Dahomey's coups, ending the era of regional deadlock and establishing a durable military government. [wiki-1972-coup, britannica-benin-coups]
- 30 November 1974: **Adoption of Marxism-Leninism** — Kérékou proclaimed Marxism-Leninism as the official ideology of the state, nationalising key sectors and aligning the country with the Eastern bloc. [wiki-people-republic, encyclopedia-kerekou]
- 30 November 1975: **Dahomey renamed Benin** — Kérékou renamed the country the People's Republic of Benin, after the Bight of Benin, formally retiring the name Dahomey inherited from the precolonial Fon kingdom. [wiki-people-republic, britannica-benin]
- 1989: **Collapse of the Marxist state** — Amid economic ruin, unpaid wages and mass protest, Kérékou renounced Marxism-Leninism, setting the stage for a negotiated transition to democracy. [wiki-people-republic, encyclopedia-kerekou]

Figures:

- Mathieu Kérékou (1933–2015) — Military ruler and President of Benin. Seized power in 1972, built a Marxist-Leninist one-party state and renamed the country Benin in 1975; after presiding over the 1990 democratic transition he later returned to power through the ballot box, ruling again from 1996 to 2006.
- Hubert Maga (1916–2000) — First President of Dahomey. Northern political leader who became the first president at independence in 1960; deposed in the 1963 coup, he briefly returned to head a rotating Presidential Council before Kérékou's 1972 takeover.

## The National Conference and modern Benin — 1990 – present

Benin's 1990 National Conference produced one of Africa's earliest peaceful transitions to multiparty democracy; today the country is a republic still reckoning with its history, including the restitution of looted royal treasures.

- 19–28 February 1990: **The National Conference** — A sovereign National Conference of the nation's 'living forces' met in Cotonou, stripped Kérékou of power and launched a transitional government — a pioneering model of peaceful democratic transition for Africa. [ifra-national-conference, freedomhouse-benin, wiki-people-republic]
- December 1990: **Multiparty constitution adopted** — Voters approved a new democratic constitution by referendum, establishing a multiparty presidential republic and ending the Marxist one-party state. [wiki-constitution-benin, freedomhouse-benin]
- 1991: **Soglo defeats Kérékou at the polls** — Nicéphore Soglo won the presidential election, and Kérékou peacefully handed over power — a rare democratic alternation in the Africa of the time. [wiki-soglo, freedomhouse-benin]
- 1996: **Kérékou returns by election** — Having peacefully left office, Mathieu Kérékou returned to the presidency by winning the 1996 election, serving until term limits obliged him to step down in 2006. [encyclopedia-kerekou, wiki-kerekou]
- November 2021: **France returns the Abomey treasures** — France formally returned 26 royal artworks looted from the palaces of Abomey during the 1892 conquest, a landmark act of cultural restitution to be displayed in Benin. [france24-restitution, voa-restitution]

Figures:

- Nicéphore Soglo (born 1934) — Transitional Prime Minister and President. Technocrat appointed to lead the transitional government after the 1990 National Conference, he won the 1991 presidential election, presiding over Benin's early democratic era before losing to Kérékou in 1996.
