# The Chronicle of Albania: From Illyrian tribes and Skanderbeg's mountain resistance to a small Balkan state forged on the edge of empires.

Founding: The Declaration of Independence at Vlore — 28 November 1912. Modern Albania dates its statehood to 28 November 1912, when the assembly gathered at Vlore under Ismail Qemali proclaimed Albania an independent and sovereign state and raised the national flag, breaking away from the Ottoman Empire during the First Balkan War. The new state's existence and frontiers were confirmed by the Great Powers at the Conference of London in 1912-13, which recognised an autonomous principality of Albania but, under pressure from Serbia, Montenegro and Greece, fixed its borders so that a large share of the Albanian-populated territory, including Kosovo, was left outside the country. The independence won at Vlore was thus both the founding act of the Albanian state and the origin of a national question that has shaped Albanian politics ever since.

Albania traces its identity to the ancient Illyrians, whose lands along the eastern Adriatic passed under Rome, Byzantium and finally the Ottomans. In the fifteenth century the warrior prince Gjergj Kastrioti, known as Skanderbeg, held the Ottomans off from his stronghold at Kruje for a quarter-century, but after his death the country was absorbed into the Ottoman Empire for nearly five hundred years, during which most Albanians embraced Islam. A nineteenth-century cultural and political revival, the Rilindja, and the League of Prizren of 1878 prepared the ground for statehood, and on 28 November 1912 Ismail Qemali proclaimed independence at Vlore amid the Balkan Wars. The Great Powers recognised Albania at the Conference of London but drew its borders so as to leave roughly half of the Albanian-populated lands, above all Kosovo, outside the new state, creating the enduring Albanian national question. After the reign of King Zog, an Italian invasion in 1939 and the Second World War, the country fell under the long Stalinist dictatorship of Enver Hoxha, who ruled in extreme isolation until 1985. Communism collapsed in 1990-91; after a turbulent transition that included the near-anarchy of 1997, Albania joined NATO in 2009 and became a candidate for European Union membership in 2014.

## Illyrians, Greeks, Rome and Byzantium — Antiquity – c. 1180

On the eastern shore of the Adriatic the Illyrians built the deep substratum of Albanian identity, their cities passing in turn to Greek colonists, Rome and the Byzantine Empire.

- from c. 8th c. BCE: **Illyrian and Greek Butrint** — Illyrian tribes inhabited the eastern Adriatic coast, where Greek colonists from about the eighth century BCE helped develop the settlement of Buthrotum (Butrint) into a flourishing city. [butrint-unesco, albania-medieval-britannica]
- 2nd c. BCE: **Roman conquest of Illyria** — Rome conquered the Illyrians in the second century BCE and absorbed their lands; Butrint flourished under Roman rule with a forum, aqueduct and public buildings. [albania-medieval-britannica, butrint-unesco]
- from late 4th c. CE: **Byzantine rule begins** — After the division of the Roman Empire the Albanian lands passed to the Byzantine Empire, remaining a contested Christian borderland for much of the following eight centuries. [albania-medieval-britannica, albania-britannica]
- 6th c. CE: **Early Christian Butrint** — Butrint became an episcopal seat with an early Christian basilica and a baptistery whose mosaic floor survives, marking the spread of Christianity in the region under Byzantium. [butrint-unesco]

Figures:

- The Illyrians (1st millennium BCE) — Ancient people of the western Balkans. A cluster of tribes and kingdoms on the eastern Adriatic, the Illyrians are regarded in Albanian tradition as the ancestors of the Albanians; their language is thought to underlie modern Albanian, and Rome conquered them in the second century BCE.
- Butrint (Buthrotum) (occupied over many centuries) — Ancient city and World Heritage Site. An Illyrian and Greek foundation that became a Roman colony and later a Byzantine and Venetian stronghold, Butrint preserves theatres, basilicas and fortifications spanning antiquity to the Middle Ages and was inscribed by UNESCO in 1992.

## Medieval Albania, the Ottoman Conquest and Skanderbeg — c. 1180 – 1479

Out of the decline of Byzantium rose Albanian principalities, soon faced with the advancing Ottomans; for a quarter-century Skanderbeg held them off from his fortress at Kruje.

- 1388: **Ottoman invasion of Albania** — The Ottoman Turks invaded Albania in 1388 and, over the following decades, brought most of the divided and contested country under their control, largely completing the conquest by about 1430. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-medieval-britannica]
- 1443: **Skanderbeg's revolt and the return to Kruje** — Gjergj Kastrioti (Skanderbeg) broke with the Ottomans after 1443, reconverted to Christianity and seized his ancestral fortress of Kruje, launching a national revolt against Ottoman rule. [skanderbeg-britannica, history-of-albania-britannica]
- 1444: **League of Lezhe** — Skanderbeg united Albanian princes in the League of Lezhe to coordinate resistance, giving the scattered lordships a measure of common purpose against the Ottoman Empire. [skanderbeg-britannica]
- 1450 & 1466-67: **The sieges of Kruje** — From his stronghold at Kruje Skanderbeg withstood repeated Ottoman sieges, including those of 1450 and 1466-67, keeping much of Albania free for some twenty-five years. [skanderbeg-britannica, history-of-albania-britannica]
- 1468: **Death of Skanderbeg** — Skanderbeg died at Lezhe in 1468; without his leadership Albanian resistance collapsed, and the Ottomans took Kruje in 1478 and Shkoder in 1479, completing the conquest. [skanderbeg-britannica, history-of-albania-britannica]

Figures:

- Skanderbeg (Gjergj Kastrioti) (1405 – 1468) — Prince and national hero. Albania's national hero, Skanderbeg defected from Ottoman service in 1443, seized Kruje and for a quarter-century led the Albanian resistance, frustrating every Ottoman attempt to reconquer the country and winning the admiration and aid of Christian Europe.
- Sultan Mehmed II (1432 – 1481) — Ottoman sultan. The conqueror of Constantinople, Mehmed II personally led campaigns against Skanderbeg, including the great siege of Kruje in 1466-67; only after Skanderbeg's death did Ottoman forces finally subdue Albania.

## Five Centuries of Ottoman Rule and the National Awakening — 1479 – 1912

Nearly five hundred years within the Ottoman Empire reshaped Albania's religion and society; in the nineteenth century a cultural and political revival turned a people into a nation.

- from 1479: **Albania within the Ottoman Empire** — Following the fall of Kruje and Shkoder, Albania was incorporated into the Ottoman Empire, beginning nearly five centuries of rule during which most Albanians converted to Islam. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-britannica]
- 18th-19th c.: **Ottoman towns of Berat and Gjirokastra** — The towns of Berat and Gjirokastra developed the distinctive Ottoman-era urban architecture, including fortified citadels and tall stone houses, that later earned them UNESCO World Heritage status. [berat-gjirokastra-unesco]
- 19th c.: **The Rilindja national awakening** — A cultural and political revival, the Rilindja, promoted the Albanian language, literature and a distinct national identity, led by writers and patriots such as the poet Naim Frasheri. [albanian-nationalism-britannica, naim-frasheri-britannica]
- 1 Jul 1878: **League of Prizren** — Founded at Prizren in response to the Congress of Berlin, the Albanian League sought to unify the Albanian lands into one autonomous unit and to resist their partition among neighbouring states. [albanian-league-britannica, albanian-nationalism-britannica]

Figures:

- Naim Frasheri (1846 – 1900) — Poet of the national awakening. Regarded as Albania's national poet, Naim Frasheri was a leading figure of the Rilindja, writing works that celebrated Albanian history and language and helped forge a modern national consciousness.
- Abdyl Frasheri (1839 – 1892) — Leader of the League of Prizren. Brother of the poet Naim, Abdyl Frasheri was a leading political voice of the national awakening and a driving force of the League of Prizren, championing Albanian autonomy within the Ottoman Empire.

## Independence, the Conference of London and the Albanian Question — 1912 – 1939

Independence proclaimed at Vlore was secured only as the Great Powers drew borders that left half the Albanians outside the state; a fragile kingdom followed, ended by Italian invasion.

- 28 Nov 1912: **Declaration of independence at Vlore** — An assembly at Vlore led by Ismail Qemali proclaimed Albania an independent state and raised the national flag, breaking from the Ottoman Empire during the First Balkan War. [vlore-proclamation-britannica, qemali-rtsh, history-of-albania-britannica]
- 1912-13: **Conference of London and the borders** — The Great Powers recognised an autonomous Albania but, pressed by its neighbours, drew borders leaving about half the Albanian-populated lands, including Kosovo, outside the state, creating the Albanian national question. [treaties-of-london-britannica, qemali-rtsh, history-of-albania-britannica]
- 1925-1928: **The rise of Zog** — Ahmed Zogu consolidated power, becoming president in 1925 and in 1928 proclaiming himself King Zog I, ruling an authoritarian monarchy increasingly dependent on Fascist Italy. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-britannica]
- 7 Apr 1939: **Italian invasion of Albania** — Fascist Italy invaded Albania on 7 April 1939; King Zog fled into exile and the Italian king Victor Emmanuel III was proclaimed king of Albania, ending its independence. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-britannica, albania-us-relations]

Figures:

- Ismail Qemali (1844 – 1919) — Founder of independent Albania. A veteran Ottoman statesman, Ismail Qemali led the assembly at Vlore that proclaimed Albanian independence on 28 November 1912 and headed the first provisional government of the new state.
- King Zog I (1895 – 1961) — President and King of the Albanians. Born Ahmed Zogu, he dominated interwar Albania, becoming president in 1925 and king in 1928. His monarchy modernised the state but fell to the Italian invasion of 1939, after which he lived in exile.

## The Second World War and Hoxha's Stalinist Albania — 1939 – 1990

Out of resistance to Italian and German occupation came a communist victory; for over four decades Enver Hoxha ruled the most isolated and tightly controlled state in Europe.

- 1944: **Communist victory and Hoxha in power** — As German forces withdrew, the communist-led partisans took Tirana and formed a provisional government under Enver Hoxha, who would rule Albania for over four decades. [enver-hoxha-britannica, history-of-albania-britannica]
- 1948 & 1961: **Breaks with Yugoslavia and the USSR** — Hoxha broke with Tito's Yugoslavia in 1948 and allied with the Soviet Union, then ruptured with Moscow in 1961 over de-Stalinisation, turning instead to Maoist China. [enver-hoxha-britannica]
- 1967: **Albania declared an atheist state** — The Hoxha regime launched a campaign against religion, closing mosques and churches and proclaiming Albania the world's first officially atheist state. [enver-hoxha-britannica, history-of-albania-britannica]
- late 1970s: **Rupture with China and total isolation** — Hoxha broke with post-Maoist China in the late 1970s, leaving Albania almost completely isolated, its borders sealed and its economy self-reliant by decree. [enver-hoxha-britannica]
- 1985: **Death of Enver Hoxha** — Hoxha died in 1985 after ruling for over forty years, leaving the most tightly controlled society in Europe to his successor Ramiz Alia. [enver-hoxha-britannica]

Figures:

- Enver Hoxha (1908 – 1985) — Communist dictator of Albania. Founder of the Albanian Communist Party and ruler from 1944 to 1985, Hoxha imposed a Stalinist regime of extreme repression and isolation, breaking successively with Yugoslavia, the Soviet Union and China and proclaiming Albania an atheist state.
- Ramiz Alia (1925 – 2011) — Hoxha's successor and last communist leader. Hoxha's chosen heir, Alia led Albania after 1985 and presided over the unravelling of communist rule, conceding political pluralism in late 1990 under mounting popular pressure.

## Transition, the 1997 Crisis, NATO and Europe — 1990 – present

From the fall of communism through the near-collapse of 1997, Albania rebuilt a democratic state, joined NATO and sought European Union membership while its ties to Kosovo deepened.

- Mar 1991: **End of one-party rule** — After the Tirana student protests of December 1990, Albania held its first multiparty elections in March 1991, and the Party of Labour's monopoly on power ended with the 1992 elections. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-britannica]
- 15 Mar 1991: **US-Albania relations resumed** — The United States and Albania resumed diplomatic relations on 15 March 1991 after a break of more than half a century dating to the Italian takeover of 1939. [albania-us-relations]
- 1997: **Pyramid scheme collapse and unrest** — The collapse of fraudulent pyramid investment schemes in early 1997 wiped out the savings of many Albanians and triggered armed unrest that brought the state close to anarchy before order was restored. [history-of-albania-britannica, albania-britannica]
- 17 Feb 2008: **Kosovo declares independence** — Kosovo, whose population is overwhelmingly ethnic Albanian and which was left outside Albania in 1913, declared independence on 17 February 2008, recognised the next day by the United States. [kosovo-state-history, kosovo-britannica]
- 1 Apr 2009: **Albania joins NATO** — Albania, with Croatia, formally joined NATO on 1 April 2009 as the alliance's 27th and 28th members, completing its strategic reorientation toward the West. [nato-albania-accession, nato-enlargement-crs]
- Jun 2014: **EU candidate status** — Albania, which applied for membership in April 2009, was granted European Union candidate status in June 2014; accession negotiations were formally opened in July 2022. [eu-albania-enlargement]

Figures:

- Ramiz Alia (1925 – 2011) — Last communist head of state. Successor to Hoxha, Alia conceded political pluralism in December 1990 under popular pressure, opening the way to multiparty elections and the end of one-party rule in Albania.
- Ismail Qemali (1844 – 1919) — Symbol of the national question. The founder of independent Albania remains the emblem of the unfinished national question: the state he proclaimed at Vlore in 1912 left half the Albanian nation, above all in Kosovo, beyond its 1913 borders.
