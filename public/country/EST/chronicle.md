Founding: The Declaration of Independence of the Republic of Estonia — 24 February 1918. The Republic of Estonia was proclaimed on 24 February 1918, when the Salvation Committee of the Estonian Provincial Assembly issued the 'Manifesto to the Peoples of Estonia' and appointed a provisional government under Konstantin Päts. After centuries of foreign rule the Estonians declared themselves an independent, democratic republic. Independence still had to be won by force: the War of Independence against Soviet Russia and Baltic German forces was fought through 1918-1920 and ended in the Treaty of Tartu of 2 February 1920, by which Soviet Russia recognised Estonian sovereignty in perpetuity. That republic was destroyed by Soviet occupation and annexation in 1940, but Estonia never accepted the legality of the act; on 20 August 1991, invoking the unbroken continuity of the state founded in 1918, the country restored its independence rather than founding a new one.

Estonia's history is the story of a small Finno-Ugric people who held the northeastern edge of Europe long before they held a state of their own. Conquered in the thirteenth century by German crusaders and Danes in the Northern Crusades, the land of the Estonians passed through Danish, Swedish and, after 1710-1721, Russian imperial hands, while a Baltic German nobility ruled over a peasant population for some seven centuries. A nineteenth-century national awakening, carried by newspapers and great song festivals, turned a peasantry into a nation. Independence was declared on 24 February 1918 and secured in the War of Independence, sealed by the Treaty of Tartu of 1920, in which Soviet Russia recognised Estonian independence forever. That first republic was extinguished in 1940 when the Soviet Union, acting on the secret protocols of the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, occupied and annexed the country, an act the Western democracies never recognised as lawful. Through Soviet and Nazi occupations, mass deportations and forty-seven years of Soviet rule, Estonia's legal existence was kept alive abroad, and in the Singing Revolution the nation sang itself back to freedom, restoring its independence on 20 August 1991 as the very same state founded in 1918, and joining NATO and the European Union in 2004.

## The Ancient Estonians and the Northern Crusades — c. 9000 BCE – 1346

A Finno-Ugric people held the northeastern Baltic for millennia until the thirteenth-century crusades dragged them, last of Europe's pagans, into Latin Christendom.

- c. 9000 BCE: **The Kunda culture** — Mesolithic hunter-fishers of the Kunda culture, named for the Estonian town of Kunda, settled the eastern Baltic forest zone after the Ice Age, among the earliest inhabitants of the region. [ancient-estonia-ref]
- 1202: **Foundation of the Brothers of the Sword** — Bishop Albert of Riga established the Livonian Brothers of the Sword, the military order that, with the Teutonic Knights and the Danes, drove the Northern Crusades into Estonia. [northern-crusades-britannica, livonian-crusade-ref]
- 1219: **Danish conquest and the founding of Tallinn** — Danish crusaders defeated the northern Estonians and built a fortress on Toompea hill, giving rise to Tallinn (Reval); northern Estonia became a Danish duchy within Terra Mariana. [tallinn-unesco, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1343–1345: **St George's Night Uprising** — Estonian peasants rose against their German and Danish overlords in a great revolt that was suppressed; in its wake Denmark sold its Estonian lands to the Teutonic Order in 1346. [estonia-history-britannica, ancient-estonia-ref]

Figures:

- Lembitu (died 1217) — Estonian elder and war leader. An elder of the Sakala county and the best-remembered leader of Estonian resistance to the crusaders, Lembitu fell in battle in 1217 against the Brothers of the Sword, becoming a lasting symbol of the ancient Estonians' fight for freedom.
- Bishop Albert of Riga (c. 1165 – 1229) — Founder of crusading Livonia. Bishop of Riga and architect of the conquest of the eastern Baltic, Albert founded the Livonian Brothers of the Sword in 1202 and organised the crusading campaigns that brought Christianity and German rule to Estonia.

## Hanseatic Reval, Danish and Swedish Estonia — 1346 – 1710

Beneath its German lords Tallinn grew rich as a Hanseatic port, and after the Livonian War the land passed from the crumbling crusader state to Sweden, remembered as the 'good old Swedish time'.

- 1285: **Reval joins the Hanseatic League** — Tallinn, known as Reval, entered the Hanseatic League and grew into a wealthy port linking Russian trade to the markets of the Baltic and the North Sea. [tallinn-unesco]
- 1558–1583: **The Livonian War** — A twenty-five-year struggle for Livonia pitted Russia against Sweden, Denmark and Poland-Lithuania, shattering the old crusader confederation as the towns and provinces sought new protectors. [livonian-war-britannica, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1561: **Reval submits to Sweden** — Amid the collapse of the Livonian Order, the city council of Reval and the nobles of northern Estonia placed themselves under the protection of the Swedish crown, the start of Swedish rule. [livonian-war-britannica, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1632: **Founding of the University of Tartu** — King Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden founded the university at Tartu (Dorpat), a lasting institution of learning and a hallmark of the era later recalled as the 'good old Swedish time'. [estonia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Gustavus Adolphus (1594 – 1632) — King of Sweden. The Swedish warrior-king who consolidated his realm's hold on the eastern Baltic and in 1632 founded the University of Tartu, a foundation that embodied the comparatively benign reputation of Swedish rule among later Estonians.
- Peter the Great (1672 – 1725) — Tsar of Russia. Tsar of Russia whose armies overran the Swedish Baltic provinces in the Great Northern War. Estonia capitulated to him in 1710, and the Treaty of Nystad of 1721 confirmed its passage to the Russian Empire he proclaimed.

## The Russian Empire and the National Awakening — 1710 – 1917

Ceded to Russia by the Treaty of Nystad, the Estonian lands lived under tsar and Baltic baron until a nineteenth-century awakening, carried by newspapers and song festivals, turned peasants into a nation.

- 1721: **Treaty of Nystad** — Sweden ceded Estonia and Livonia to Russia, which organised them into governorates and confirmed the privileges of the Baltic German nobility, beginning two centuries of Russian imperial rule. [nystad-britannica, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1816–1819: **Abolition of serfdom** — Serfdom was abolished in the Estonian and Livonian governorates ahead of the rest of Russia, freeing the peasantry and laying the social groundwork for the later national awakening. [estonia-history-britannica]
- 1869: **The first Estonian Song Festival** — The first all-Estonian Song Festival was held at Tartu, founded by Johann Voldemar Jannsen, making mass choral singing the central expression of an awakening national identity. [song-festival-ref, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1878: **Jakobson and the national press** — Carl Robert Jakobson founded the newspaper Sakala, a leading voice of the national awakening that demanded equal rights for Estonians and an end to Baltic German privilege. [jakobson-ref, estonia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Carl Robert Jakobson (1841 – 1882) — Journalist and national leader. A leading figure of the Estonian national awakening, Jakobson founded the political newspaper Sakala in 1878 and led the radical wing that demanded equal rights with the Baltic Germans, shaping the economic and political programme of the national movement.
- Johann Voldemar Jannsen (1819 – 1890) — Publisher and founder of the Song Festival. Journalist and publisher who initiated the first Estonian Song Festival in 1869 and helped popularise the very name 'Estonians' for a people long called simply 'country folk', founding a tradition that became the heartbeat of national identity.

## Independence, the War of Independence and the First Republic — 1917 – 1939

Declared on 24 February 1918 and won in arms against Soviet Russia, Estonian independence was sealed by the Treaty of Tartu, in which Russia renounced its rights over Estonia forever.

- 24 Feb 1918: **Declaration of Independence** — The Salvation Committee proclaimed the independent Republic of Estonia and appointed a provisional government under Konstantin Päts, the founding act of the Estonian state. [independence-decl-ref, estonia-history-britannica]
- 1918–1920: **The War of Independence** — The Estonian army, with Finnish, British and other allied support, repelled the Soviet Red Army and the Baltic German Landeswehr, securing the survival of the new republic. [independence-decl-ref, estonia-history-britannica]
- 2 Feb 1920: **Treaty of Tartu** — Soviet Russia recognised Estonian independence de jure and renounced 'voluntarily and forever' all sovereign rights over Estonia, ending the war and fixing the eastern border at Narva and Petseri. [tartu-peace-vm, tartu-tartu100, tartu-britannica]
- 1934: **The 'era of silence'** — Amid depression and political instability, Konstantin Päts assumed authoritarian powers, suspending parliamentary politics in an interlude later called the 'era of silence'. [estonia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Konstantin Päts (1874 – 1956) — Founding statesman and President. Head of the provisional government proclaimed in 1918 and a dominant figure of the first republic, Päts became its first president; he assumed authoritarian powers in 1934 and, after the 1940 Soviet occupation, was deported to Russia, where he died in 1956.
- Jaan Poska (1866 – 1920) — Foreign minister and chief negotiator at Tartu. Estonia's foreign minister and the head of its delegation at the Tartu peace talks, Poska secured Soviet Russia's recognition of independence in 1920, calling it 'the most important day of the past 700 years for Estonia'.

## Occupation, Annexation and the Long Night — 1939 – 1987

Assigned to Stalin by the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, Estonia was occupied, annexed and deported into the Soviet Union, an erasure the Western democracies refused to recognise as lawful.

- 23 Aug 1939: **The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact** — The secret protocol of the German-Soviet pact assigned Estonia to the Soviet sphere of influence, sealing the country's fate without its knowledge or consent. [mrp-ref, estonia-occupation-britannica]
- Jun–Aug 1940: **Soviet occupation and annexation** — The Red Army occupied Estonia on 16-17 June 1940 and, after a staged election, the country was annexed into the Soviet Union in August, ending the independent republic. [estonia-occupation-britannica, mrp-ref]
- 14 Jun 1941: **The June deportation** — Soviet authorities deported more than 10,000 people from Estonia to Siberia in a single operation, most of them women, children and the elderly, days before the German invasion. [deportations-valitsus, estonia-occupation-britannica]
- 1944: **Soviet reoccupation** — After three years of Nazi occupation, the Soviet Union reconquered Estonia in 1944, beginning a second occupation marked by further mass deportations and armed 'Forest Brother' resistance. [estonia-occupation-britannica, deportations-valitsus]
- from 1940: **Western non-recognition** — Following the Stimson Doctrine, the United States and many other states refused to recognise the Soviet annexation de jure, holding that Estonia remained a sovereign state under occupation. [continuity-icds, estonia-occupation-britannica]

Figures:

- Jüri Uluots (1890 – 1945) — Last prewar prime minister and continuity claimant. Estonia's last lawful prime minister before the occupation, Uluots maintained the constitutional continuity of the republic during the war, asserting governmental authority under the 1938 constitution before dying in exile in 1945.
- August Rei (1886 – 1963) — Diplomat and government-in-exile leader. An Estonian statesman and diplomat who headed the government of the republic in exile, embodying the legal continuity of the state that Western non-recognition of the annexation kept alive through the Soviet decades.

## The Singing Revolution, Restoration and the Return to Europe — 1987 – present

Estonia sang its way out of the Soviet Union, restored the independence of 1991 as the very same state of 1918, and anchored itself in NATO and the European Union.

- Sep 1988: **The Singing Revolution** — Some 300,000 people gathered at the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds in mass patriotic singing; the artist Heinz Valk named the movement the 'Singing Revolution'. [singing-revolution-ew, restoration-err]
- 23 Aug 1989: **The Baltic Way** — On the pact's fiftieth anniversary, about two million people formed a 600-kilometre human chain from Tallinn to Vilnius, demanding independence and condemning the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. [singing-revolution-ew, mrp-ref]
- 20 Aug 1991: **Restoration of independence** — The Supreme Council voted to restore Estonian independence on the basis of legal continuity with the 1918 republic during the failed Moscow coup; recognition and UN membership swiftly followed. [restoration-err, continuity-icds]
- 29 Mar 2004: **Accession to NATO** — Estonia became a full member of NATO when its instruments of accession were deposited in Washington, anchoring its security in the Western alliance. [nato-mfa]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to the European Union** — Estonia joined the European Union, completing its post-Soviet return to Europe after a 2003 referendum in which two-thirds of voters approved membership. [eu-mfa-timeline]
- 2005 & 2014: **The unratified border treaty** — A border treaty with Russia, settling the post-war frontier at Narva and Petseri, was signed in 2005 and again in 2014 but remains unratified, leaving the boundary formally unconcluded. [border-trialoog, border-err]

Figures:

- Heinz Valk (born 1936) — Artist and Singing Revolution figure. The artist and activist who coined the term 'Singing Revolution' and gave the movement its enduring slogan, 'One day, no matter what, we will win', voicing the peaceful mass mobilisation that carried Estonia to freedom.
- Lennart Meri (1929 – 2006) — Writer and first president of the restored republic. Writer, film-maker and statesman deported as a child in 1941, Meri served as foreign minister and then as the first president of the restored Estonia, championing its integration into Europe and the transatlantic alliance.
