Founding: Independence from Portugal — 11 November 1975. Angola became a sovereign state on 11 November 1975, when, following the 1974 Carnation Revolution in Lisbon and the collapse of Portugal's colonial empire, the Popular Movement for the Liberation of Angola (MPLA) proclaimed the People's Republic of Angola in Luanda under its leader Agostinho Neto, who became the country's first president. That moment rested atop a deep precolonial past — the Kingdom of Kongo to the north, the Kimbundu kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba where Queen Njinga resisted the Portuguese in the seventeenth century, and the Ovimbundu and Lunda states of the interior. Portuguese presence began with the founding of Luanda in 1576, after which Angola became a major source of enslaved Africans for the Atlantic trade, especially to Brazil; the conquest of the interior was only completed in the early twentieth century. Independence in 1975 was contested between three rival liberation movements and ushered in a civil war that would last until 2002.

The land that became Angola was home to powerful Central African polities long before European arrival: the great Kingdom of Kongo, whose influence reached the northern reaches of present-day Angola, and the Kimbundu-speaking kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, where in the seventeenth century Queen Njinga (Nzinga) Mbande waged a celebrated decades-long resistance against Portuguese encroachment. Further inland lay the Ovimbundu kingdoms of the central highlands and the Lunda states of the east. After Paulo Dias de Novais founded Luanda in 1576, Angola became, over the following centuries, one of the principal sources of enslaved Africans for the Atlantic trade, above all to Portuguese Brazil. The Portuguese conquest of the interior was slow and bloody, completed only in the early twentieth century. Anti-colonial revolt broke out in 1961, fought by the rival movements MPLA, FNLA and UNITA, and Angola won independence on 11 November 1975 under the MPLA's Agostinho Neto. That triumph gave way at once to a long Cold War-era civil war between the MPLA and Jonas Savimbi's UNITA, drawing in Cuban, Soviet, South African and United States forces, which ended only with Savimbi's death in 2002. Modern Angola, rebuilt on its vast oil wealth, has remained under MPLA rule.

## Kongo, Ndongo and the kingdoms of Central Africa — c. 1300 – 1576

Before the Portuguese arrived, the region was shaped by the great Kingdom of Kongo to the north and the Kimbundu kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, with the Ovimbundu and Lunda peoples taking form in the interior.

- 1483: **Portuguese reach the Kingdom of Kongo** — The navigator Diogo Cão reached the mouth of the Congo River and made contact with the Kingdom of Kongo, opening relations between this powerful Central African monarchy and Portugal. [britannica-angola, worldhistory-angola, wiki-kongo]
- c. 1491: **Kongo adopts Christianity** — The Kongo ruler was baptised and, under his son Afonso I, the kingdom embraced Catholicism, literacy and sustained diplomatic ties with Portugal and the papacy. [wiki-kongo, britannica-angola]
- c. 15th–16th centuries: **Rise of Ndongo under the ngola** — The Kimbundu-speaking Kingdom of Ndongo grew in strength under its ruler the ngola, asserting independence from Kongo; the title would give the future colony its name. [wiki-ndongo, britannica-mbundu, britannica-angola]
- c. 16th–17th centuries: **Ovimbundu and Lunda states take shape** — In the central highlands the Ovimbundu kingdoms emerged, while in the east the Lunda states built a wide commercial and political network across the interior savanna. [britannica-angola, countrystudies-ovimbundu]

## Luanda, Queen Njinga and the Atlantic slave trade — 1576 – 1700

The Portuguese founded Luanda in 1576 and pressed inland for captives, provoking the long resistance of Queen Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba, as Angola became a heartland of the Atlantic slave trade to Brazil.

- 1576: **Portuguese found Luanda** — Paulo Dias de Novais established São Paulo da Assunção de Loanda on the Angolan coast, creating the base from which Portuguese power and the slave trade would expand. [blackpast-luanda, wiki-portuguese-angola, worldhistory-angola]
- 1617: **Founding of Benguela** — The Portuguese established the port of Benguela to the south, which would become a second major outlet for the export of enslaved Africans to the Americas. [wiki-portuguese-angola, wiki-slavery-angola]
- 1624: **Njinga becomes queen of Ndongo** — After leading a diplomatic mission to the Portuguese in 1621, Njinga Mbande took the throne of Ndongo, beginning a decades-long struggle against Portuguese expansion. [wiki-nzinga, oxford-njinga, britannica-angola]
- c. 1631: **Njinga rules from Matamba** — Driven from Ndongo by the Portuguese, Njinga rebuilt her power in the eastern kingdom of Matamba, from which she resisted, raided and negotiated with the colonisers until peace in 1656. [wiki-nzinga, oxford-njinga]
- 1641: **Atlantic slave trade at its height** — Luanda became the leading slave-trading port of the entire Atlantic, funnelling vast numbers of captives seized in interior wars to the Americas, overwhelmingly to Brazil. [slavevoyages, blackpast-luanda, wiki-slavery-angola]

Figures:

- Njinga (Nzinga) Mbande (c. 1583–1663) — Queen of Ndongo and Matamba. Ruler whose diplomatic skill and decades of armed resistance against the Portuguese made her the most celebrated figure of early Angolan history, remembered as a symbol of African resistance.

## The slow conquest of the interior — 1700 – 1930

For centuries Portuguese control scarcely reached beyond the coast and trade routes; only after the Berlin Conference did a slow, violent conquest bring the Ovimbundu, Lunda and other interior peoples under colonial rule by the early twentieth century.

- 1836: **Portugal abolishes the slave trade** — Portugal formally outlawed the transatlantic slave trade, ending Luanda's centuries as a slaving port, though clandestine traffic and later forced labour persisted in Angola. [wiki-slavery-angola, wiki-portuguese-angola]
- 1884–1885: **Berlin Conference and the Scramble** — The Berlin Conference required Portugal to demonstrate effective occupation of Angola, spurring the military campaigns that would subdue the independent interior kingdoms. [wiki-portuguese-angola, britannica-angola]
- c. 1890–1904: **Conquest of the Ovimbundu kingdoms** — Portuguese forces conquered the Ovimbundu kingdoms of the central highlands, bringing one of Angola's largest peoples under colonial control. [countrystudies-ovimbundu, britannica-angola]
- 1904: **Battle of the Cunene** — Heavy fighting against the Kwanhama (Ovambo) in the south marked the violent and protracted character of the Portuguese conquest of Angola's interior frontier. [wiki-history-angola, britannica-angola]
- by the 1920s: **Effective occupation completed** — Only in the early twentieth century, with pacification campaigns continuing into the 1920s, did Portugal establish effective control over the whole of Angola. [wiki-history-angola, britannica-angola]

## Revolt, liberation movements and independence — 1961 – 1975

Armed revolt broke out in 1961 and was carried forward by three rival movements — the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA — until Portugal's 1974 revolution opened the way to independence under the MPLA on 11 November 1975.

- January–March 1961: **Revolt breaks out** — The Baixa de Cassanje cotton workers' revolt, the 4 February attack on a Luanda prison, and the UPA's 15 March uprising in the north marked the start of Angola's war of independence against Portugal. [wiki-history-angola, britannica-angola, museu-cassanje]
- 1966: **Founding of UNITA** — Jonas Savimbi founded the National Union for the Total Independence of Angola, drawing support from the Ovimbundu and creating the third of the rival liberation movements alongside the MPLA and FNLA. [wiki-unita, britannica-mpla]
- 25 April 1974: **Carnation Revolution in Portugal** — A military coup overthrew Portugal's dictatorship, and the new government moved to decolonise, opening the path to Angolan independence. [statedept-angola-crisis, britannica-angola]
- January 1975: **Alvor Agreement** — Portugal, the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA signed the Alvor Agreement, providing for a transitional government and independence, but trust between the movements quickly collapsed into open conflict. [statedept-angola-crisis, britannica-angola]
- 11 November 1975: **Independence proclaimed** — As the Portuguese departed, the MPLA proclaimed the People's Republic of Angola in Luanda under Agostinho Neto, marking the country's independence amid a contest with the rival movements. [statedept-angola-crisis, aaregistry-independence, britannica-angola]

Figures:

- Agostinho Neto (1922–1979) — Independence leader and first President of Angola. Poet, physician and leader of the MPLA who proclaimed Angola's independence on 11 November 1975 and served as its first president until his death in 1979.
- Jonas Savimbi (1934–2002) — Founder and leader of UNITA. Founder of UNITA in 1966 and its leader through the war of independence and the long civil war, in which he fought the MPLA with Western and South African backing until his death in 2002.

## The long civil war — 1975 – 2002

From the moment of independence, Angola was plunged into a twenty-seven-year civil war between the MPLA government and Savimbi's UNITA — a Cold War proxy conflict drawing in Cuban, Soviet, South African and United States forces — that ended only with Savimbi's death in 2002.

- October–November 1975: **Cold War intervention** — As civil war erupted, South Africa invaded the south and Cuba sent troops to support the MPLA, turning Angola into a major proxy battleground between the superpower blocs. [statedept-angola-crisis, wiki-civil-war, blackpast-civil-war]
- 1987–1988: **Battle of Cuito Cuanavale** — Cuban, Angolan and South African forces fought a major conventional battle around Cuito Cuanavale, a turning point that helped lead to negotiated foreign troop withdrawals. [wiki-civil-war, blackpast-civil-war]
- 1991: **Bicesse Accords** — A ceasefire under the Bicesse Accords legalised UNITA as a party and set the stage for multiparty elections, briefly raising hopes of an end to the war. [wiki-civil-war, sahistory-civil-war]
- 1992: **Disputed elections and renewed war** — The MPLA's dos Santos led UNITA's Savimbi in Angola's first multiparty elections, but Savimbi rejected the result and returned to war, plunging the country back into conflict. [blackpast-civil-war, wiki-civil-war]
- 22 February 2002: **Death of Savimbi ends the war** — Jonas Savimbi was killed in an ambush by government forces; within weeks UNITA and the MPLA signed the Luena Memorandum, ending the twenty-seven-year civil war. [blackpast-civil-war, wiki-civil-war, sahistory-civil-war]

Figures:

- José Eduardo dos Santos (1942–2022) — President of Angola (1979–2017). Succeeded Neto as MPLA leader and president in 1979, led the government through the civil war to victory in 2002, and ruled for thirty-eight years before stepping down in 2017.

## Peace and oil-rich modern Angola — 2002 – present

Since 2002 Angola has rebuilt on the strength of its vast oil wealth, remaining under MPLA rule, with a long-delayed leadership transition in 2017 from José Eduardo dos Santos to João Lourenço.

- 2002 onward: **Reconstruction and the oil boom** — Peace allowed Angola to rebuild on the back of surging oil revenues, driving rapid growth in the 2000s amid persistent inequality and corruption. [britannica-angola, wiki-history-angola]
- 2007: **Angola joins OPEC** — Angola became a member of OPEC, underscoring its standing as one of Africa's leading oil producers and the centrality of crude oil to its economy. [wiki-history-angola, globalist-dos-santos]
- 2017: **Lourenço succeeds dos Santos** — After thirty-eight years in power, José Eduardo dos Santos stepped down and João Lourenço became president, with the MPLA retaining control of the government. [globalist-dos-santos, britannica-dos-santos]

Figures:

- João Lourenço (born 1954) — President of Angola (since 2017). Former defence minister who succeeded dos Santos in 2017, launching anti-corruption drives and efforts to diversify Angola's oil-dependent economy while keeping the MPLA in power.
