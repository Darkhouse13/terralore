Founding: Independence of the Republic of Uzbekistan — 1 September 1991. On 31 August 1991, amid the collapse of the Soviet Union, the Supreme Council of the Uzbek SSR in Tashkent adopted a declaration of state independence; 1 September is marked as Independence Day. A national referendum on 29 December 1991 overwhelmingly confirmed independence, and Islam Karimov, the republic's Communist-era leader, was elected the first president of the new state. The modern republic is the latest chapter in a history stretching back through the Uzbek khanates, the empire of Timur, the Islamic Golden Age of Samanid Bukhara and the ancient civilisation of Sogdiana.

Uzbekistan occupies the historic heart of Central Asia, the land the Greeks knew as Sogdiana and Bactria, where the oasis cities of Samarkand and Bukhara grew rich as crossroads of the Silk Road. Conquered by Alexander the Great and later by Arab armies, the region became a jewel of the Islamic Golden Age under the Samanids of Bukhara before the Mongols of Genghis Khan laid it waste. From this turmoil rose Timur (Tamerlane), who made Samarkand the dazzling capital of an empire and patron of a cultural renaissance. After centuries under the Uzbek khanates, the Russian conquest and Soviet rule reshaped the land into a cotton monoculture that drained the Aral Sea, until Uzbekistan declared independence on 1 September 1991 and, after the long authoritarian rule of Islam Karimov, began a cautious reformist opening under Shavkat Mirziyoyev.

## Sogdiana, Bactria and the Silk Road — c. 1000 BCE – 651 CE

On the fertile valleys between the Oxus and the Jaxartes rose the ancient lands of Sogdiana and Bactria, whose oasis cities became the great merchant crossroads of the Silk Road.

- c. 1000–500 BCE: **Sogdiana settled and under Achaemenid rule** — The Zeravshan valley of Sogdiana, centred on Maracanda (Samarkand), is settled and passes under the Achaemenid Persian Empire; Bukhara grows as a Silk Road trade and crafts centre. [britannica-sogdiana, britannica-samarkand]
- 329 BCE: **Alexander the Great conquers Sogdiana** — Alexander the Great captures Maracanda (Samarkand) and advances to the Jaxartes, overcoming two years of fierce Sogdian resistance under Spitamenes. [britannica-samarkand, worldhistory-alexander]
- 327 BCE: **Alexander marries Roxana of Bactria** — To cement his conquest, Alexander marries Roxana, daughter of the Bactrian chieftain Oxyartes, symbolising the union of Macedonian and Central Asian peoples. [britannica-roxana]
- 5th–8th centuries CE: **Sogdians dominate Silk Road trade** — Sogdian merchants from Samarkand and Bukhara become the leading caravan traders of the Silk Road, their language a lingua franca of Inner Asian commerce and culture. [britannica-sogdiana]

Figures:

- Spitamenes (died c. 328 BCE) — Sogdian war leader against Alexander. A Sogdian nobleman who led the most determined resistance to Alexander the Great's conquest of Central Asia, waging a guerrilla war that delayed Macedonian control of Sogdiana for two years.

## The Arab Conquest and the Samanid Golden Age — 651 – 999 CE

Arab armies brought Islam across the Oxus, and under the Persian Samanid dynasty Bukhara became a rival to Baghdad and a beacon of the Islamic Golden Age.

- c. 706–712 CE: **Arab conquest of Bukhara and Samarkand** — The Umayyad commander Qutaybah ibn Muslim takes Bukhara and Samarkand and annexes Transoxiana, beginning the Islamisation of the region beyond the Oxus. [britannica-qutaybah, britannica-bukhara]
- 751 CE: **Battle of Talas** — Abbasid Arab forces defeat the army of Tang China at the Battle of Talas, confirming Islam's hold on Central Asia and, by tradition, spreading papermaking westward. [britannica-qutaybah]
- 9th–10th centuries CE: **Samanid Bukhara, a centre of the Islamic Golden Age** — The Persian Samanid dynasty makes Bukhara its capital, a rival to Baghdad whose court fosters Persian literature, science and the refined art and architecture of the Islamic Golden Age. [britannica-samanid, britannica-bukhara]
- 810–870 CE: **Imam al-Bukhari and the Sahih** — Muhammad al-Bukhari, born in Bukhara, compiles the Sahih, accepted by Sunni Muslims as the most authoritative collection of the Prophet's sayings after the Qur'an. [britannica-albukhari]

Figures:

- Avicenna (Ibn Sina) (c. 980–1037 CE) — Physician and philosopher of the Samanid era. Born near Bukhara and educated at the Samanid court, Avicenna became one of the foremost thinkers of the Islamic Golden Age. His Canon of Medicine remained a standard medical text in Europe and the Islamic world for centuries.
- Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870 CE) — Compiler of hadith. A native of Bukhara and one of the greatest Muslim scholars of hadith, whose collection, the Sahih, is regarded by Sunni Muslims as the most reliable record of the Prophet Muhammad's sayings and deeds.

## The Mongol Storm and the Empire of Timur — 1220 – 1500 CE

Genghis Khan's armies shattered the oasis cities, but from the ruins rose Timur, who made Samarkand the capital of an empire and the heart of a glittering cultural renaissance.

- 1220 CE: **Genghis Khan sacks Bukhara and Samarkand** — The Mongol armies of Genghis Khan storm Transoxiana, destroying Bukhara and Samarkand in a devastation from which the oasis cities took generations to recover. [britannica-samarkand, worldhistory-genghis]
- c. 1370 CE: **Timur takes power and makes Samarkand his capital** — Timur (Tamerlane) seizes control of Transoxiana and makes Samarkand the capital of an empire reaching from Anatolia to India, adorning it as the cultural heart of Central Asia. [britannica-timur, britannica-samarkand]
- 1399–1404 CE: **The Bibi-Khanym mosque and Timurid Samarkand** — Timur summons artisans from across his conquests to build the monumental Bibi-Khanym mosque and other masterpieces, making Samarkand one of the great cities of the medieval world. [unesco-samarkand, britannica-timur]
- c. 1428 CE: **Ulugh Beg builds his observatory** — Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg, ruler and astronomer, builds a great observatory at Samarkand and compiles star tables so accurate they were used in Europe two centuries later. [britannica-ulughbeg]

Figures:

- Timur (Tamerlane) (c. 1336–1405) — Founder of the Timurid Empire. A Turco-Mongol conqueror born near Shakhrisyabz who built an empire from Samarkand stretching across Central Asia, Persia and beyond. Feared for the ferocity of his campaigns, he was also a great patron who made his capital a wonder of Islamic art and architecture.
- Ulugh Beg (1394–1449) — Timurid ruler and astronomer. Grandson of Timur and governor of Samarkand, whose true passion was astronomy. His observatory and the precise star catalogue he compiled made him one of the most important scientists of the medieval world.

## The Uzbek Khanates — 1500 – 1865 CE

Nomadic Uzbek tribes swept away the Timurids and founded the khanates of Bukhara, Khiva and Kokand, the states that gave the land its modern name.

- c. 1500–1510 CE: **Shaybanid Uzbeks conquer Transoxiana** — Muhammad Shaybani Khan leads the Uzbeks south, expelling the last Timurids from Samarkand and Herat and founding the Shaybanid dynasty ruling from Bukhara. [britannica-shaybanid, britannica-earlyuzbeks]
- 1512 CE: **The khanate of Khiva is founded** — A branch of the Shaybanid dynasty establishes the khanate of Khiva in the Khwarezm oasis, one of the three Uzbek states that would endure until the Russian conquest. [britannica-khiva, unesco-khiva]
- early 18th century: **The khanate of Kokand emerges** — A third Uzbek state, the khanate of Kokand, rises in the fertile Fergana Valley, completing the trio of khanates of Bukhara, Khiva and Kokand. [britannica-earlyuzbeks]
- 17th–19th centuries: **Islamic monuments of Bukhara and Khiva** — The khanates raise the madrasas, minarets and palaces that survive in the historic centres of Bukhara and the walled Itchan Kala of Khiva, today UNESCO World Heritage sites. [unesco-bukhara, unesco-khiva]

Figures:

- Muhammad Shaybani Khan (c. 1451–1510) — Founder of the Shaybanid dynasty. The Uzbek leader who conquered Transoxiana from the Timurids around 1500 and gave his adopted name to the Shaybanid dynasty, ruling from Bukhara and founding the line of Uzbek khanates.

## Russian Conquest and Soviet Rule — 1865 – 1991 CE

The Russian Empire absorbed the khanates into Turkestan, and the Soviet Uzbek SSR turned the land into a cotton monoculture that drained the Aral Sea.

- 1865 CE: **Russia takes Tashkent** — Russian imperial forces storm Tashkent, beginning the conquest of the Uzbek lands; Samarkand falls in 1868 as the empire pushes into Central Asia. [loc-russianconquest, britannica-russiansoviet]
- 1868–1876 CE: **Khanates absorbed into Russian Turkestan** — Bukhara (1868) and Khiva (1873) become Russian protectorates and Kokand is annexed in 1876, completing the conquest and the creation of Russian Turkestan. [loc-russianconquest, britannica-russiansoviet]
- 1924–1925 CE: **Creation of the Uzbek SSR** — The Soviet national delimitation of 1924 carves Central Asia into ethnic republics; the Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic is created and becomes a union republic of the USSR in 1925. [britannica-russiansoviet, loc-russianconquest]
- 1960s–1980s: **Cotton monoculture and the death of the Aral Sea** — Soviet irrigation for cotton diverts the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, and the Aral Sea shrinks drastically from the 1960s — a notorious man-made environmental catastrophe. [soviethistory-aral, britannica-russiansoviet]

## Independent Uzbekistan — 1991 – present

Independence in 1991 brought a quarter-century of authoritarian rule under Islam Karimov, followed by a cautious reformist opening under Shavkat Mirziyoyev.

- 1 September 1991: **Uzbekistan declares independence** — The Supreme Council of the Uzbek SSR declares independence on 31 August 1991; 1 September becomes Independence Day, and a December referendum confirms it with Islam Karimov as first president. [statedept-uzbekistan, britannica-karimov]
- May 2005: **The Andijan events** — Government forces open fire on protesters in the city of Andijan; rights groups estimate that hundreds of civilians were killed, drawing international condemnation of Karimov's rule. [britannica-karimov]
- 2016 CE: **Mirziyoyev succeeds Karimov and opens reforms** — After Islam Karimov's death, Shavkat Mirziyoyev becomes president and launches a 'New Uzbekistan' programme of economic liberalisation and cautious political opening. [britannica-mirziyoyev]
- by 2022: **End of systemic forced labour in cotton** — After sustained reforms and international monitoring, civil-society groups conclude that Uzbekistan has dismantled the systemic state-imposed forced labour in its cotton harvest. [hrw-forcedlabor]

Figures:

- Islam Karimov (1938–2016) — First president of Uzbekistan. Born in Samarkand, the Soviet-era leader who became independent Uzbekistan's first president in 1991 and ruled for 25 years. He prized stability over rights, suppressing dissent and independent Islam until his death in 2016.
- Shavkat Mirziyoyev (born 1957) — Second president of Uzbekistan. Karimov's prime minister, who succeeded him in 2016 and surprised observers with a programme of economic liberalisation, diplomatic opening and reform, including moves to end forced labour in the cotton industry.
