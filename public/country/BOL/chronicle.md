Founding: Proclamation of the Republic of Bolivia — 6 August 1825. On 6 August 1825 a congress meeting at Chuquisaca (now Sucre) formally proclaimed the independence of the former province of Upper Peru, or Charcas, as a sovereign republic. The new state was named Bolivia in honour of Simon Bolivar, the Liberator, whose campaigns alongside those of Antonio Jose de Sucre had broken Spanish power in the Andes. The decisive military victory had come at the Battle of Ayacucho in Peru on 9 December 1824, where Sucre routed the last great royalist army; he then crossed into Charcas, convened the congress, and became the republic's first constitutionally elected president in 1826. The city of Chuquisaca was later renamed Sucre in his honour, and remains Bolivia's constitutional capital.

Bolivia's history reaches back to Tiwanaku, the great pre-Hispanic civilisation that flourished near Lake Titicaca between roughly 500 and 900 CE, and to the Aymara lordships and the Inca empire that followed. After the Spanish conquest, the silver of Cerro Rico at Potosi made Upper Peru one of the richest centres of the Spanish empire, worked by mitayos, indigenous men compelled under the mita to labour in mines so deadly the mountain was called the one that eats men. On 6 August 1825 a congress at Chuquisaca proclaimed an independent republic, named Bolivia in honour of Simon Bolivar, with Antonio Jose de Sucre as its first president. The defeat of Bolivia in the War of the Pacific (1879-1883) cost it the Pacific coast and left it landlocked, while the lost Chaco War with Paraguay (1932-1935) helped trigger the National Revolution of 1952; after decades of military rule, democracy returned in 1982, and in 2006 Evo Morales became Bolivia's first indigenous president, beginning an era of indigenous-majority politics that endured through his disputed 2019 election and resignation, the return of the MAS, and the turn to the centre-right in 2025.

## Tiwanaku, the Aymara, and the Inca — c. 500 - 1532 CE

On the high Altiplano around Lake Titicaca rose Tiwanaku, one of the great civilisations of the ancient Andes; after its decline the Aymara lordships dominated the highlands until they in turn were absorbed into the expanding Inca empire.

- c. 500-900 CE: **Tiwanaku reaches its apogee** — The city of Tiwanaku near Lake Titicaca flourishes as the spiritual and political centre of a powerful Andean culture, building the monumental complex later inscribed by UNESCO. [unesco-tiwanaku, britannica-bolivia-early]
- c. 12th-15th century: **The Aymara lordships** — After Tiwanaku's decline, Aymara-speaking kingdoms dominate the highlands around Lake Titicaca until the rise of the Inca. [britannica-bolivia-early]
- 15th century: **Inca conquest of the highlands** — The Inca empire expands south into the region the Spanish would call Charcas, absorbing the Aymara lands while permitting them their language and identity. [britannica-bolivia-early]

## Conquest, the Silver of Potosi, and Charcas — 1532 - 1780

The Spanish conquest folded the Andean highlands into the empire as Charcas, or Upper Peru; the discovery of silver at Cerro Rico made Potosi one of the wealthiest cities on earth, its mines fed by the forced labour of indigenous mitayos.

- 1538-1559: **Spanish conquest of Charcas** — Spanish forces subdue the highlands of Upper Peru, organising them as the Audiencia of Charcas within the Viceroyalty of Peru. [britannica-bolivia-early, loc-bolivia-study]
- 1545 CE: **Silver discovered at Cerro Rico** — The discovery of immense silver deposits in the mountain above Potosi sets off a boom that makes the city one of the largest and richest in the world. [unesco-potosi, britannica-bolivia-early]
- 1570s: **Toledo systematises the mita** — Viceroy Francisco de Toledo organises the mita, a forced labour draft that compels indigenous men across the Andes to work the deadly mines and mercury refineries of Potosi. [britannica-bolivia-early, unesco-potosi]
- 16th-17th century: **Potosi feeds the Spanish empire** — Potosi's silver becomes the principal source of bullion for the Spanish crown, financing its empire while consuming the lives of indigenous mitayos. [unesco-potosi, britannica-bolivia-early]

Figures:

- Francisco de Toledo (1515-1582) — Viceroy of Peru. Spanish viceroy who reorganised the colonial economy of the Andes in the 1570s and systematised the mita labour draft that supplied forced indigenous workers to the silver mines of Potosi.

## The Revolt of Tupac Katari and Independence — 1780 - 1825

The great Andean rebellion of 1780-1781 brought the Aymara leader Tupac Katari to lay siege to La Paz; a generation later the wars of independence, won at Ayacucho by Sucre, gave birth in 1825 to the Republic of Bolivia.

- 1781 CE: **Tupac Katari besieges La Paz** — The Aymara leader Tupac Katari, part of the great Andean rebellion begun by Tupac Amaru II, lays siege to La Paz before being captured and executed. [britannica-tupac-amaru-ii, loc-bolivia-study]
- 1809 CE: **Uprisings at Chuquisaca and La Paz** — Revolts in Upper Peru in 1809, sparked by the Spanish crisis under Napoleon, are remembered among the first independence movements in the Americas. [britannica-bolivia-early, loc-bolivia-study]
- 9 Dec 1824: **Battle of Ayacucho** — Antonio Jose de Sucre routs the last great royalist army at Ayacucho, sealing the independence of Spanish South America and opening the way to liberate Charcas. [britannica-sucre, britannica-bolivar-bolivia]
- 6 Aug 1825: **Republic of Bolivia proclaimed** — A congress at Chuquisaca declares the independence of Upper Peru as a republic named Bolivia in honour of Simon Bolivar, with Sucre soon its first president. [britannica-bolivar-bolivia, britannica-sucre]

Figures:

- Tupac Katari (c. 1750-1781) — Aymara rebel leader. Born Julian Apasa, he led the Aymara of the La Paz region in the great Andean rebellion of 1781, besieging the city for months before his capture and execution. He is now honoured as a symbol of indigenous resistance.
- Antonio Jose de Sucre (1795-1830) — Liberator and first president of Bolivia. Bolivar's foremost lieutenant, victor of the Battle of Ayacucho in 1824, who liberated Charcas and became Bolivia's first constitutionally elected president, embarking on social and economic reforms before resigning in 1828.

## Lost Wars, the Sea, and the Tin Republic — 1825 - 1935

A turbulent young republic lost its Pacific coast to Chile in the War of the Pacific and was left landlocked; an economy built on tin enriched a few mine owners, and a disastrous war with Paraguay over the Chaco shattered the old order.

- 1879-1883: **War of the Pacific** — Chile defeats Bolivia and its ally Peru and annexes Bolivia's coast; Bolivia loses its Pacific province of Antofagasta and becomes landlocked. [britannica-war-pacific, loc-bolivia-study]
- 1904 CE: **Treaty confirms the loss of the coast** — A treaty between Bolivia and Chile formally cedes the Pacific coast to Chile, fixing Bolivia's landlocked status and a lasting national grievance. [britannica-war-pacific, britannica-bolivia, loc-bolivia-study]
- early 20th century: **The age of the tin barons** — Tin replaces silver as Bolivia's great export, making a handful of mine owners enormously rich while the indigenous mine workers labour in poverty. [loc-bolivia-study, britannica-bolivia-early]
- 1932-1935: **The Chaco War with Paraguay** — Bolivia is defeated by Paraguay in a brutal war over the Gran Chaco, losing most of the disputed land and discrediting the old governing order. [britannica-chaco-war, loc-bolivia-study]

## The National Revolution and Military Rule — 1935 - 1982

The shock of the Chaco War gave birth to the National Revolution of 1952, which nationalised the tin mines, gave indigenous Bolivians the vote and the land, and remade the nation, before two decades of military dictatorship overtook it.

- April 1952: **The National Revolution** — A popular insurrection brings the MNR and Victor Paz Estenssoro to power, launching reforms that reshape Bolivia. [britannica-paz-estenssoro, loc-bolivia-study]
- 1952-1953: **Tin nationalised, suffrage and land reform** — The revolutionary government nationalises the tin mines, grants universal suffrage to the indigenous majority, and decrees a sweeping agrarian reform. [britannica-paz-estenssoro, loc-bolivia-study]
- 1964 CE: **Military coup ends the revolution** — The armed forces overthrow Paz Estenssoro, ending the revolutionary era and beginning eighteen years of military rule. [loc-bolivia-study, britannica-paz-estenssoro]
- 1967 CE: **Death of Che Guevara** — The revolutionary Ernesto Che Guevara is captured and killed by the Bolivian army while leading a failed guerrilla campaign in the southeast. [loc-bolivia-study]
- Oct 1982: **Return to democracy** — After years of military rule and the violent Garcia Meza regime, the armed forces cede power to Congress, which makes Hernan Siles Zuazo president. [loc-bolivia-study, statedept-bolivia]

Figures:

- Victor Paz Estenssoro (1907-2001) — President and revolutionary leader. Founder and principal leader of the MNR and three times president of Bolivia, who led the 1952 National Revolution that nationalised the tin mines, extended the vote to the indigenous majority, and carried out a sweeping agrarian reform.

## Democracy and the Indigenous-Majority Era — 1982 - present

Restored democracy weathered hyperinflation and unrest before the rise of Evo Morales, Bolivia's first indigenous president, whose long tenure ended in the disputed election and resignation of 2019; the MAS returned, then fractured, and in 2025 the country turned to the centre-right.

- 1985 CE: **Hyperinflation and stabilisation** — Amid catastrophic hyperinflation, a returning Paz Estenssoro imposes a drastic stabilisation programme that ends the crisis but brings severe austerity. [britannica-paz-estenssoro, loc-bolivia-study]
- Jan 2006: **Evo Morales takes office** — Evo Morales, leader of the MAS, becomes Bolivia's first indigenous president, beginning an era of indigenous-majority politics and hydrocarbon nationalisation. [britannica-morales, statedept-bolivia]
- 2009 CE: **Plurinational constitution** — A new constitution refounds Bolivia as a plurinational state recognising its indigenous nations and expanding their rights. [britannica-morales]
- Nov 2019: **Disputed election and Morales's resignation** — After a contested October vote that an OAS audit found marred by irregularities, weeks of protest and the loss of military and police support lead Morales to resign and go into exile. [britannica-morales, britannica-arce]
- 2020 CE: **Return of the MAS under Arce** — Luis Arce wins the presidency for the MAS, returning the party to power a year after Morales's fall. [britannica-arce]
- Oct 2025: **Turn to the centre-right** — Amid economic crisis and a split between Arce and Morales, the centrist Rodrigo Paz wins the presidency, ending nearly twenty years of MAS rule. [britannica-arce]

Figures:

- Evo Morales (b. 1959) — First indigenous president of Bolivia. Aymara former coca-growers' union leader and founder of the MAS who in 2006 became Bolivia's first indigenous president. He nationalised hydrocarbons, cut poverty and refounded the state as plurinational, but resigned in 2019 after a disputed election that split the country.
- Luis Arce (b. 1963) — President of Bolivia (2020-2025). Economist and former finance minister under Morales who led the MAS back to power in 2020, then broke with Morales in a rivalry that fractured the party amid a worsening economic crisis.
