# The Chronicle of Moldova: From the principality of Stephen the Great to a partitioned borderland on the road to Europe.

Founding: Independence of the Republic of Moldova — 27 August 1991. The modern Moldovan state dates its sovereignty to 27 August 1991, when the parliament in Chisinau adopted the Declaration of Independence from the collapsing Soviet Union, days after the failed hardline coup in Moscow. The republic was the Soviet heir to historic Bessarabia, the eastern half of the medieval Principality of Moldavia that Russia had annexed in 1812. Independence was immediately shadowed by conflict: the predominantly Russian-speaking strip on the east bank of the Dniester, Transnistria, refused to accept the new state, and a war in 1992, decided by the intervention of the Russian 14th Army, left a frozen separatist enclave that endures, with Russian forces still present, into the present day.

Moldova's history is that of a borderland repeatedly divided between empires. A principality founded in the fourteenth century reached its golden age under Stephen the Great, who held off the Ottomans, Hungarians and Poles before Moldavia settled into long centuries of Ottoman vassalage and the venal rule of Phanariot Greek princes. In 1812 the Russian Empire seized the eastern half of the principality, Bessarabia, between the Prut and the Dniester, beginning more than a century of Russian and then Soviet rule broken only by a brief union with Romania between 1918 and 1940. The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact delivered Bessarabia to Stalin in 1940, who forged the Moldavian Soviet Socialist Republic and visited deportations and a man-made famine upon it. Independence came on 27 August 1991, but war soon broke out in the Russian-backed breakaway region of Transnistria, where Russian troops remain to this day, while the Gagauz minority secured autonomy by negotiation. Moldova was granted European Union candidate status in June 2022 and opened accession negotiations in 2024.

## The Principality of Moldavia and the Golden Age of Stephen the Great — c. 1349 – 1504

A Vlach principality founded east of the Carpathians rose under Stephen the Great into a defiant Christian power that held the Ottomans, Hungarians and Poles at bay.

- c. 1349: **Founding of the Principality of Moldavia** — Vlach migrants from Maramures established a principality east of the Carpathians; its independence is traditionally attributed to the voivode Bogdan I, making Moldavia the second Romanian principality. [moldavia-britannica, moldova-history-britannica]
- 1457–1504: **Reign of Stephen the Great** — Stephen IV ruled Moldavia for nearly half a century, winning European renown for resisting the Ottomans while defending the principality against Hungary and Poland and endowing many churches and monasteries. [stephen-britannica, moldavia-britannica]
- 1475: **Battle of Vaslui** — Stephen the Great destroyed a much larger Ottoman army at Vaslui, a victory celebrated across Christian Europe that briefly halted the Turkish advance into Moldavia. [stephen-britannica]
- 1476: **Defeat at Valea Alba** — An Ottoman counter-offensive under Sultan Mehmed II defeated Stephen at Valea Alba; though he survived and recovered, the campaign exposed the mounting pressure on the small principality. [stephen-britannica]

Figures:

- Bogdan I (died c. 1367) — Founding voivode of Moldavia. Bogdan the Founder crossed from Maramures into the lands east of the Carpathians and asserted Moldavia's independence from Hungary around the 1360s, an act to which historians attribute the foundation of the principality.
- Stephen the Great (c. 1433 – 1504) — Prince of Moldavia. Reigning from 1457 to 1504, Stephen IV defended Moldavian independence against the Ottomans, Hungarians and Poles, won a famous victory at Vaslui in 1475, and endowed the churches and monasteries that became emblems of Moldavian culture.

## Ottoman Vassalage and the Phanariot Century — 1504 – 1812

Reduced to a tributary of the sultan, Moldavia kept its church and identity but lost control of its own throne to the venal rule of Phanariot Greek princes.

- 16th century: **Moldavia becomes an Ottoman tributary** — Following the death of Stephen the Great, Moldavia was reduced to a tributary vassal of the Ottoman Empire, keeping its princes, church and laws but paying tribute and following the sultan's lead in foreign affairs. [moldavia-britannica, moldova-history-britannica]
- 1711: **Beginning of Phanariot rule** — After Prince Dimitrie Cantemir's failed alliance with Russia, the Porte ended native rule and began appointing Greek princes from the Phanar district of Constantinople, who governed until 1821. [moldova-history-britannica, moldavia-britannica]
- 1812: **Partition of Moldavia at the Treaty of Bucharest** — The Treaty of Bucharest ending the Russo-Turkish War allowed Russia to annex Bessarabia, the eastern half of the principality between the Prut and the Dniester, splitting Moldavia in two. [bucharest-britannica, bessarabia-britannica]

Figures:

- Dimitrie Cantemir (1673 – 1723) — Prince of Moldavia and scholar. A celebrated scholar and the last notable native prince before the Phanariot era, Cantemir allied with Peter the Great of Russia against the Ottomans in 1711; his defeat prompted the Porte to replace native rule with Greek Phanariot princes.
- Stephen the Great (c. 1433 – 1504) — Prince of Moldavia. The reign of Stephen IV marked the last era in which Moldavia stood as a fully independent power; after his death the principality slid into the tributary subordination to the Ottoman Empire that shaped the following three centuries.

## Russian Bessarabia — 1812 – 1917

The Russian Empire took the land between the Prut and the Dniester, renamed it Bessarabia, and ruled it for a century, colonizing and Russifying a province severed from the rest of Moldavia.

- 28 May 1812: **Annexation of Bessarabia** — By the Treaty of Bucharest, Russia annexed the land between the Prut and the Dniester from the Ottoman Empire, organizing it as the province of Bessarabia and severing it from the rest of Moldavia. [bucharest-britannica, bessarabia-britannica]
- 1828–1829: **Curtailment of Bessarabian autonomy** — After an initial period of limited self-government, Russia integrated Bessarabia into the imperial administration, beginning a policy of Russification that made Russian the language of government and schooling. [bessarabia-britannica, moldova-history-britannica]
- 1856: **Southern Bessarabia briefly returned** — The Treaty of Paris ending the Crimean War returned the southern strip of Bessarabia to Moldavia; Russia recovered it in 1878 at the Congress of Berlin, underlining the province's status as a contested borderland. [bessarabia-britannica, moldova-history-britannica]
- April 1903: **Kishinev pogrom** — A violent anti-Jewish pogrom in the provincial capital Kishinev (Chisinau) drew international condemnation and became a notorious symbol of the persecution of Jews in the late Russian Empire. [bessarabia-britannica, moldova-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Bogdan Petriceicu Hasdeu (1838 – 1907) — Scholar and writer. Born in Bessarabia under Russian rule, Hasdeu became one of the foremost Romanian philologists, historians and writers of the nineteenth century, embodying the cultural link between the annexed province and the wider Romanian world.
- Alexander I of Russia (1777 – 1825) — Emperor of Russia. It was under the emperor Alexander I that Russia acquired Bessarabia by the Treaty of Bucharest in 1812, incorporating the eastern half of Moldavia into the empire and beginning more than a century of Russian rule.

## Union with Romania and the Soviet Creation of a Moldavian Republic — 1917 – 1940

When the Russian Empire fell, Bessarabia proclaimed itself and voted to join Romania, while the Soviet Union, refusing to accept the loss, built a rival Moldavian republic on the east bank of the Dniester.

- 15 Dec 1917: **Moldavian Democratic Republic proclaimed** — After the Russian Revolution, the Sfatul Tarii (national council) in Chisinau proclaimed the Moldavian Democratic Republic, soon declaring full independence from Russia amid the turmoil of 1917-1918. [moldova-history-britannica, union-1918-reference]
- 27 Mar 1918: **Union of Bessarabia with Romania** — The Sfatul Tarii voted to unite Bessarabia with Romania (27 March, Old Style; 9 April, New Style); the union, at first conditional on autonomy, was made unconditional in November 1918. [union-1918-reference, moldova-history-britannica]
- 1924: **Creation of the Moldavian ASSR** — Refusing to recognize the loss of Bessarabia, the Soviet Union established the Moldavian Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic on the east bank of the Dniester within Soviet Ukraine, as a standing claim to the territory across the river. [moldova-history-britannica, moldavia-britannica]
- 28 Oct 1920: **International recognition of the union** — A treaty signed in Paris by Romania, Britain, France, Italy and Japan recognized the union of Bessarabia with Romania, though the Soviet Union never accepted it. [union-1918-reference, moldova-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Ion Inculet (1884 – 1940) — President of the Sfatul Tarii. Inculet presided over the Sfatul Tarii, the Bessarabian national council that proclaimed the Moldavian Democratic Republic in 1917 and voted the union with Romania in 1918, becoming a central figure of the brief independent republic.
- Pantelimon Halippa (1883 – 1979) — Bessarabian nationalist leader. A leading advocate of Bessarabian self-determination, Halippa was a driving force in the Sfatul Tarii and in the movement for union with Romania, later persecuted under Soviet and communist rule.

## Soviet Moldavia: Annexation, Famine and Deportation — 1940 – 1991

The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact handed Bessarabia to Stalin, who built the Moldavian SSR and subjected it to war, a man-made famine, mass deportations and half a century of Sovietization.

- 28 Jun 1940: **Soviet annexation of Bessarabia** — Acting on the secret protocol of the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, the Red Army occupied Bessarabia after an ultimatum to Romania, ending more than two decades of Romanian rule. [soviet-occupation-reference, moldova-history-britannica]
- 2 Aug 1940: **Creation of the Moldavian SSR** — The Soviet Union proclaimed the Moldavian Soviet Socialist Republic, joining most of annexed Bessarabia to the Moldavian ASSR east of the Dniester, with the new republic's borders deliberately reshaped. [soviet-occupation-reference, moldova-history-britannica]
- 1946–1947: **The man-made famine** — A famine aggravated by harsh Soviet grain requisitioning swept Moldavia in 1946-1947, killing well over a hundred thousand people in one of the least-known episodes of Stalinist terror. [famine-reference, moldova-history-britannica]
- 6 Jul 1949: **Operation South deportations** — On the night of 5-6 July 1949 Soviet authorities deported tens of thousands of Moldavians condemned as kulaks to Siberia and Kazakhstan, accelerating the forced collectivization of agriculture. [deportations-reference, moldova-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Vyacheslav Molotov (1890 – 1986) — Soviet foreign minister. As Stalin's foreign minister, Molotov signed the 1939 pact with Nazi Germany whose secret protocol assigned Bessarabia to the Soviet sphere, leading directly to the 1940 annexation and the creation of the Moldavian SSR.
- Mircea Snegur (1940 – 2023) — First President of Moldova. A communist official turned national leader, Snegur emerged at the head of Moldova's drive for sovereignty in the late Soviet years, led the republic to independence in 1991 and became its first president.

## Independence, the Transnistria Conflict and the Road to Europe — 1991 – present

Independence in 1991 brought war in Transnistria and a frozen separatist conflict policed by Russian troops, an autonomy settlement for the Gagauz, and, after 2022, a decisive turn toward the European Union.

- 27 Aug 1991: **Declaration of Independence** — Moldova's parliament declared independence from the Soviet Union on 27 August 1991, in the wake of the failed Moscow coup, with Mircea Snegur as the new state's first president. [independence-reference, moldova-history-britannica]
- 21 Jul 1992: **The Transnistria war and ceasefire** — War with the Russian-backed Transnistrian separatists peaked in the battle for Bender; the intervention of the Russian 14th Army proved decisive, and a ceasefire on 21 July 1992 froze the conflict, leaving Russian troops in place. [transnistria-jamestown, osce-conflict]
- 1993: **OSCE Mission to Moldova** — The OSCE established its Mission to Moldova to facilitate a comprehensive settlement consolidating Moldova's sovereignty and territorial integrity with a special status for Transnistria, later working through the 5+2 format. [osce-about, osce-conflict]
- 1994: **Autonomy for Gagauzia** — A Moldovan law granted broad self-government to the Turkic-speaking Gagauz of the south, creating the autonomous territorial unit of Gagauzia and peacefully resolving their bid for separation. [osce-gagauzia]
- 22 Jun 2018: **UN resolution on foreign forces** — The UN General Assembly adopted resolution 72/282 by 64 votes to 15, urging Russia to complete the unconditional withdrawal of its Operational Group of Forces from Moldova, in line with the 1999 OSCE Istanbul commitments. [un-resolution-72-282, transnistria-jamestown]
- 23 Jun 2022: **EU candidate status** — After applying for membership on 3 March 2022, Moldova was granted European Union candidate status by the European Council on 23 June 2022, opening accession negotiations in 2024. [eu-candidate-ec, eu-candidate-ep]

Figures:

- Maia Sandu (born 1972) — President of Moldova. A reformist and former World Bank economist, Sandu was elected president in 2020 and steered Moldova's application for European Union membership and its 2022 grant of candidate status amid the war in neighbouring Ukraine.
- Alexander Lebed (1950 – 2002) — Commander of the Russian 14th Army. The Russian general whose 14th Army intervened decisively in the 1992 Transnistria war, ensuring the survival of the breakaway region and a continuing Russian military presence on Moldovan soil.
