Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 12 December 1963. Kenya became an independent state on 12 December 1963, ending nearly seven decades of British rule that had begun with the East Africa Protectorate in 1895. The independence settlement was negotiated through three Lancaster House conferences in London between 1960 and 1963, where the rival parties KANU and KADU contested whether the new state would be unitary or federal. Jomo Kenyatta, jailed during the Mau Mau emergency and freed in 1961, became the country's first prime minister at independence; a year later, on 12 December 1964, Kenya became a republic with Kenyatta as its first president. The date 12 December — Jamhuri Day — remains Kenya's central national holiday.

Kenya holds one of the richest records of human origins on earth, its Turkana basin yielding hominin fossils spanning millions of years, including the near-complete Homo erectus skeleton known as Turkana Boy. Over later millennia Bantu and Nilotic peoples settled its highlands and lakes, while a string of Swahili city-states — Mombasa, Malindi, Lamu and Gede — grew rich on Indian Ocean trade and gave rise to the Swahili language and an Afro-Islamic coastal culture. The Portuguese seized the coast in the sixteenth century and built Fort Jesus, only to be expelled by Omani Arabs in 1698, after which the coast fell under the orbit of the sultanate of Zanzibar. From 1895 Britain ruled the interior as the East Africa Protectorate, drove the Uganda Railway inland, and settled the fertile highlands, dispossessions that fed the Mau Mau uprising of 1952–60. Kenya won independence on 12 December 1963 under Jomo Kenyatta and, after decades of one-party rule under Kenyatta and Daniel arap Moi, returned to multiparty politics in 1991, endured the 2007–08 post-election violence, and adopted a new devolved constitution in 2010.

## Cradle of Humankind: the Turkana Basin — c. 4 million years ago – c. 1000 BCE

The shores of Lake Turkana and the Koobi Fora badlands have yielded one of the world's most complete records of early human ancestors.

- c. 1.6 million years ago: **Turkana Boy lives near Lake Turkana** — A Homo erectus youth, later catalogued KNM-WT 15000, lives near what is now the Nariokotome River; his fossilised skeleton would become the most complete of its species ever recovered. [britannica-nariokotome, si-knmwt15000, tbi-turkanaboy]
- c. 1.9 million years ago: **Hominins at Koobi Fora** — The Koobi Fora deposits east of Lake Turkana preserve fossils of Paranthropus boisei and early Homo, making the area one of the world's leading sites for the study of human evolution. [nmk-koobifora, britannica-koobifora]
- c. 3rd millennium BCE: **Cushitic pastoralists enter the north** — Southern Cushitic-speaking herders move into the arid lands of northern Kenya, among the earliest food-producing communities in the region. [wiki-history-kenya]
- c. 1000 BCE: **Olorgesailie tool traditions and the Stone Age** — Sites such as Olorgesailie in the Rift Valley preserve vast accumulations of hand-axes and other tools, documenting hundreds of thousands of years of Stone Age life in Kenya. [wiki-history-kenya, nmk-koobifora]

Figures:

- Kamoya Kimeu (1938–2022) — Kenyan palaeontologist and fossil-hunter. The most renowned of Kenya's fossil-hunters, who found Turkana Boy in 1984 and many other key hominin specimens, earning a central place in the study of human origins.

## Migrations and the Swahili Coast — c. 1000 BCE – 1498 CE

Bantu and Nilotic peoples settled the interior while a chain of cosmopolitan Swahili city-states grew rich on Indian Ocean trade.

- c. 1000 BCE: **Bantu expansion reaches western Kenya** — Iron-using Bantu-speaking farmers spread into western Kenya, part of the wider Bantu expansion that reshaped much of eastern and southern Africa. [wiki-history-kenya, britannica-kenya-people]
- c. 8th–10th century CE: **Rise of the Swahili coast** — Coastal Bantu communities trading across the Indian Ocean develop the Swahili language and an Afro-Islamic urban culture, founding settlements that grow into city-states. [wiki-swahili-coast, britannica-kenya-people]
- 1331: **Ibn Battuta visits Mombasa** — The traveller Ibn Battuta describes Mombasa and Kilwa as prosperous, pious Muslim trading towns, attesting to the wealth of the medieval Swahili coast. [wiki-history-kenya, wiki-swahili-coast]
- 1418: **Zheng He's fleet reaches Malindi** — Vessels of the Chinese admiral Zheng He's treasure fleet are recorded reaching Malindi, marking the eastern extent of the Indian Ocean trade that linked the Swahili coast to China. [wiki-history-kenya]
- c. 15th century: **Southern Luo migrate into western Kenya** — Nilotic-speaking Luo communities move into the Lake Victoria basin of western Kenya, the last major wave of the migrations that formed the country's peoples. [wiki-history-kenya]

## Portuguese and Omani Rule on the Coast — 1498 – 1895

Vasco da Gama opened an era of Portuguese dominance symbolised by Fort Jesus, until Omani Arabs seized the coast and bound it to Zanzibar.

- April 1498: **Vasco da Gama reaches the Kenyan coast** — The Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama calls at Mombasa and Malindi en route to India, beginning a century of Portuguese involvement on the Swahili coast. [wiki-history-kenya, britannica-vascodagama]
- 1593–1596: **Portuguese build Fort Jesus** — The Portuguese construct Fort Jesus at Mombasa to a Renaissance design by Giovanni Battista Cairati, anchoring their control of the coast and the Indian Ocean trade routes. [unesco-fortjesus, nmk-fortjesus]
- December 1698: **Omani Arabs capture Fort Jesus** — After a siege of some thirty-three months, Omani forces take Fort Jesus, ending Portuguese dominance and bringing the Kenyan coast, Zanzibar and Pemba under Omani Arab control. [wiki-siege-mombasa, nmk-fortjesus]
- 1840: **Zanzibar becomes the Omani capital** — Sultan Seyyid Said moves the Omani capital from Muscat to Zanzibar, making the island the centre of a commercial empire to which the Kenyan coast was bound. [wiki-history-kenya, britannica-zanzibar]
- 2011: **Fort Jesus inscribed by UNESCO** — Fort Jesus is added to the UNESCO World Heritage List as an outstanding example of sixteenth-century military architecture and a witness to the contest for the Indian Ocean trade. [unesco-fortjesus]

Figures:

- Seyyid Said bin Sultan — Sultan of Oman and Zanzibar (r. 1807–1856). The Omani ruler who shifted his capital to Zanzibar in 1840 and built a clove-and-ivory commercial empire along the East African coast, of which the Kenyan towns were part.

## British East Africa and the Settler Colony — 1895 – 1952

Britain pushed the Uganda Railway inland, opened the highlands to white settlers, and reduced Kenya's peoples to subjects of a colony.

- 1 July 1895: **East Africa Protectorate established** — Britain proclaims the East Africa Protectorate over the territory that would become Kenya, beginning nearly seven decades of formal British rule. [wiki-history-kenya, statedept-kenya]
- 1896–1901: **The Uganda Railway is built** — The 'Lunatic Line' is driven from Mombasa to Lake Victoria, built largely by indentured Indian labourers; it reaches Nairobi in 1899 and opens the interior to settlement. [wiki-uganda-railway, wiki-history-kenya]
- 1920: **Kenya Colony created** — The protectorate's interior becomes the Crown colony of Kenya, while a coastal strip leased from the sultan of Zanzibar remains a protectorate. [wiki-history-kenya, statedept-kenya]
- 1921: **Harry Thuku founds early protest** — Harry Thuku establishes the Young Kikuyu Association, one of the first organised African political movements against colonial land and labour policies. [wiki-history-kenya]
- 1947: **Kenyatta leads the Kenya African Union** — Jomo Kenyatta, back from years in Britain, becomes president of the Kenya African Union, the leading vehicle of African nationalist demands. [britannica-kenyatta, wiki-history-kenya]

## Mau Mau and the Road to Independence — 1952 – 1963

The Mau Mau uprising and a brutal state of emergency hastened the end of empire, and Kenya won independence under Jomo Kenyatta.

- October 1952: **State of emergency declared** — As the Mau Mau revolt spreads, the colonial government declares a state of emergency that lasts until 1960, ushering in mass detentions and a brutal counter-insurgency. [britannica-maumau, blackpast-maumau, iwm-kenya-emergency]
- 1953: **Jomo Kenyatta convicted** — Kenyatta is tried and imprisoned for allegedly directing the Mau Mau, in proceedings later widely denounced as a sham; he is freed in 1961. [britannica-kenyatta, blackpast-maumau]
- 1960–1963: **Lancaster House conferences** — Three constitutional conferences in London negotiate Kenya's path to independence amid rivalry between KANU and the federalist KADU. [wiki-lancaster-house, wiki-history-kenya]
- 1 June 1963: **Internal self-government (Madaraka)** — Kenya attains internal self-government under a KANU government led by Kenyatta, six months before full independence. [wiki-history-kenya, statedept-kenya]
- 12 December 1963: **Kenya becomes independent** — Kenya gains independence from the United Kingdom with Jomo Kenyatta as prime minister; one year later, on 12 December 1964, it becomes a republic. [britannica-maumau, statedept-kenya, britannica-kenyatta]

Figures:

- Jomo Kenyatta (c. 1897–1978) — Independence leader; first president of Kenya. Nationalist leader of the Kikuyu Central Association and the Kenya African Union, imprisoned during the Mau Mau emergency, who became independent Kenya's first prime minister in 1963 and first president in 1964.

## The Republic of Kenya — 1963 – present

From one-party rule under Kenyatta and Moi, Kenya returned to multiparty democracy and adopted a new devolved constitution in 2010.

- 1978: **Moi succeeds Kenyatta** — On Kenyatta's death, Vice-President Daniel arap Moi becomes president, beginning a 24-year tenure that formalises the one-party state. [britannica-moi, wiki-history-kenya]
- 1982: **Failed coup attempt** — Air-force officers attempt to overthrow Moi; the coup is crushed and followed by tighter political control and a constitutionally entrenched one-party state. [wiki-history-kenya, britannica-moi]
- 1991: **Return to multiparty politics** — Under domestic and international pressure, Kenya amends its constitution to legalise opposition parties, paving the way for the multiparty elections of 1992. [wiki-history-kenya, statedept-kenya]
- 2002: **Kibaki ends KANU rule** — Mwai Kibaki wins the presidency, ending nearly four decades of unbroken KANU government since independence. [britannica-kibaki, wiki-history-kenya]
- 2007–2008: **Post-election violence** — Kibaki's disputed re-election sparks ethnic and political violence that kills more than 1,100 people; a power-sharing deal brokered by Kofi Annan is signed on 28 February 2008. [hrw-kenya-pev, wiki-history-kenya]
- 4 August 2010: **New constitution adopted** — Kenyans approve a new constitution that limits presidential power, expands rights, and devolves authority to 47 county governments. [ballotpedia-kenya-2010, wiki-history-kenya]

Figures:

- Daniel arap Moi (1924–2020) — Second president of Kenya (1978–2002). Kenyatta's successor, who ruled for 24 years over a one-party state, survived a 1982 coup attempt, and eventually presided over the return to multiparty politics before stepping down in 2002.
