# The Chronicle of Mauritania: From the Sanhaja desert and the Almoravid jihad to the caravan ksour and the modern Islamic Republic.

Founding: Independence from France — 28 November 1960. The Islamic Republic of Mauritania became fully independent of France on 28 November 1960. The territory had been organised as a French colony within French West Africa from 1904 and became an autonomous republic within the French Community in 1958. Moktar Ould Daddah, who had served as the colony's first prime minister from 1957, led the country to sovereignty and became its first president in 1961, governing until he was deposed in a military coup in 1978.

Mauritania spans the western Sahara and the Sahel, a bridge between the Arab-Berber Maghreb and Black Africa. Its territory held the southern reach of the ancient Ghana (Wagadou) Empire, whose city of Koumbi Saleh was a great trans-Saharan emporium, and it was the cradle of the Almoravid movement, the eleventh-century Sanhaja Berber jihad that came to rule from the Senegal River to Spain. From the thirteenth century onward, waves of Arab Beni Hassan tribes migrated south, and after the Char Bouba war of the seventeenth century their Hassaniya Arabic and warrior elite shaped a Moorish society organised into the emirates of Trarza, Brakna, Tagant and Adrar, with the caravan towns of Chinguetti, Ouadane, Tichitt and Oualata as renowned centres of Islamic learning. France gradually subdued the region between 1900 and the 1930s; Mauritania gained independence on 28 November 1960 under Moktar Ould Daddah. Since a 1978 coup the country has experienced repeated military takeovers, a brief and costly involvement in the Western Sahara conflict, and a long struggle against the legacy of slavery, abolished in law in 1981 and criminalised in 2007.

## The Ghana Empire and the Sanhaja Sahara — c. 700 – 1050 CE

Before Islam took deep root, the southern reaches of Mauritania lay within the great trans-Saharan trade empire of Ghana, while the veiled Sanhaja Berbers ruled the desert routes to the north.

- c. 8th century: **Ghana Empire dominates the trade routes** — The Soninke kingdom of Ghana (Wagadou) controls the trans-Saharan gold-and-salt trade, with its influence reaching into the south of present-day Mauritania. [britannica-mauritania, wiki-history-mauritania]
- c. 9th–11th century: **Koumbi Saleh as a Saharan emporium** — Koumbi Saleh, widely identified as Ghana's capital, grows into a major trading town with a Muslim merchants' quarter and, at its peak, tens of thousands of inhabitants. [wiki-koumbi-saleh, wiki-history-mauritania]
- early 11th century: **Break-up of the Sanhaja confederation** — The confederation of veiled Sanhaja Berbers controlling the western Saharan trade routes fragments, producing warfare and unrest among the desert tribes. [wiki-history-mauritania, britannica-almoravids]

## The Almoravid Jihad — c. 1040 – 1147 CE

Out of the Sanhaja desert rose the Almoravids, a puritan reform movement that swept north into Morocco and Spain and south against the Ghana Empire.

- c. 1040: **Abdallah ibn Yasin founds the Almoravid movement** — The theologian Abdallah ibn Yasin begins preaching a reformed Islam among the Sanhaja, gathering the disciplined religious-military following known as the al-Murabitun (Almoravids). [britannica-almoravids, wiki-ibn-yasin]
- 1059: **Death of Ibn Yasin; Abu Bakr leads the desert wing** — After Ibn Yasin is killed in 1059, Abu Bakr ibn Umar takes command of the Almoravid forces in the Sahara, while Yusuf ibn Tashfin leads the northern conquests. [wiki-abu-bakr, britannica-almoravids]
- c. 1076: **Almoravids said to take Koumbi Saleh** — Tradition records that Almoravid forces under Abu Bakr captured the Ghanaian capital of Koumbi Saleh around 1076, contributing to the decline of the Ghana Empire—though the campaign's scale is debated. [wiki-history-mauritania, britannica-almoravids, wiki-koumbi-saleh]
- 1147: **Fall of the Almoravid empire** — The Almoravid empire, which had stretched from the Senegal River to Spain, is overthrown by the Almohads, but Islam remains firmly rooted in the western Sahara. [britannica-almoravids]

Figures:

- Abdallah ibn Yasin (died 1059) — Founder of the Almoravid movement. A Muslim theologian who preached a stricter Islam among the Sanhaja Berbers and forged them into the al-Murabitun (Almoravids), the reform movement that would conquer the western Maghreb and Spain.
- Abu Bakr ibn Umar (amir c. 1056–1087) — Almoravid amir of the Sahara. Sanhaja leader of the Almoravids' southern wing, associated with the Adrar fortress of Azougui, who directed the movement's campaigns against the Ghana Empire while his cousin Yusuf ibn Tashfin expanded northward.

## Arab Migration, the Char Bouba War and the Caravan Ksour — c. 1200 – 1700

Successive waves of Arab Beni Hassan tribes moved south into the desert, and after the Char Bouba war their language and warrior elite remade Moorish society around the great caravan towns.

- 13th–17th century: **Beni Hassan Arab migration** — Waves of Banu Maqil Arabs, including the Beni Hassan, migrate south into the western Sahara, spreading Hassaniya Arabic and gradually transforming the region's Berber society. [wiki-beni-hassan, wiki-history-mauritania, britannica-mauritania]
- 11th–12th century: **Foundation of the caravan ksour** — The fortified towns of Ouadane, Chinguetti, Tichitt and Oualata are founded to serve the trans-Saharan caravans, becoming trading and religious centres of Islamic culture. [unesco-ksour, wiki-ksour]
- c. 1644–1674: **The Char Bouba (Marabout) war** — Sanhaja Berber religious leaders fight the Arab Beni Hassan tribes; the war's outcome confirms Arab warrior dominance and the social hierarchy of Moorish society. [wiki-char-bouba, britannica-mauritania]
- late 17th century: **Rise of the Moorish emirates** — Moorish society consolidates into the emirates of Trarza, Brakna, Tagant and Adrar, each governed by an emir of the Hassan warrior elite. [britannica-mauritania, wiki-brakna]

## French Conquest and Pacification — 1900 – 1960

France advanced into the desert at the turn of the twentieth century, but resistance among the emirates and nomadic tribes meant the colony was not fully 'pacified' until the 1930s.

- 1902: **Coppolani's pacification campaign begins** — Xavier Coppolani launches France's drive to extend control over Mauritania, combining alliances with marabout tribes and military pressure on the emirates. [britannica-mauritania, wiki-history-mauritania]
- 1904: **Mauritania organised within French West Africa** — The territory is formally constituted as the civil territory of Mauritania within French West Africa. [wiki-history-mauritania, britannica-mauritania]
- 1905: **Assassination of Coppolani** — Xavier Coppolani is killed at Tidjikja, a setback to the French advance, though the conquest of the interior continues afterward. [wiki-history-mauritania, britannica-mauritania]
- 1930s: **Completion of the conquest** — After decades of resistance by the desert emirates and nomadic tribes, French military control over the far north of Mauritania is finally consolidated in the 1930s. [britannica-mauritania, wiki-history-mauritania]

## Independence and the Daddah Republic — 1958 – 1978

Mauritania became a sovereign Islamic republic in 1960 under Moktar Ould Daddah, built a new capital at Nouakchott, and then over-reached into the Western Sahara conflict.

- 1958: **Autonomous republic and Nouakchott** — Mauritania becomes an autonomous republic within the French Community, and the new capital of Nouakchott is established on the coast. [britannica-mauritania, blackpast-daddah]
- 28 November 1960: **Independence of the Islamic Republic** — Mauritania gains full independence from France; Moktar Ould Daddah leads the new state and becomes its first president in 1961. [britannica-daddah, blackpast-daddah, britannica-mauritania]
- 1973: **National currency and Arab orientation** — Mauritania leaves the franc zone and introduces the ouguiya, reflecting Ould Daddah's policy of national consolidation and a turn toward the Arab world. [britannica-mauritania, blackpast-daddah]
- 1976: **Annexation of southern Western Sahara** — Following the 1975 Madrid Accords, Mauritania and Morocco partition Western Sahara; Mauritania annexes the southern third as Tiris al-Gharbiyya, drawing it into war with the Polisario Front. [wiki-annexation-wsahara, britannica-mauritania]
- 10 July 1978: **Coup ends the Daddah era** — A military coup deposes President Moktar Ould Daddah after eighteen years in power, as the costly Western Sahara war destabilises the country. [britannica-daddah, britannica-mauritania]

Figures:

- Moktar Ould Daddah (1924–2003) — First president of Mauritania. The first Mauritanian to become a lawyer and the colony's first prime minister, he led the country to independence in 1960 and governed for eighteen years before being overthrown in 1978 amid the Western Sahara war.

## Coups, Withdrawal and the Modern State — 1978 – 2024

After withdrawing from Western Sahara, Mauritania endured a succession of military coups while confronting the deep social legacy of slavery, outlawed in 1981 and criminalised in 2007.

- 5 August 1979: **Withdrawal from Western Sahara** — Mauritania renounces its claim to Tiris al-Gharbiyya and withdraws its forces, ending its part in the Western Sahara war; Morocco then absorbs the vacated territory. [wiki-annexation-wsahara, britannica-polisario]
- 1981: **Slavery formally abolished** — Mauritania abolishes slavery by decree—frequently cited as the last country in the world to do so—though no penalties were initially attached to the ban. [antislavery-mauritania, minorityrights-mauritania]
- 12 December 1984: **Ould Taya takes power** — Colonel Maaouya Ould Sid'Ahmed Taya seizes power in a coup and goes on to rule Mauritania for some twenty years. [britannica-mauritania]
- August 2005: **Coup ends the Taya era** — A military council overthrows President Ould Taya, beginning a period of transition punctuated by a further coup in 2008. [britannica-mauritania]
- 2007: **Slavery criminalised** — A law makes slavery a criminal offence; the constitution later recognises it as a crime against humanity in 2012, although rights groups report that descent-based slavery has persisted in practice. [antislavery-mauritania, minorityrights-mauritania]
