Founding: The Velvet Divorce and an independent Slovakia — 1 January 1993. Independent Slovakia was born on 1 January 1993, when Czechoslovakia separated peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia in the so-called Velvet Divorce, the only nonviolent dissolution of a state in post-war Europe. After the June 1992 elections exposed deep differences between Czech and Slovak leaders over the pace of market reform and the shape of the federation, the two governments agreed to part rather than hold a referendum. The new Slovak Republic, with its capital at Bratislava, was the first fully sovereign Slovak state in history, even though the Slovak lands had been a distinct community for more than a thousand years, at the core of Great Moravia in the ninth century and then part of the Kingdom of Hungary from around 1000. Within little more than a decade the young republic joined NATO and the European Union in 2004 and adopted the euro in 2009.

Slovakia's history is the story of a Slavic people who held the same Carpathian uplands for more than a millennium while almost never ruling themselves. Their lands lay at the core of ninth-century Great Moravia, centred partly on Nitra, where the Byzantine missionaries Cyril and Methodius created a Slavonic liturgy and the first Slavic script. After Great Moravia fell to the Magyars, the Slovak lands were absorbed around the year 1000 into the Kingdom of Hungary, where they remained for roughly a thousand years as 'Upper Hungary', and during the Ottoman wars Bratislava (Pressburg) served as the kingdom's capital and coronation city. A national awakening in the nineteenth century, crowned by Ľudovít Štúr's codification of a Slovak literary language in the 1840s, gave the people a modern identity in the face of Magyarization. Statehood came only in 1918, when Slovaks joined the Czechs in Czechoslovakia; a clerical-authoritarian Slovak State allied to Nazi Germany followed in 1939, the deportation of Slovak Jews and the Slovak National Uprising of 1944, then restoration, communism and federalization. The Velvet Revolution of 1989 ended communist rule, and the peaceful 'Velvet Divorce' created an independent Slovakia on 1 January 1993. It joined NATO and the European Union in 2004 and adopted the euro in 2009.

## Great Moravia and the Mission of Cyril and Methodius — c. 800 – 907

The Slovak lands lay at the heart of the first great Slavic state, where two Byzantine brothers created a written language for the Slavs before the Magyars swept it away.

- c. 833: **Founding of Great Moravia** — Prince Mojmír I expelled Pribina of Nitra and united the principalities of Moravia and Nitra into Great Moravia, the first powerful Slavic state of central Europe, with the Slovak lands at its core. [slovakia-travel-history, moravia-britannica]
- 863: **Mission of Cyril and Methodius** — At Prince Rostislav's request the Byzantine brothers Cyril and Methodius arrived in Great Moravia, devised the Glagolitic script and translated the liturgy into Old Church Slavonic, founding a written Slavic culture. [cyril-methodius-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]
- c. 870–894: **Reign of Svatopluk I** — Under Svatopluk I Great Moravia reached its greatest extent and won recognition from the papacy, becoming the dominant power of the middle Danube before the state fragmented after his death. [svatopluk-britannica, moravia-britannica]
- 906–907: **Fall of Great Moravia to the Magyars** — Weakened by internal strife and Frankish wars, Great Moravia collapsed as the Magyars occupied the Carpathian Basin; a Magyar victory in 906–907 is taken to mark the end of the state. [moravia-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]

Figures:

- Saints Cyril and Methodius (9th century) — Byzantine missionaries to the Slavs. Sent from Constantinople in 863, the brothers Cyril and Methodius created the Glagolitic alphabet and translated the scriptures and liturgy into Old Church Slavonic, giving the Slavs their first written language and church.
- Svatopluk I (reigned c. 870 – 894) — Prince of Great Moravia. The greatest ruler of Great Moravia, Svatopluk expanded the realm to its widest extent and secured recognition of his independent standing from Pope John VIII, but his state did not long survive his death.

## A Thousand Years in the Kingdom of Hungary — c. 1000 – 1780

Absorbed into the Hungarian kingdom around the year 1000, the Slovak lands became 'Upper Hungary', and in the Ottoman age Bratislava rose to be the kingdom's capital and coronation city.

- c. 1000: **Incorporation into the Kingdom of Hungary** — The Slovak lands were absorbed into the Kingdom of Hungary founded by Stephen I, beginning roughly a thousand years under the Hungarian crown as the region known as 'Upper Hungary'. [slovakia-travel-history, slovakia-britannica]
- 1467: **Universitas Istropolitana at Pressburg** — Slovakia's first university, the Universitas Istropolitana, was founded at Pressburg, an early sign of the towns' wealth and learning within the Hungarian kingdom. [slovakia-travel-history]
- 1536: **Pressburg made capital of Hungary** — As the Ottomans overran central Hungary, the parliament promoted Pressburg (now Bratislava) to capital of the Kingdom of Hungary, a secure stronghold close to Habsburg Vienna. [slovakia-travel-history, bratislava-coronation]
- 1563–1830: **Pressburg the coronation city** — Between 1563 and 1830, eleven Hungarian kings and eight royal consorts were crowned at St Martin's Cathedral in Pressburg, including Maria Theresa in 1741, making the city the ceremonial heart of the kingdom. [bratislava-coronation]

Figures:

- Maria Theresa (1717 – 1780) — Archduchess of Austria and Queen of Hungary. Crowned Queen of Hungary at St Martin's Cathedral in Pressburg in 1741, Maria Theresa made the city a centre of her eastern kingdom during a long reign that reshaped the Habsburg lands the Slovaks inhabited.
- Stephen I of Hungary (c. 975 – 1038) — First king of Hungary. The founder and first crowned king of the Christian Kingdom of Hungary around the year 1000, whose new state absorbed the Slovak lands of former Great Moravia into a realm that would hold them for a millennium.

## The Slovak National Awakening — 1780 – 1918

Against the pressure of Magyarization, scholars and patriots forged a modern Slovak nation around a literary language codified by Ľudovít Štúr.

- 1780s: **Bernolák's first written Slovak** — The Catholic priest Anton Bernolák codified an early written Slovak based on western dialects, an opening move in the national awakening that the next generation would supersede. [slovak-literature-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]
- 1843: **Štúr codifies literary Slovak** — Ľudovít Štúr and his associates resolved to base a unified Slovak literary language on the central Slovak dialects; his standard, refined by later grammarians, became modern literary Slovak. [stur-britannica, slovak-literature-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]
- 1867: **Magyarization under the Compromise** — After the Austro-Hungarian Compromise the Hungarian government intensified Magyarization, pressing the Magyar language on Slovak schools and public life and denying the Slovaks recognition as a nation. [slovakia-britannica, slovak-literature-britannica]
- 1848–1849: **Slovaks in the revolutions of 1848** — During the revolutions of 1848 Slovak leaders, Štúr among them, set out national demands and raised volunteers, asserting the Slovaks as a distinct people within the Hungarian kingdom. [stur-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]

Figures:

- Ľudovít Štúr (1815 – 1856) — Codifier of the Slovak literary language. Scholar, poet and politician, Štúr led the Slovak national revival and in the 1840s codified a literary language from the central Slovak dialects, the basis of modern standard Slovak and a foundation of national identity.
- Anton Bernolák (1762 – 1813) — Priest and linguist. A Catholic priest who in the 1780s produced the first systematic attempt at a written Slovak, drawn from western dialects; though later superseded by Štúr's standard, his work began the modern history of the Slovak language.

## Czechoslovakia, the Slovak State and the Uprising — 1918 – 1945

Statehood arrived inside Czechoslovakia, was perverted into a wartime clerical regime allied to Hitler, and was reclaimed in the Slovak National Uprising of 1944.

- 28–30 Oct 1918: **Czechoslovakia and the Martin Declaration** — Czechoslovakia was declared in Prague on 28 October 1918; on 30 October the Slovak National Council endorsed the union in the Martin Declaration, giving the Slovaks their first modern statehood alongside the Czechs. [czechoslovakia-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]
- 14 Mar 1939: **Proclamation of the Slovak State** — As Germany dismembered Czechoslovakia, a separate Slovak State was proclaimed under the priest Jozef Tiso, a clerical-authoritarian regime that became a client and ally of Nazi Germany. [tiso-ushmm, holocaust-slovakia-ushmm]
- 1942: **Deportation of Slovak Jews** — Slovakia became the first Axis partner to consent to the deportation of its Jews; between March and October 1942 some 57,000 Slovak Jews were deported to German-occupied Poland, with more than 60,000 of Slovakia's Jews murdered overall. [holocaust-slovakia-ushmm, tiso-ushmm]
- 29 Aug 1944: **Slovak National Uprising** — The Slovak National Uprising broke out at Banská Bystrica against the Tiso regime and German occupation; after two months of fighting the rising was crushed on 27 October 1944 amid brutal reprisals. [snp-ww2museum, slovakia-travel-history]

Figures:

- Milan Rastislav Štefánik (1880 – 1919) — Co-founder of Czechoslovakia. A Slovak astronomer, general and diplomat, Štefánik worked with Masaryk and Beneš to win Allied support for a Czechoslovak state and to raise its legions abroad, becoming a national hero before his death in an air crash in 1919.
- Jozef Tiso (1887 – 1947) — President of the wartime Slovak State. A Catholic priest and leader of the Slovak People's Party, Tiso headed the clerical-authoritarian Slovak State allied to Nazi Germany, under which Slovakia deported tens of thousands of Jews. He was tried and executed for treason and collaboration in 1947.

## Communism, the Velvet Revolution and the Velvet Divorce — 1945 – 1993

Restored Czechoslovakia fell under Soviet-bloc communism, gained a federal Slovak republic in 1968, threw off one-party rule in 1989 and then parted peacefully into two states.

- 1948: **Communist takeover** — The Communist Party seized full power in Czechoslovakia, establishing a one-party state under Soviet domination that would last for four decades. [czechoslovakia-britannica, velvet-revolution-britannica]
- 1968–1969: **Federalization of Czechoslovakia** — After the Prague Spring was crushed, the Constitutional Law of Federation of 28 October 1968 created, from 1 January 1969, a Slovak Socialist Republic within a federal Czechoslovakia, the Slovaks' first republic in name. [czechoslovakia-britannica]
- Nov 1989: **The Velvet Revolution** — Beginning with a student march in Bratislava on 16 November and protests in Prague the next day, six weeks of demonstrations led the Communist Party to give up its monopoly, ending one-party rule peacefully. [velvet-revolution-britannica, slovakia-travel-history]
- 1 Jan 1993: **The Velvet Divorce** — Czechoslovakia split peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, creating, after more than a thousand years, the first fully independent Slovak state. [czechoslovakia-britannica, state-dept-slovakia]

Figures:

- Alexander Dubček (1921 – 1992) — Reform communist leader. A Slovak who as Communist Party leader launched the 1968 Prague Spring's 'socialism with a human face'; ousted after the Soviet invasion, he re-emerged in 1989 as a symbol of the Velvet Revolution and chaired the federal parliament.
- Vladimír Mečiar (born 1942) — First Prime Minister of independent Slovakia. The dominant Slovak politician of the early 1990s, Mečiar led the Slovak negotiations that produced the Velvet Divorce and became prime minister of the new republic, though his later authoritarian style delayed Slovakia's entry into NATO.

## Independent Slovakia in Europe — 1993 – present

The young republic anchored itself in the West, settled a Danube dispute with Hungary at the World Court, and joined NATO, the European Union and the euro.

- 25 Sep 1997: **ICJ judgment on Gabčíkovo–Nagymaros** — The International Court of Justice ruled in the Gabčíkovo–Nagymaros Project case (Hungary/Slovakia) that both states had breached their obligations over the Danube dam treaty of 1977, ordering them to negotiate in good faith. [icj-gabcikovo, icj-gabcikovo-summary]
- 29 Mar 2004: **Slovakia joins NATO** — Slovakia officially became a member of NATO on 29 March 2004, part of the largest single enlargement in the Alliance's history, anchoring the young republic in the Western security community. [nato-slovakia]
- 1 May 2004: **Accession to the European Union** — Slovakia joined the European Union on 1 May 2004, completing its integration into Western Europe little more than a decade after independence. [state-dept-slovakia, eu-slovakia-euro]
- 1 Jan 2009: **Slovakia adopts the euro** — Slovakia adopted the euro on 1 January 2009, becoming the sixteenth member of the euro area and the first of the Visegrád states to do so. [eu-slovakia-euro]

Figures:

- Michal Kováč (1930 – 2016) — First President of independent Slovakia. Elected by parliament in 1993 as the first head of state of the new Slovak Republic, Kováč defended the country's fledgling democratic institutions during the politically turbulent early years of independence.
- Andrej Kiska (born 1963) — President of Slovakia. A philanthropist elected president in 2014, Kiska led Slovakia during a period of full integration into the European Union and the euro area and became a prominent voice for the rule of law.
