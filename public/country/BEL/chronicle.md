# The Chronicle of Belgium: From the cloth towns of Flanders to the heart of Europe, a young kingdom carved from the old Low Countries.

Founding: The Belgian Revolution and the birth of an independent kingdom — 1830. Modern Belgium dates its independence to the revolution of 1830, when the southern provinces of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, Catholic and largely French-speaking, rose against rule from The Hague. Fighting broke out in Brussels in August and September 1830, a National Congress declared independence and adopted a liberal constitution, and in 1831 the German prince Leopold of Saxe-Coburg was installed as Leopold I, first King of the Belgians, taking his oath on 21 July 1831, still celebrated as the national day. The new state's existence, borders and status as a 'perpetually neutral' country were finally guaranteed by Austria, Britain, France, Prussia and Russia in the Treaty of London of 19 April 1839, the document Germany would tear up when it invaded in 1914.

Belgium occupies the southern half of the historic Low Countries, a crossroads of Germanic and Latin Europe that has been one of the continent's wealthiest and most fought-over corners. In the Middle Ages its cloth towns, Bruges, Ghent and Ypres, were among the richest cities north of the Alps, and the County of Flanders and the Duchy of Brabant were dense with self-governing burghers. Through marriage and inheritance these provinces passed to the dukes of Burgundy, then to the Habsburgs, becoming in turn the Spanish and the Austrian Netherlands; the revolt against Spain split the Low Countries permanently into a Protestant north and a Catholic south. After French annexation and a brief union with the Dutch, the southern provinces rose in the Belgian Revolution of 1830 and won independence under King Leopold I, their borders and perpetual neutrality guaranteed by the great powers in the Treaty of London of 1839. That neutrality, dismissed in 1914 by Germany as a 'scrap of paper', made Belgium the first battlefield of both World Wars. King Leopold II's brutal private rule over the Congo Free State, the slow federalization of a country divided by language, and Brussels' role as the seat of the European Union and NATO have all shaped the modern Belgian state.

## The Medieval Low Countries: Flanders, Brabant and the Cloth Towns — c. 850 – 1384

Between the Rhine and the North Sea, a cluster of counties and duchies grew rich on cloth and trade, their burghers winning liberties unmatched in feudal Europe.

- 11 Jul 1302: **Battle of the Golden Spurs** — A Flemish town militia, mostly craft guildsmen, destroyed a French army of knights near Courtrai, collecting the golden spurs of the slain nobles and asserting the independence of the Flemish towns. [golden-spurs-britannica, belgium-history-britannica]
- 12th–14th c.: **The Flemish cloth boom** — Importing English wool, Bruges, Ghent and Ypres became among the richest cloth-producing cities in Europe, with Bruges the leading port and financial centre of the north. [flemish-cloth-discovering, belgium-history-britannica]
- 1245: **Foundation of the beguinages** — Communities of beguines, women living a religious life without taking monastic vows, established enclosed beguinages in the Flemish towns; thirteen survive as a UNESCO World Heritage site bearing witness to this medieval tradition. [beguinages-unesco]
- 1384: **Flanders passes to Burgundy** — On the death of the last count, the County of Flanders was inherited by Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, beginning the gathering of the Low Countries under a single dynasty. [belgium-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Jacob van Artevelde (c. 1290 – 1345) — Flemish statesman of Ghent. A wealthy Ghent burgher who led the Flemish towns during the early Hundred Years' War, steering Flanders toward neutrality and the English wool trade on which its prosperity depended, until he was killed in a riot in 1345.
- Philip the Bold (1342 – 1404) — Duke of Burgundy, Count of Flanders. Through his marriage to the heiress of Flanders he acquired the county in 1384, beginning the Burgundian assembly of the Low Countries into a single, increasingly unified state.

## The Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands — 1384 – 1713

United by the dukes of Burgundy and inherited by the Habsburgs, the Low Countries flourished, then split apart in the revolt against Spain into a Protestant north and a Catholic south.

- 1477: **The Habsburg inheritance** — After Charles the Bold of Burgundy was killed, his daughter Mary married the Habsburg archduke Maximilian of Austria, passing the Low Countries to the Habsburg dynasty. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 1555: **Abdication of Charles V in Brussels** — The emperor Charles V, born in Ghent, abdicated the Netherlands to his son Philip II in Brussels in 1555, and the provinces passed under the rule of Habsburg Spain. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 1579: **Union of Arras and the split of the Low Countries** — The Catholic southern provinces reaffirmed loyalty to Spain in the Union of Arras while the northern provinces formed the Union of Utrecht, beginning the permanent division of the Low Countries into Belgium and the Netherlands. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 17 Aug 1585: **Fall of Antwerp** — After a siege of over a year, the Spanish commander Alessandro Farnese took the great Protestant port of Antwerp, confirming Spanish control of the southern Netherlands and driving its merchants and craftsmen north. [antwerp-britannica]
- 1713: **From Spanish to Austrian Netherlands** — By the Treaty of Utrecht ending the War of the Spanish Succession, the southern Netherlands passed from Spain to the Austrian Habsburgs, becoming the Austrian Netherlands. [belgium-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Charles V (1500 – 1558) — Holy Roman Emperor, lord of the Netherlands. Born in Ghent, Charles V ruled the seventeen provinces of the Low Countries at the heart of a global empire before abdicating in Brussels in 1555 and dividing his dominions, leaving the Netherlands to Spain.
- Peter Paul Rubens (1577 – 1640) — Painter of the Spanish Netherlands. The towering figure of Baroque art in the southern Netherlands, Rubens worked from Antwerp as both painter and diplomat, his vast output making the city a centre of Counter-Reformation culture under Spanish rule.

## Revolution, Independence and the Guaranteed Neutral Kingdom — 1713 – 1865

After French annexation and an unhappy union with the Dutch, the southern provinces rose in 1830 and won independence as a neutral kingdom guaranteed by the powers of Europe.

- 1795: **French annexation of the southern Netherlands** — Revolutionary France annexed the Austrian Netherlands and the prince-bishopric of Liège, ending Habsburg rule and governing the region directly from Paris for twenty years. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 1815: **Union with the Netherlands** — After Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo on Belgian soil, the Congress of Vienna merged the southern provinces with the Dutch into the United Kingdom of the Netherlands under William I, as a buffer against France. [belgium-history-britannica, treaty-london-fww]
- Aug–Sep 1830: **The Belgian Revolution** — Rebellion in Brussels drove out the Dutch garrison; a National Congress proclaimed independence and adopted a liberal constitution, breaking the southern provinces away from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 21 Jul 1831: **Leopold I, first King of the Belgians** — The German prince Leopold of Saxe-Coburg was installed as the first king and took his constitutional oath on 21 July 1831, a date still observed as Belgium's national day. [leopold1-britannica, belgium-history-britannica]
- 19 Apr 1839: **Treaty of London guarantees Belgian neutrality** — Austria, Britain, France, Prussia, Russia and the Netherlands recognised Belgium as an independent and perpetually neutral state and guaranteed its neutrality, the treaty later cited by Britain to enter the First World War. [treaty-london-fww, belgium-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Leopold I (1790 – 1865) — First King of the Belgians. A prince of Saxe-Coburg and widower of the British heir Princess Charlotte, Leopold accepted the Belgian crown in 1831 and ruled as a shrewd constitutional monarch, anchoring the new kingdom's stability and its place in European diplomacy.
- William I of the Netherlands (1772 – 1843) — King of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands. The Dutch king whose centralising rule and favouring of the north provoked the Belgian Revolution; he refused for years to accept the loss of the southern provinces, recognising Belgian independence only in 1839.

## Leopold II, the Congo, and the Curiosities of the Border — 1865 – 1914

An industrial kingdom acquired a vast and brutal private empire in Africa, while its own frontier sprouted some of Europe's strangest scraps of disputed sovereignty.

- 1865: **Accession of Leopold II** — Leopold II succeeded Leopold I as King of the Belgians, ruling an industrialising kingdom while pursuing his own colonial ambitions in central Africa. [leopold2-britannica]
- 1885: **Foundation of the Congo Free State** — After winning recognition at the Berlin Conference, Leopold II proclaimed the Congo Free State as his private possession, a regime whose forced labour and atrocities caused massive loss of life. [congo-free-state-britannica, leopold2-britannica]
- 1908: **Annexation of the Congo by Belgium** — Under international pressure over the atrocities, Leopold II was forced to cede his private colony to the Belgian state, which took it over as the Belgian Congo. [belgian-congo-britannica, congo-free-state-britannica]
- 1816–1920: **Neutral Moresnet** — Unable to agree on ownership of a village and its zinc mine, the powers created Neutral Moresnet, a tiny condominium of about 3.5 square kilometres jointly administered by Prussia and the Netherlands, then Prussia and Belgium, until the Treaty of Versailles awarded it to Belgium. [moresnet-vrt, eupen-malmedy-1418online]
- from the 12th c.: **The Baarle enclaves take shape** — Medieval land deals between the lords of Breda and the dukes of Brabant scattered sovereignty across Baarle, leaving the Belgian Baarle-Hertog as a cluster of enclaves inside the Netherlands, some containing Dutch counter-enclaves. [baarle-discovering]

Figures:

- Leopold II (1835 – 1909) — King of the Belgians, sovereign of the Congo Free State. King from 1865, Leopold II ruled a prosperous Belgium but acquired the Congo as a private domain in 1885; the brutal exploitation of the Congo Free State, which killed vast numbers of Africans, forced its transfer to Belgium in 1908 and remains his enduring infamy.
- Albert I (1875 – 1934) — King of the Belgians. Nephew of Leopold II, Albert I came to the throne in 1909 and would soon lead Belgian resistance to the German invasion of 1914, becoming a national hero as the 'Soldier King' on the Yser front.

## The Two World Wars and the Gains of Versailles — 1914 – 1945

Twice Germany violated Belgian neutrality and overran the country; out of the first war Belgium gained Eupen-Malmedy and a German-speaking minority of its own.

- 4 Aug 1914: **Germany invades neutral Belgium** — Executing the Schlieffen Plan, Germany invaded Belgium after King Albert I refused free passage; the violation of the 1839 neutrality treaty, dismissed as a 'scrap of paper', brought Britain into the First World War. [treaty-london-fww, belgium-history-britannica]
- Oct 1914: **The Battle of the Yser** — Falling back behind the river Yser, the Belgian army opened the sluices and flooded the polders to halt the German advance, holding a last strip of free Belgium around Nieuwpoort and Diksmuide for the rest of the war. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 1919–1920: **Versailles: Eupen-Malmedy and the end of Moresnet** — The Treaty of Versailles ended Belgian neutrality, abolished Neutral Moresnet and transferred Eupen, Malmedy and Sankt Vith from Germany to Belgium, giving the kingdom a German-speaking minority after a much-criticised popular consultation in 1920. [eupen-malmedy-1418online, moresnet-vrt, eupen-malmedy-britannica]
- 10 May 1940: **Second German invasion** — Germany again invaded neutral Belgium on 10 May 1940, this time thrusting through the Ardennes, and rapidly overwhelmed the defending Belgian, French and British forces. [ushmm-1940, belgium-history-britannica]
- 28 May 1940: **Surrender of King Leopold III** — After eighteen days of fighting Leopold III surrendered the Belgian army without consulting his government, beginning four years of German occupation and a political crisis over the king's conduct. [ushmm-1940, belgium-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Albert I (1875 – 1934) — King of the Belgians, the 'Soldier King'. King Albert I personally led the Belgian army's resistance in 1914 and shared the Yser front with his troops, becoming a national hero of the First World War before dying in a mountaineering accident in 1934.
- Leopold III (1901 – 1983) — King of the Belgians. Albert's son, Leopold III surrendered the army to Germany on 28 May 1940 and remained in occupied Belgium; the controversy over his wartime conduct, the 'Royal Question', led to his abdication in favour of his son Baudouin in 1951.

## A Federal Kingdom at the Heart of Europe — 1945 – present

A founder of European integration and NATO, post-war Belgium remade itself from a unitary state into a federation of language communities and regions.

- 4 Apr 1949: **Founding member of NATO** — Belgium was one of twelve states that signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington on 4 April 1949; NATO headquarters later moved to Brussels in 1967. [nato-belgium-gov, nato-focus-belgium]
- 1951–1957: **Founding the European Community** — Belgium was one of the six founders of the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951 and the European Economic Community by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, with Brussels becoming the centre of European integration. [eu-history-europa, nato-focus-belgium]
- 30 Jun 1960: **Independence of the Congo** — The Belgian Congo became the independent Democratic Republic of the Congo, ending Belgium's central African colony amid turmoil and a swift descent into crisis. [belgian-congo-britannica]
- 1962–1963: **The linguistic frontier fixed** — Language laws drew a fixed border between Dutch-, French- and German-speaking areas and created four language regions, entrenching the divide between Belgium's communities. [belgium-history-britannica]
- 1993: **Belgium becomes a federal state** — The fourth state reform amended the constitution to declare Belgium a federal state composed of Communities and Regions, completing the devolution of the once-unitary kingdom. [belgium-history-britannica, eupen-malmedy-britannica]

Figures:

- Paul-Henri Spaak (1899 – 1972) — Statesman, architect of European integration. Repeatedly Belgium's prime and foreign minister, Spaak was a leading founder of Benelux, the European Communities and NATO, serving as the alliance's secretary general, and is counted among the principal architects of post-war Western Europe.
- Baudouin (1930 – 1993) — King of the Belgians. Coming to the throne in 1951 after the abdication of his father Leopold III, Baudouin reigned for over four decades, presiding over the decolonisation of the Congo and the long transformation of Belgium into a federal state.
