Founding: Independence from Spain — 20 July 1810. Colombia traces its birth as a nation to 20 July 1810, when the patriots of Santa Fe de Bogota deposed the Spanish authorities and formed a governing junta in the 'Cry of Independence' (Grito de Independencia), the date still marked as the national day. Full independence was secured by Simon Bolivar's decisive victory at the Battle of Boyaca on 7 August 1819, after which the liberated territory of New Granada joined Bolivar's union of Gran Colombia. When that union dissolved in 1830 the country continued as New Granada and, from the constitution of 1886, took the name it bears today, the Republic of Colombia.

Colombia's story begins with the chiefdoms of the northern Andes, above all the Muisca, whose ritual offerings of gold gave rise to the legend of El Dorado, and the megalith carvers of San Agustin and the tomb builders of Tierradentro. Spanish conquistadors led by Gonzalo Jimenez de Quesada founded Santa Fe de Bogota in 1538, and the territory became the heart of the Viceroyalty of New Granada, with the fortified Caribbean port of Cartagena guarding the empire's wealth. Independence was won between 1810 and 1819, crowned by Simon Bolivar's victory at Boyaca, and for a decade Colombia formed part of Bolivar's Gran Colombia before it broke apart in 1830. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries were scarred by Liberal-Conservative wars, the loss of Panama, the bloodletting of La Violencia, and a long internal armed conflict, before the 2016 peace accord with the FARC opened a new and still-contested chapter.

## Goldsmiths and Megalith Carvers — Pre-1500

Long before any Spanish sail reached the Caribbean coast, the highlands and valleys of present-day Colombia were home to chiefdoms whose mastery of gold and stone produced the legend of El Dorado and the necropolises of San Agustin and Tierradentro.

- 1st-8th c. CE: **San Agustin megaliths** — In the upper Magdalena valley, the San Agustin culture carves the largest assemblage of monumental religious statuary in South America, later a UNESCO World Heritage site. [unesco-san-agustin]
- 6th-10th c. CE: **Tierradentro tombs** — The people of Tierradentro hew vast painted underground burial chambers into the mountains, a unique record of a developed pre-Hispanic Andean society. [unesco-tierradentro]
- c. 800-1500 CE: **Rise of the Muisca** — The Muisca chiefdoms flourish on the high plateau around Bogota and Tunja, celebrated for goldwork, salt, and trade across the Andes. [britannica-colombia, banrep-museo-oro]
- by 1500 CE: **The El Dorado tradition** — At Lake Guatavita, Muisca ritual offerings of gold to the waters give rise to the legend of El Dorado that would lure Spanish expeditions inland. [banrep-museo-oro, britannica-colombia]

## Conquest and the Viceroyalty of New Granada — 1499 - 1810

Spanish conquistadors overran the Muisca highlands in the 1530s, founded Santa Fe de Bogota, and built a colonial society whose wealth flowed through the fortified Caribbean port of Cartagena and was governed from the Viceroyalty of New Granada.

- 6 Aug 1538: **Founding of Bogota** — Gonzalo Jimenez de Quesada founds Santa Fe de Bogota after subduing the Muisca, establishing the capital of the New Kingdom of Granada. [britannica-colombia, britannica-bogota]
- 1549 CE: **Audiencia of Bogota** — The crown establishes a royal court and administrative tribunal at Bogota, opening the formal colonial era in New Granada. [britannica-colombia]
- 16th-18th c.: **Cartagena fortified** — Cartagena de Indias is built into the most complete fortified port in South America, guarding the empire's wealth against repeated pirate and naval assaults. [unesco-cartagena]
- 1717, restored 1739: **Viceroyalty of New Granada** — Spain governs the territory through the Viceroyalty of New Granada, capital Bogota, encompassing modern Colombia, Panama, Ecuador, and Venezuela. [britannica-new-granada]
- 1781 CE: **The Comunero revolt** — A popular rising against new Bourbon taxes sweeps the interior, an early sign of the unrest that would lead to the independence movement. [britannica-colombia, loc-colombia]

Figures:

- Gonzalo Jimenez de Quesada (c. 1509-1579) — Conquistador and founder of Bogota. Lawyer turned military commander who led the harrowing expedition up the Magdalena into the Muisca highlands and founded Santa Fe de Bogota in 1538, opening the conquest of the territory that became Colombia.

## Independence and Gran Colombia — 1810 - 1830

A movement that began in Bogota in 1810 became, through years of war, the liberation of New Granada at the Battle of Boyaca in 1819, and the heart of Simon Bolivar's vast republic of Gran Colombia, which broke apart within a decade.

- 20 Jul 1810: **Cry of independence** — A junta seizes power in Bogota, beginning the New Granadan independence movement amid the crisis of Spanish royal authority. [britannica-colombia]
- 7 Aug 1819: **Battle of Boyaca** — Bolivar and Santander rout the royalist army at the Boyaca bridge, securing the liberation of New Granada and opening the road to Bogota. [britannica-boyaca, britannica-bolivar]
- 1821 CE: **Congress of Cucuta** — The republic of Gran Colombia is formally organised, uniting New Granada, Venezuela, and Ecuador under Bolivar's presidency. [britannica-gran-colombia]
- 1830 CE: **Breakup of Gran Colombia** — Venezuela and Ecuador secede and Bolivar dies near Santa Marta; the union dissolves, leaving New Granada as a separate state. [britannica-gran-colombia, britannica-bolivar]

Figures:

- Simon Bolivar (1783-1830) — The Liberator, President of Gran Colombia. Venezuelan-born revolutionary who led the liberation of New Granada at Boyaca in 1819 and founded Gran Colombia. His dream of a single great republic fractured before his death near Santa Marta in 1830.
- Francisco de Paula Santander (1792-1840) — General and vice president. New Granadan commander who fought alongside Bolivar at Boyaca and served as vice president of Gran Colombia. A champion of constitutional, federalist government, he became a founding figure of the later Colombian republic, remembered as the Man of Laws.

## Partisan Wars and the Loss of Panama — 1830 - 1930

The nineteenth-century republic was repeatedly torn by civil wars between Liberals and Conservatives over the church, the constitution, and the structure of the state, culminating in the catastrophic War of a Thousand Days and the secession of Panama in 1903.

- 1863 CE: **United States of Colombia** — A Liberal-led federal constitution reorganises the country as the loosely united States of Colombia, intensifying partisan and regional rivalry. [britannica-colombia, loc-colombia]
- 1886 CE: **The Constitution of 1886** — A Conservative-led charter creates a centralised Republic of Colombia with a strong presidency and a renewed alliance with the Catholic Church. [britannica-colombia]
- 1899-1902: **War of a Thousand Days** — Liberals and the Conservative government fight Colombia's deadliest civil war, with an estimated 100,000 dead, leaving the country ruined. [britannica-thousand-days]
- 3 Nov 1903: **Separation of Panama** — Backed by U.S. warships, Panama secedes after Colombia rejects the canal treaty; the United States recognises it and secures the canal zone. [history-state-panama, britannica-thousand-days]

## La Violencia and the National Front — 1930 - 1964

The mid-century brought modernisation and the rise of the magic-realist imagination of Gabriel Garcia Marquez, but also the assassination that ignited La Violencia, a decade of partisan slaughter that the two parties ended only by agreeing to share power.

- 9 Apr 1948: **Assassination of Gaitan and the Bogotazo** — The killing of Liberal leader Jorge Eliecer Gaitan triggers the Bogotazo riots that devastate Bogota and ignite a decade of bloodshed. [britannica-colombia, loc-colombia]
- c. 1948-1958: **La Violencia** — Partisan warfare between Liberals and Conservatives ravages the countryside, killing an estimated two hundred thousand or more Colombians. [britannica-colombia, loc-colombia]
- 1958-1974: **The National Front** — Liberals and Conservatives agree to alternate the presidency and share offices, ending partisan war but closing politics to other movements. [britannica-colombia]
- 1958 CE: **Garcia Marquez and Macondo** — The journalist and novelist Gabriel Garcia Marquez gives literary form to Colombia's history of violence and wonder, later winning the 1982 Nobel Prize. [britannica-garcia-marquez]

Figures:

- Jorge Eliecer Gaitan (1903-1948) — Liberal leader and orator. Charismatic Liberal politician who electrified the urban poor and rural campesinos with calls for social justice. His assassination in Bogota on 9 April 1948 set off the Bogotazo and the era of La Violencia.
- Gabriel Garcia Marquez (1927-2014) — Novelist and Nobel laureate. Colombian writer whose masterpiece One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967) made the fictional town of Macondo a mirror of Latin American history. He won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1982 and became the foremost exponent of magic realism.

## Armed Conflict and the Search for Peace — 1964 - present

From the 1960s Colombia was gripped by a long internal armed conflict among leftist guerrillas, right-wing paramilitaries, the state, and drug-trafficking organisations, a war the 2016 peace accord with the FARC sought to end even as violence and the legacies of the drug trade persisted.

- 1964 CE: **Founding of the FARC** — Peasant self-defence groups consolidate into the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, the largest of the leftist guerrilla movements. [britannica-farc, britannica-colombia]
- 1980s-1990s: **Cartels and paramilitaries** — Cocaine cartels and right-wing paramilitary forces intensify the conflict with assassinations, bombings, kidnappings, and massacres of civilians. [britannica-colombia]
- 2016 CE: **Peace accord with the FARC** — After four years of talks in Havana, the Santos government and the FARC sign a peace agreement; Santos receives the Nobel Peace Prize. [nobel-santos, britannica-colombia]
- 2022 CE: **Truth Commission report** — The official Truth Commission estimates about 450,000 killed in 1985-2018 and millions displaced, the great majority of the dead civilians. [truth-commission]
- 2022 CE: **Election of Gustavo Petro** — Colombia elects Gustavo Petro, a former guerrilla, as its first leftist president, pledging a policy of total peace with remaining armed groups. [britannica-colombia]

Figures:

- Juan Manuel Santos (born 1951) — President and Nobel laureate. President of Colombia from 2010 to 2018 who pursued the negotiations in Havana that produced the 2016 peace agreement with the FARC, for which he was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.
