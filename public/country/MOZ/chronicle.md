# The Chronicle of Mozambique: From the gold ports of the Swahili coast to Portuguese conquest, a hard-won independence and a long road out of war.

Founding: Independence from Portugal — 25 June 1975. Mozambique became an independent state on 25 June 1975, ending nearly five centuries of Portuguese presence on its coast and some eight decades of formal colonial rule over the interior. Independence followed the Carnation Revolution of April 1974 in Lisbon, which toppled the Estado Novo dictatorship, and the Lusaka Accord of 7 September 1974, by which Portugal agreed to transfer power to the liberation front FRELIMO. Samora Machel, FRELIMO's leader since the assassination of Eduardo Mondlane in 1969, became the first president of the People's Republic of Mozambique. The handover was accompanied by the departure of most of the territory's roughly 200,000–250,000 Portuguese settlers, taking with them much of the country's skilled workforce and capital.

For a thousand years Mozambique's coast lay at the heart of the Indian Ocean trade, its town of Sofala channelling the gold of Great Zimbabwe and the Mutapa state out to Arab, Swahili and Indian merchants, while the Island of Mozambique grew into a cosmopolitan Afro-Islamic port. Vasco da Gama reached the coast in 1498, and over the following century the Portuguese seized the trade, fortified the island with the great Fort São Sebastião, and pushed up the Zambezi where settlers held semi-feudal prazo estates. Effective colonial rule came only in the 1890s, with the defeat of Gungunhana's Gaza Empire in 1895 and the grant of vast territories to chartered companies that imposed forced cultivation of cotton and the forced-labour system known as chibalo. The liberation front FRELIMO launched a war of independence in 1964 under Eduardo Mondlane and then Samora Machel, winning independence on 25 June 1975. Almost at once the new state was engulfed in a devastating civil war against RENAMO, backed by Rhodesia and apartheid South Africa, which killed about a million people before the Rome General Peace Accords of 1992. Modern Mozambique has held multiparty elections since 1994 and discovered vast offshore gas reserves, but since 2017 has faced a brutal insurgency in the northern province of Cabo Delgado.

## The Swahili Coast and the Gold of Sofala — c. 900 – 1498 CE

Coastal towns from Sofala to the Island of Mozambique grew rich channelling the gold of the interior into the wider Indian Ocean world.

- 10th century CE: **Sofala recorded as a gold port** — The geographer al-Masʿudi describes Muslim trade in the Sofala region; Sofala becomes the chief outlet for gold carried down from the interior highlands. [britannica-sofala, africanhistoryextra-towns, wiki-sofala]
- c. 11th–15th century: **Coastal trading towns flourish** — Settlements at Sofala, Angoche, the Island of Mozambique, the Querimba Islands and the Zambezi mouth grow into nodes of the Swahili Indian Ocean trade. [britannica-moz, africanhistoryextra-towns]
- 14th–15th century: **Kilwa controls the Sofala gold trade** — The sultanate of Kilwa dominates the export of interior gold through Sofala, drawing wealth from the Zimbabwe and Mutapa goldfields into the wider Islamic world. [britannica-sofala, wiki-sofala]
- c. 15th century: **Rise of the Mutapa state** — The Mutapa (Monomotapa) state emerges on the Zimbabwean plateau, its gold and ivory passing down the Zambezi and out through the Mozambican ports. [britannica-moz, africanhistoryextra-towns]

## Da Gama and the Portuguese Seizure of the Coast — 1498 – 1700

Vasco da Gama opened a route to India past the Mozambican coast; within a century the Portuguese had seized the gold trade and fortified the Island of Mozambique.

- 2 March 1498: **Vasco da Gama reaches the coast** — Bound for India, Vasco da Gama anchors off the Island of Mozambique, where Muslim merchants receive him with suspicion; he sails on, marking the first European contact with the coast. [britannica-dagama, wiki-history-moz, marinersmuseum-dagama]
- 1505: **Portuguese occupy Sofala** — The Portuguese seize Sofala and build a fort to capture the gold trade, but find the port in decline and shift their base to the Island of Mozambique. [britannica-moz, wiki-history-moz]
- 1522: **Chapel of Nossa Senhora do Baluarte built** — On the Island of Mozambique the Portuguese raise the small chapel of Nossa Senhora do Baluarte, often called the oldest surviving European building in the southern hemisphere. [unesco-ilha, natgeo-ilha]
- 1558 – c. 1620: **Fort São Sebastião is built** — Construction begins on Fort São Sebastião on the Island of Mozambique, the largest Portuguese fortress in East Africa and the oldest complete fort still standing in sub-Saharan Africa. [unesco-ilha, wiki-fort-saosebastiao, natgeo-ilha]
- 1698: **Omanis take Fort Jesus at Mombasa** — Omani Arabs capture Fort Jesus in Mombasa, rolling back Portuguese power on the northern Swahili coast and confining it largely to the territory of modern Mozambique. [britannica-moz, wiki-history-moz]

Figures:

- Vasco da Gama (c. 1460s–1524) — Portuguese navigator. Commanding the first fleet to sail from Europe to India around Africa, da Gama anchored off the Island of Mozambique in 1498, opening the era of Portuguese intrusion into the Indian Ocean trade.

## Prazos, the Gaza Empire and Conquest — 1700 – 1918

Semi-feudal estates lined the Zambezi and the Nguni built the Gaza Empire, until Portugal conquered the interior and defeated Gungunhana in 1895.

- 18th–19th century: **Prazo estates along the Zambezi** — The crown leases great estates, the prazos da coroa, to settlers along the Zambezi; their holders rule as semi-feudal lords with armies of slave-soldiers and a heavy stake in the slave trade. [wiki-prazo, britannica-moz, historyrise-zambezia]
- c. 1820s: **Soshangane founds the Gaza Empire** — Nguni warriors fleeing the upheavals of the Mfecane, led by Soshangane, conquer much of southern Mozambique and establish the Gaza Empire. [wiki-gaza-empire, wiki-gungunhana]
- 1884–1885: **Berlin Conference and the scramble** — The Berlin Conference requires Portugal to occupy the territory it claims, spurring a drive to subdue the Mozambican interior. [britannica-moz, wiki-history-moz]
- 1895: **Defeat and capture of Gungunhana** — Portuguese forces crush the Gaza army at Coolela and capture King Gungunhana at Chaimite, ending the Gaza Empire; he is exiled to the Azores, where he dies. [wiki-gungunhana, wiki-gaza-empire, britannica-moz]

Figures:

- Gungunhana (Ngungunyane) (c. 1850–1906) — Last king of the Gaza Empire. Grandson of Soshangane, Gungunhana led the Gaza Empire until his defeat and capture by the Portuguese in 1895; exiled to the Azores, he became an enduring symbol of resistance to colonial conquest.

## Chartered Companies and Forced Labour — 1891 – 1962

Vast concessions to chartered companies, forced cultivation of cotton and the chibalo labour system made Portuguese rule among the harshest in colonial Africa.

- 1891: **Mozambique Company chartered** — The Mozambique Company receives a vast concession with powers to tax, govern and recruit labour, one of several chartered companies to which Portugal subcontracts colonial rule. [britannica-moz, wiki-history-moz, historyrise-zambezia]
- 1926: **Estado Novo and tightened control** — The authoritarian Estado Novo regime takes shape in Portugal, hardening colonial policy and entrenching forced labour and compulsory cultivation in Mozambique. [britannica-moz, wiki-chibalo]
- 1930s onward: **Forced cotton cultivation and chibalo** — Compulsory cotton-growing for Portuguese industry and the chibalo forced-labour system compel Mozambicans to grow cash crops and build infrastructure, causing hardship and hunger. [wiki-chibalo, slafnet-moz, britannica-moz]
- 1951: **Colonies redesignated 'overseas provinces'** — Portugal renames its African colonies overseas provinces to resist decolonisation, while withholding citizenship and political rights from the African majority. [britannica-moz, wiki-history-moz]

## FRELIMO, Liberation War and Independence — 1962 – 1975

FRELIMO united the nationalist movements and fought a decade-long war that, with the fall of Portugal's dictatorship, won independence on 25 June 1975.

- 1962: **FRELIMO is founded** — Exiled nationalist groups unite in Dar es Salaam to form FRELIMO under Eduardo Mondlane, dedicated to ending Portuguese rule in Mozambique. [ebsco-frelimo, wiki-war-of-independence, wiki-mondlane]
- 25 September 1964: **War of independence begins** — FRELIMO launches its first armed attacks in Cabo Delgado, opening a decade-long guerrilla war against Portuguese colonial forces. [wiki-war-of-independence, ebsco-frelimo]
- 3 February 1969: **Assassination of Eduardo Mondlane** — FRELIMO's founding president is killed by a parcel bomb in Dar es Salaam; after a leadership struggle, Samora Machel emerges at the head of the movement. [wiki-mondlane, wiki-machel]
- 7 September 1974: **Carnation Revolution and the Lusaka Accord** — Lisbon's Carnation Revolution of April 1974 ends the dictatorship; in September Portugal and FRELIMO sign the Lusaka Accord, agreeing a transition to independence. [conciliation-context, wiki-war-of-independence]
- 25 June 1975: **Independence of Mozambique** — The People's Republic of Mozambique is proclaimed with Samora Machel as first president; most of the colony's Portuguese settlers depart, taking skills and capital with them. [britannica-moz, wiki-machel, wiki-history-moz]

Figures:

- Eduardo Mondlane (1920–1969) — Founding president of FRELIMO. A US-educated sociologist, Mondlane united Mozambique's nationalist movements into FRELIMO in 1962 and led its early liberation struggle until his assassination by parcel bomb in 1969.
- Samora Machel (1933–1986) — First president of Mozambique. FRELIMO's military commander and successor to Mondlane, Machel led the movement to victory and became Mozambique's first president in 1975, governing a Marxist-Leninist state until his death in an air crash in 1986.

## Civil War, Peace and Modern Mozambique — 1976 – 2026

A devastating civil war ended at Rome in 1992; multiparty democracy, offshore gas and a northern insurgency have shaped the decades since.

- c. 1977: **Civil war and the rise of RENAMO** — RENAMO, backed first by Rhodesia and then by apartheid South Africa, wages war on the FRELIMO government; the conflict devastates the countryside for fifteen years. [blackpast-civil-war, conciliation-context, peaceagreements-moz]
- 19 October 1986: **Death of Samora Machel** — President Machel dies when his aircraft crashes near the South African border; Joaquim Chissano succeeds him and begins to move FRELIMO toward reform and negotiation. [wiki-machel, wiki-history-moz]
- 4 October 1992: **Rome General Peace Accords** — Mediated by the Community of Sant'Egidio, President Chissano and RENAMO leader Afonso Dhlakama sign a peace agreement in Rome, ending a war that killed about a million people. [wiki-rome-accords, conciliation-context, vatican-rome]
- 1994: **First multiparty elections** — Under UN supervision Mozambique holds its first multiparty elections, won by FRELIMO; the following year the country joins the Commonwealth. [conciliation-context, wiki-history-moz]
- 2010–2011: **Offshore gas discovered in the Rovuma basin** — Major natural-gas finds in the deep waters of the Rovuma basin attract tens of billions of dollars in planned investment and reshape the country's economic prospects. [opendemocracy-gas, theconversation-gas, wiki-coral-field]
- 2017 onward: **Cabo Delgado insurgency** — An Islamist insurgency erupts in Cabo Delgado; its 2021 assault on Palma halts the main gas project, and the violence kills thousands and displaces close to a million people. [opendemocracy-gas, theconversation-gas, defenceweb-cabo]

Figures:

- Joaquim Chissano (born 1939) — President of Mozambique 1986–2005. Succeeding Machel in 1986, Chissano steered FRELIMO away from Marxism, signed the 1992 Rome peace accords ending the civil war, and won the country's first multiparty elections in 1994.
