# The Chronicle of Tanzania: From the footprints of Laetoli and the gold of Kilwa to Nyerere's union and a modern East African republic.

Founding: Independence of Tanganyika from the United Kingdom — 9 December 1961. Mainland Tanganyika became an independent state on 9 December 1961, ending British rule that had begun as a League of Nations mandate after the First World War. Independence was won not by armed struggle but through constitutional negotiation led by Julius Nyerere and the Tanganyika African National Union (TANU), which had swept the territory's elections; Nyerere became prime minister, and on 9 December 1962 the country became a republic with him as its first president. The modern state of Tanzania was formed afterwards: following the Zanzibar Revolution of January 1964, Tanganyika and the islands of Zanzibar united on 26 April 1964 to create the United Republic of Tanzania, with Nyerere as president and Zanzibar's Abeid Karume as vice-president.

Tanzania holds some of the oldest evidence of humankind on earth: at Laetoli, near Olduvai Gorge in the north, lie 3.6-million-year-old hominin footprints, while the gorge itself yielded the fossils and stone tools that made the Leakey family famous and the region a cradle of humanity. Over later millennia Bantu-speaking farmers settled the interior, and along the Indian Ocean shore a literate, Muslim Swahili civilisation arose, its greatest city — Kilwa Kisiwani — growing rich on the medieval gold trade. The Portuguese disrupted the coast after Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1498, and from the 1830s the Omani Arab sultanate of Zanzibar dominated it, building a clove-plantation economy on a brutal slave and ivory trade. Germany seized the mainland as German East Africa from the 1880s, crushing the Maji Maji rebellion of 1905–07 with a scorched-earth campaign that brought catastrophic famine; after the First World War Britain held mainland Tanganyika as a League of Nations mandate. Tanganyika won independence under Julius Nyerere and TANU on 9 December 1961, and after the Zanzibar Revolution of January 1964 the two states united on 26 April 1964 to form Tanzania, which Nyerere then steered toward Ujamaa socialism through the 1967 Arusha Declaration.

## Cradle of Humankind: Olduvai and Laetoli — c. 3.7 million years ago – c. 1000 BCE

The gorges and plains of northern Tanzania preserve some of the oldest traces of our ancestors anywhere on earth.

- c. 3.6 million years ago: **Hominins leave footprints at Laetoli** — Early hominins, attributed to Australopithecus afarensis, cross a layer of fresh volcanic ash near Laetoli; their footprints harden and survive, giving direct evidence of upright walking. [britannica-laetoli, wiki-laetoli, ncaa-laetoli]
- c. 1.8 million years ago: **Homo habilis and Oldowan tools at Olduvai** — Early members of the genus Homo at Olduvai Gorge make simple flaked-pebble tools of the Oldowan industry, among the earliest manufactured implements known. [wiki-olduvai, iugs-laetoli-olduvai]
- July 1959: **Mary Leakey finds the Zinjanthropus skull** — At Olduvai Gorge, Mary Leakey uncovers the robust hominin skull OH 5 (Zinjanthropus / Paranthropus boisei), a landmark in the study of human evolution. [wiki-olduvai, wiki-mary-leakey]
- 1976–1978: **Discovery of the Laetoli footprints** — Mary Leakey's team excavates the trail of 3.6-million-year-old hominin footprints at Laetoli, confirming early bipedalism and cementing Tanzania's place in human prehistory. [britannica-laetoli, wiki-laetoli, ncaa-laetoli]

Figures:

- Mary Leakey (1913–1996) — Palaeoanthropologist. British archaeologist whose discoveries at Olduvai Gorge — including the Zinjanthropus skull in 1959 — and her excavation of the Laetoli footprints in the 1970s transformed the study of human origins.

## Bantu Settlers and the Swahili Coast — c. 1000 BCE – 1498 CE

Iron-using Bantu farmers settled the interior while a wealthy, Muslim Swahili civilisation rose along the Indian Ocean shore.

- late 1st millennium BCE: **Bantu farmers reach the coast** — Iron-using Bantu-speaking farmers settle the Tanzanian coast and interior, part of the wider Bantu expansion that reshaped eastern and southern Africa. [wiki-history-tanzania, wiki-swahili-language]
- c. 8th–10th century CE: **Rise of the Swahili coast** — Coastal communities trading across the Indian Ocean develop the Swahili language and an Afro-Islamic urban culture, founding settlements that grow into trading city-states. [britannica-swahili-language, wiki-swahili-language]
- c. 11th–12th century: **Shirazi dynasty founds Kilwa** — According to the Kilwa Chronicle, the Shirazi line founds the ruling house of Kilwa Kisiwani; the city begins its rise as a Swahili trading power. [whc-kilwa, wiki-kilwa]
- 13th–15th centuries: **Kilwa dominates the gold trade** — Controlling the gold exported through Sofala, Kilwa reaches its peak of wealth, minting coins and building the Great Mosque, the largest in the region. [whc-kilwa, wiki-kilwa, natgeo-kilwa]
- 1331: **Ibn Battuta visits Kilwa** — The traveller Ibn Battuta describes Kilwa as a prosperous, devout and beautifully built Muslim city, attesting to the wealth of the medieval Swahili coast. [whc-kilwa, wiki-kilwa]

## The Portuguese and Omani Zanzibar — 1498 – 1884

European intrusion and then Omani Arab dominance reshaped the coast, with Zanzibar becoming the hub of a clove economy built on the slave and ivory trade.

- 1498: **Vasco da Gama reaches the coast** — The Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama arrives on the East African coast en route to India, opening an era of Portuguese intrusion that disrupted the Swahili city-states. [wiki-portuguese-east-africa, worldhistory-portuguese-east-africa]
- 1840: **Said bin Sultan moves his capital to Zanzibar** — The Omani ruler Said bin Sultan transfers his capital from Muscat to Zanzibar, making the islands the commercial heart of the western Indian Ocean. [britannica-said-ibn-sultan, blackpast-zanzibar-sultanate]
- mid-19th century: **Clove plantations and the caravan trade** — Zanzibar and Pemba become the world's leading clove producers, while caravans reaching to Lake Tanganyika feed a vast and brutal trade in enslaved people and ivory. [britannica-said-ibn-sultan, blackpast-zanzibar-sultanate, wiki-slavery-zanzibar]
- 1856: **Zanzibar becomes a separate sultanate** — On Said's death his domains are divided; his son Majid becomes the first sultan of Zanzibar, separating the islands from Oman. [blackpast-zanzibar-sultanate, britannica-said-ibn-sultan]
- 1873: **Zanzibar bans the seaborne slave trade** — Under British pressure Sultan Barghash signs a treaty prohibiting the maritime slave trade and closing the Stone Town slave market, though domestic slavery endures for decades. [wiki-slavery-zanzibar, ebsco-zanzibar-slavery]

Figures:

- Said bin Sultan (1791–1856) — Sultan of Oman and Zanzibar. Omani ruler who moved his capital to Zanzibar in 1840, built its clove-plantation economy and made it the dominant commercial power of the western Indian Ocean, while presiding over its slave and ivory trade.

## German East Africa and the British Mandate — 1884 – 1961

Germany seized the mainland and crushed the Maji Maji rebellion; after the First World War Britain ruled Tanganyika under a League of Nations mandate.

- 1885: **Germany establishes German East Africa** — Following Carl Peters's treaties, the German government charters his company, founding the colony of German East Africa over the Tanzanian mainland and the Great Lakes territories. [britannica-german-east-africa, worldhistory-german-east-africa]
- 1905–1907: **Maji Maji rebellion** — Inspired by the prophet Kinjikitile Ngwale, southern peoples rise against German rule, forced labour and cotton-growing; the revolt spreads widely before being crushed. [britannica-maji-maji, wiki-maji-maji, blackpast-maji-maji]
- 1906–1907: **Scorched earth and the Great Hunger** — German forces suppress the rebellion by destroying crops and villages; the ensuing famine kills tens to hundreds of thousands of people, a catastrophe scholars term genocidal. [wiki-maji-maji, blackpast-maji-maji]
- 1919–1922: **Tanganyika becomes a British mandate** — After the First World War the Treaty of Versailles strips Germany of the colony; Britain administers the mainland as the Tanganyika Territory under a League of Nations mandate from 1922. [britannica-german-east-africa, wiki-tanganyika-territory]

Figures:

- Kinjikitile Ngwale (died 1905) — Spirit medium and leader of the Maji Maji rebellion. Spirit medium whose teaching of a protective sacred water (maji) galvanised the 1905 uprising against German rule; he was captured and executed early in the revolt, which continued in his name.

## Independence, Revolution and the Union — 1954 – 1967

Nyerere and TANU led Tanganyika to independence; the Zanzibar Revolution and a union created Tanzania, which then embraced Ujamaa socialism.

- 1954: **Founding of TANU** — Julius Nyerere builds the Tanganyika African National Union, which campaigns for independence by constitutional means and comes to dominate the territory's politics. [britannica-nyerere, wiki-tanganyika-1961]
- 9 December 1961: **Tanganyika gains independence** — Mainland Tanganyika becomes independent from Britain under Nyerere and TANU; it becomes a republic with Nyerere as president on 9 December 1962. [britannica-nyerere, wiki-tanganyika-1961]
- 12 January 1964: **Zanzibar Revolution** — The African majority overthrows the Arab-led sultanate of Zanzibar in a bloody uprising, establishing a republic under Abeid Karume. [blackpast-zanzibar-revolution, britannica-tanzania-independence]
- 26 April 1964: **Union of Tanganyika and Zanzibar** — Tanganyika and Zanzibar unite to form the United Republic of Tanzania, with Nyerere as president and Karume as vice-president; Zanzibar keeps its own government. [wiki-articles-of-union, britannica-tanzania-independence]
- 5 February 1967: **The Arusha Declaration** — Nyerere proclaims Ujamaa, committing Tanzania to socialism and self-reliance, nationalising key sectors and launching the policy of collective villages. [britannica-arusha-declaration, wiki-arusha-declaration, blackpast-arusha-declaration]

Figures:

- Julius Nyerere (1922–1999) — First president of Tanzania; founder of TANU. Known as Mwalimu ('the teacher'), he led Tanganyika to independence in 1961, forged the union with Zanzibar in 1964, and shaped the nation through his philosophy of Ujamaa socialism set out in the 1967 Arusha Declaration.
- Abeid Amani Karume (1905–1972) — Revolutionary leader; first vice-president of Tanzania. Led the Zanzibar Revolution of January 1964, became president of Zanzibar, and joined Nyerere in the union of April 1964, serving as the first vice-president of Tanzania.

## Modern Tanzania — 1985 – present

After the Ujamaa years Tanzania moved peacefully to multiparty democracy and steady growth, becoming one of East Africa's most stable nations.

- 1985: **Nyerere steps down** — Julius Nyerere voluntarily retires from the presidency, handing power to Ali Hassan Mwinyi in a peaceful transition and opening the way to economic liberalisation. [britannica-nyerere, wiki-president-tanzania]
- 1992: **Return to multiparty politics** — Tanzania amends its constitution to permit multiple parties, ending decades of one-party rule; the first multiparty elections follow in 1995. [wiki-history-tanzania, wiki-president-tanzania]
- 1996: **Dodoma confirmed as capital** — Tanzania advances the long-planned move of its capital to the central city of Dodoma, while Dar es Salaam remains the largest city and commercial centre. [wiki-history-tanzania]
- March 2021: **Samia Suluhu Hassan becomes president** — On the death of President John Magufuli, Vice-President Samia Suluhu Hassan is sworn in as the first female president of the United Republic of Tanzania. [wiki-samia-suluhu, wiki-president-tanzania]
