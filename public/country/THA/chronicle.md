Founding: Foundation of the Chakri dynasty and Bangkok — 1782. Although Thai national tradition reaches back to the founding of Sukhothai in 1238, the direct ancestor of the present state is the kingdom established in 1782, when the general Chao Phraya Chakri took the throne as Rama I after the fall of Taksin and moved the capital across the Chao Phraya River to Bangkok. His Chakri dynasty has reigned ever since, making 1782 the birth year of the Rattanakosin (Bangkok) kingdom that became modern Thailand. The realm that Rama I rebuilt — Buddhist in faith, monarchical in form, and centred on Bangkok — endured the imperial century intact, reorganised itself as a constitutional monarchy in 1932, and adopted the name Thailand, 'land of the free,' in 1939.

Thailand is the modern heir of a succession of Tai kingdoms that arose on the Chao Phraya plain amid older Mon and Khmer civilisations. The conventional national story begins with Sukhothai in 1238 and runs through the long, cosmopolitan reign of Ayutthaya (1351–1767), the dynasty's destruction by Burmese armies, the swift reunification under Taksin, and the foundation of the Chakri dynasty and Bangkok in 1782. Alone in Southeast Asia, the kingdom — known to the world as Siam — preserved its independence through the age of European empire, surviving as a buffer between British Burma and French Indochina while kings Mongkut and Chulalongkorn modernised the state from within. A bloodless revolution in 1932 turned the absolute monarchy into a constitutional one, the country was renamed Thailand in 1939, and the decades since have followed a turbulent cycle of elected governments and military coups beneath a deeply revered crown.

## Tai Origins and the Sukhothai Kingdom — c. 1000 – 1438

Tai-speaking peoples settling among older Mon and Khmer civilisations founded Sukhothai in 1238, the kingdom remembered as the cradle of the Thai nation, language, and Theravada Buddhist culture.

- 1238: **Founding of the Sukhothai kingdom** — Tai chiefs cast off Khmer rule and establish Sukhothai in the upper Chao Phraya valley, traditionally counted as the first independent Thai kingdom. [eb-sukhothai, unesco-sukhothai]
- 1283: **Creation of the Thai script** — King Ramkhamhaeng is traditionally credited with devising the Thai alphabet, recorded on a stone inscription dated 1292, the earliest known text in the Thai language. [eb-ramkhamhaeng]
- c. 1279–1298: **Reign of Ramkhamhaeng the Great** — Sukhothai reaches its zenith under Ramkhamhaeng, who expands its reach, opens relations with China, and champions Theravada Buddhism. [eb-ramkhamhaeng, eb-sukhothai]
- by 1438: **Sukhothai absorbed by Ayutthaya** — After a century of decline, Sukhothai is reduced to a vassal and finally incorporated into the expanding kingdom of Ayutthaya. [eb-sukhothai, eb-thailand]

Figures:

- Ramkhamhaeng (c. 1239–1298) — Third king of Sukhothai. Remembered as Ramkhamhaeng the Great, he made the small kingdom of Sukhothai the first major Tai power in Southeast Asia, is credited with devising the Thai script in 1283, and patronised the Theravada Buddhism that became central to Thai identity.

## The Kingdom of Ayutthaya — 1351 – 1767

For more than four centuries Ayutthaya was one of Asia's great cosmopolitan capitals, a wealthy hub of commerce and diplomacy whose splendour ended in catastrophe at the hands of a Burmese army in 1767.

- 1351: **Foundation of Ayutthaya** — King Ramathibodi I founds Ayutthaya at the meeting of three rivers, beginning a kingdom that would dominate the Chao Phraya basin for over four centuries. [eb-thailand, unesco-ayutthaya]
- 1569: **First Burmese conquest of Ayutthaya** — Burmese forces capture Ayutthaya and reduce it to vassalage; the kingdom later recovers its full independence under King Naresuan. [eb-thailand]
- 1656–1688: **Reign of King Narai and the French embassies** — Under Narai, Ayutthaya reaches a height of international contact, exchanging embassies with Louis XIV's France and welcoming traders from across Eurasia. [eb-thailand, unesco-ayutthaya]
- 1767: **Sack of Ayutthaya by Burma** — A Burmese army storms and burns Ayutthaya after a prolonged siege, ending the kingdom and leaving the celebrated city in ruins. [eb-thailand, eb-taksin, unesco-ayutthaya]

## Thonburi, Taksin, and the Founding of the Chakri Dynasty — 1767 – 1851

From the ashes of Ayutthaya the general Taksin reunited the shattered kingdom in a few years, and in 1782 his successor founded the Chakri dynasty and the new capital of Bangkok.

- 1767: **Taksin establishes Thonburi** — Taksin rallies resistance after the fall of Ayutthaya, drives out the Burmese, and founds a new capital at Thonburi, where he is crowned king. [eb-taksin]
- 1782: **Founding of the Chakri dynasty and Bangkok** — After Taksin's fall, the general Chao Phraya Chakri takes the throne as Rama I, founds the Chakri dynasty, and establishes the new capital of Bangkok. [eb-rama-i, eb-thailand]
- 1782–1809: **Reign of Rama I** — Rama I rebuilds the Siamese state, revising its laws and Buddhist scriptures and restoring its power over neighbouring vassal states. [eb-rama-i]

Figures:

- Taksin (1734–1782) — King and reunifier of Siam. A general who escaped the doomed siege of Ayutthaya, Taksin reunited the kingdom within a few years, founded the capital at Thonburi, and ruled as king from 1767 until his deposition in 1782.
- Rama I (Chao Phraya Chakri) (1737–1809) — Founder of the Chakri dynasty. Taksin's foremost general, he took the throne in 1782, founded the still-reigning Chakri dynasty, and established Bangkok as the capital, rebuilding the institutions of the Siamese monarchy.

## Siam's Survival: Mongkut, Chulalongkorn, and the Imperial Century — 1851 – 1910

As European empires swallowed its neighbours, Siam alone preserved its independence — opening to the West under King Mongkut and modernising itself from within under his son Chulalongkorn.

- 1855: **The Bowring Treaty** — King Mongkut signs the Bowring Treaty with Britain, opening Siam to Western trade and granting extraterritoriality; comparable treaties with other powers follow. [eb-bowring, eb-mongkut]
- 1868–1910: **Reforms of Chulalongkorn (Rama V)** — Chulalongkorn abolishes slavery, reorganises the administration, and modernises law, finance, education, and infrastructure, building a centralised modern state. [eb-chulalongkorn, eb-thailand]
- 1893: **Franco-Siamese crisis and loss of Laos** — After French gunboats threaten Bangkok, Siam cedes the Lao territories east of the Mekong to French Indochina, the heaviest of its frontier concessions. [eb-thailand]
- 15 January 1896: **Anglo-French guarantee of Siam's independence** — Britain and France declare their mutual respect for the independence of Siam's core territory, fixing the kingdom as a buffer state between their empires. [siam-society-1896, eb-thailand]

Figures:

- Mongkut (Rama IV) (1804–1868) — King of Siam. A scholar-monk for nearly three decades before becoming king in 1851, Mongkut opened Siam to Western influence through the Bowring Treaty of 1855 and began the reforms that would help the kingdom preserve its independence.
- Chulalongkorn (Rama V) (1853–1910) — King of Siam and reformer. In a reign often regarded as the greatest in Thai history, he abolished slavery, modernised the administration, law, and economy, and navigated the rivalry of Britain and France to keep Siam the only uncolonised Southeast Asian state.

## Constitutional Revolution, the Name Thailand, and the World Wars — 1910 – 1957

A bloodless revolution in 1932 ended the absolute monarchy; in 1939 Siam took the name Thailand, and through the Second World War and after, the country lurched between civilian reformers and military strongmen.

- 24 June 1932: **Constitutional revolution** — The People's Party stages a bloodless coup forcing King Prajadhipok to accept a constitution, ending the absolute monarchy and creating a constitutional one. [eb-pridi, eb-thailand]
- 1939: **Siam renamed Thailand** — The nationalist government of Phibun changes the country's name from Siam to Thailand, asserting a Tai national identity. [eb-thailand]
- 1942: **Alliance with Japan in World War II** — Phibun's government allies Thailand with Japan and declares war on Britain and the United States, while the Free Thai movement under Pridi resists covertly. [eb-thailand, eb-pridi]
- 1946: **Death of King Ananda and accession of Bhumibol** — King Ananda Mahidol (Rama VIII) dies of a gunshot in unexplained circumstances and is succeeded by his brother Bhumibol Adulyadej (Rama IX). [eb-bhumibol, eb-thailand]

Figures:

- Pridi Phanomyong (1900–1983) — Statesman and revolutionary. A French-trained lawyer and leading civilian of the 1932 revolution, he drafted Thailand's early constitutions, led the Free Thai resistance in the Second World War, and briefly served as prime minister in 1946 before being driven into exile.

## Cold War Ally, Coups, and the Modern Kingdom — 1957 – present

A close Cold War ally of the United States, modern Thailand has cycled between elected governments and military coups beneath a revered monarchy, even as it grew into a major Southeast Asian economy.

- 14 October 1973: **Student uprising ends military rule** — A mass student-led uprising, with royal intervention by King Bhumibol, forces the ruling generals from power and briefly opens a democratic interlude. [eb-bhumibol, eb-thailand]
- 19 September 2006: **Coup against Thaksin Shinawatra** — The military ousts populist Prime Minister Thaksin Shinawatra while he is abroad, opening nearly two decades of conflict between his movement and the establishment. [eb-bhumibol, eb-thailand]
- May 2014: **Coup against Yingluck Shinawatra** — The army overthrows the government of Yingluck Shinawatra, ruling directly and later entrenching its role in a new constitution adopted in 2017. [eb-vajiralongkorn, eb-thailand]
- 13 October 2016: **Death of Bhumibol; accession of Vajiralongkorn** — King Bhumibol Adulyadej dies after a 70-year reign and is succeeded by his son Maha Vajiralongkorn (Rama X), who formally accepts the throne in December 2016. [eb-bhumibol, eb-vajiralongkorn]

Figures:

- Bhumibol Adulyadej (Rama IX) (1927–2016) — King of Thailand. Thailand's longest-reigning monarch, on the throne from 1946 to 2016, he became the revered symbol of national unity and used his moral authority to intervene decisively at moments of crisis such as the 1973 uprising.
- Vajiralongkorn (Rama X) (born 1952) — King of Thailand. Son and successor of Bhumibol, he became king in 2016 and gave royal assent to the military-backed 2017 constitution, reigning over a kingdom still contending with recurring political upheaval.
