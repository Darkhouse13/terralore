# The Chronicle of Afghanistan: Crossroads of empires, from Bactria and the Bamiyan Buddhas to the Durrani state and the wars of the modern age.

Founding: Ahmad Shah Durrani founds the Durrani Empire — 1747. After the assassination of the Persian conqueror Nadir Shah in 1747, a council (jirga) of Afghan chiefs at Kandahar elected the young Abdali commander Ahmad Shah as their ruler. Crowned near Kandahar, where he struck coins in his own name and established his capital, he took the title Durr-i-Durran ('pearl of pearls'), giving his clan and empire the name Durrani. The realm he built stretched from the Amu Darya to the Indian Ocean and from Khorasan into Kashmir, the Punjab and Sindh, and is widely regarded as the origin of the modern Afghan state, with Ahmad Shah remembered as the founder of the nation.

Ringed by the Hindu Kush at the meeting point of Persia, Central Asia and India, Afghanistan has for millennia been a crossroads of armies, faiths and trade. Its soil bore the Greco-Bactrian kingdoms, the Buddhist civilisation that carved the colossal Bamiyan Buddhas, the Islamic conquest and the brilliant Ghaznavid, Ghurid and Timurid courts. The modern state was founded in 1747 when Ahmad Shah Durrani was elected ruler at Kandahar, after which the country became the buffer and battlefield of the nineteenth-century 'Great Game' between Britain and Russia, winning full control of its foreign affairs in 1919. Monarchy gave way in 1973 to a republic, then to a communist coup, the Soviet war, civil war and the Taliban, whose movement fell to a US-led intervention in 2001 and returned to power in August 2021.

## Bactria, Gandhara and the Buddhist Age — c. 600 BCE – 650 CE

At the meeting of Persia, India and the steppe, ancient Afghanistan gave rise to Greco-Bactrian kingdoms, the Kushan empire and a Buddhist civilisation that carved the colossal Bamiyan Buddhas.

- 329–327 BCE: **Alexander the Great conquers Bactria** — Alexander of Macedon overruns Bactria and the lands of modern Afghanistan in a gruelling campaign, leaving Greek garrisons and settlers that seeded later Hellenistic kingdoms. [worldhistory-bactria, britannica-afghanistan]
- c. 256 BCE: **The Greco-Bactrian kingdom** — Diodotus I breaks Bactria away from the Seleucid empire to found the independent Greco-Bactrian kingdom, a Hellenistic state that fused Greek and local traditions north of the Hindu Kush. [worldhistory-bactria, ebsco-grecobactria]
- 2nd century CE: **The Kushan empire and Gandhara art** — Under rulers such as Kanishka the Kushans build an empire across Bactria and Gandhara, spreading Buddhism along the Silk Road and giving rise to the Greco-Buddhist Gandhara school of sculpture. [britannica-kushan, idp-kushan]
- 6th century CE: **The Bamiyan Buddhas carved** — Monks carve two colossal Buddhas, about 55 and 38 metres high, into the cliffs of the Bamiyan Valley, a great centre of Buddhist pilgrimage on the routes through the Hindu Kush. [unesco-bamiyan, britannica-bamiyan]

Figures:

- Kanishka (reigned c. 127–150 CE) — Kushan emperor. The greatest Kushan ruler, under whom the empire spanning Bactria, Gandhara and northern India reached its height. A patron of Buddhism, he helped spread the faith along the Silk Road and presided over the flowering of Gandhara art.

## Islam, the Ghaznavids, Ghurids and Timurids — 642 – 1747 CE

The Arab conquest brought Islam, and from Ghazni, Ghur and Herat rose empires whose courts made Afghanistan a heartland of Islamic art, learning and conquest.

- c. 642 CE: **Arab conquest and the coming of Islam** — Arab Muslim forces take Herat in the wake of the conquest of Persia, beginning the gradual and long-contested Islamisation of the Afghan lands. [britannica-muslimdynasties, loc-afghanistan]
- 998–1030 CE: **Mahmud of Ghazni and the Ghaznavid empire** — Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni builds a vast empire from Afghanistan into Iran and India and turns his capital Ghazni into a centre of Islamic culture and learning. [britannica-ghaznavid, britannica-mahmud]
- c. 1150 CE: **The Ghurids sack Ghazni** — The Ghurids of central Afghanistan overthrow Ghaznavid power, sacking Ghazni and extending Muslim rule into northern India; their Minaret of Jam survives as a UNESCO World Heritage site. [britannica-ghaznavid, britannica-ghazni]
- 15th century CE: **Herat, capital of the Timurid renaissance** — Shah Rukh moves the Timurid capital to Herat, where he and Queen Gawhar Shad make the city a dazzling centre of architecture, poetry and miniature painting. [britannica-herat, loc-afghanistan]

Figures:

- Mahmud of Ghazni (971–1030) — Sultan of the Ghaznavid empire. The most powerful Ghaznavid ruler, who from his Afghan capital Ghazni built an empire reaching into Iran and India and made his court a celebrated centre of Persian and Islamic culture.
- Gawhar Shad (c. 1378–1457) — Timurid queen and patron of the arts. Wife of Shah Rukh and a great patron of architecture and learning, she helped make fifteenth-century Herat a jewel of the Islamic world, endowing mosques, madrasas and her own celebrated mausoleum.

## The Durrani Empire and the Great Game — 1747 – 1919

Ahmad Shah Durrani founded the modern Afghan state in 1747, which the nineteenth century made the buffer and battlefield of imperial rivalry between Britain and Russia.

- 1747: **Ahmad Shah Durrani founds the Afghan state** — A jirga at Kandahar elects Ahmad Shah Durrani ruler after Nadir Shah's death; the Durrani Empire he founds is regarded as the origin of the modern Afghan state. [britannica-durrani, loc-afghanistan]
- 1839–1842: **First Anglo-Afghan War** — Britain invades to install a friendly ruler but is overwhelmed; a British-Indian force is destroyed during its disastrous 1842 retreat from Kabul, an early clash of the Great Game. [britannica-angloafghanwars]
- 1878–1880: **Second Anglo-Afghan War and the Treaty of Gandamak** — Britain invades again over fears of Russian influence; the 1879 Treaty of Gandamak gives Britain control of Afghan foreign affairs in return for subsidy and protection. [britannica-angloafghanwars]
- 1893: **The Durand Line drawn** — Amir Abdur Rahman Khan agrees the Durand Line with British India, fixing a frontier that divides Pashtun communities and remains a source of dispute with Pakistan to this day. [loc-afghanistan, britannica-afghanistan]

Figures:

- Ahmad Shah Durrani (c. 1722–1772) — Founder of the Durrani Empire and modern Afghanistan. Elected ruler at Kandahar in 1747, he built an empire stretching from Central Asia to the Indian Ocean and is revered as the founder of the Afghan nation, the 'father of Afghanistan'.
- Abdur Rahman Khan (reigned 1880–1901) — Amir of Afghanistan, the 'Iron Amir'. A forceful centralising ruler who consolidated the Afghan state after the Second Anglo-Afghan War and agreed the borders, including the contested Durand Line, that largely define the country today.

## Independence, Monarchy and the Republic — 1919 – 1978

Amanullah Khan won full independence in 1919 and tried to modernise; after his fall came four decades of cautious monarchy, a 1964 constitution, and the 1973 republic.

- 1919: **Independence and the Treaty of Rawalpindi** — Amir Amanullah Khan repudiates British control of foreign affairs and, after the Third Anglo-Afghan War, secures the Treaty of Rawalpindi (8 August 1919) recognising Afghan control of its own foreign relations. [britannica-angloafghanwars, nam-thirdafghanwar]
- 1929: **Amanullah's reforms collapse** — Sweeping modernising reforms provoke tribal and religious revolt; Amanullah Khan abdicates and flees into exile in January 1929, plunging the country briefly into civil strife. [britannica-amanullah, loc-afghanistan]
- 1933–1973: **The long reign of Zahir Shah** — Mohammad Zahir Shah rules for four decades of cautious modernisation and neutrality, introducing in 1964 a constitution that opens a brief era of parliamentary politics. [britannica-zahirshah, loc-afghanistan]
- 1973: **Daoud Khan founds the republic** — Mohammad Daoud Khan overthrows his cousin Zahir Shah in a bloodless coup, abolishes the monarchy and proclaims Afghanistan a republic with himself as president. [loc-afghanistan, britannica-afghanistan]

Figures:

- Amanullah Khan (1892–1960) — Amir and later King of Afghanistan. The reformer who won full independence from British control of Afghan foreign affairs in 1919 and tried to modernise the country, only to be driven into exile in 1929 when his reforms outran tradition.
- Mohammad Zahir Shah (1914–2007) — Last King of Afghanistan. Afghanistan's last monarch, who reigned from 1933 to 1973. He pursued gradual modernisation and neutrality and granted the liberal 1964 constitution before being deposed by his cousin Daoud Khan.

## Revolution, the Soviet War and Civil War — 1978 – 1996

A 1978 communist coup triggered a decade-long Soviet war and, after the Red Army's withdrawal, a destructive civil war among the mujahideen factions.

- April 1978: **The Saur Revolution** — The communist PDPA overthrows and kills President Daoud Khan, seizing power in the Saur Revolution and imposing radical reforms that ignite armed rebellion across the country. [statedept-sovietinvasion, loc-afghanistan]
- 24 Dec 1979: **The Soviet invasion** — Soviet forces invade on 24 December 1979, kill the Afghan leader Hafizullah Amin and install Babrak Karmal, beginning a decade-long war against the US-backed mujahideen. [statedept-sovietinvasion, britannica-sovietinvasion]
- 15 Feb 1989: **Soviet withdrawal** — Under the 1988 Geneva Accords the Soviet Union pulls out; the last troops leave on 15 February 1989 after a war that killed huge numbers of Afghans and displaced millions. [britannica-sovietinvasion, statedept-sovietinvasion]
- 1992–1996: **Collapse of the communist government and civil war** — Najibullah's government falls in 1992 and rival mujahideen factions turn on one another, devastating Kabul in a civil war that opens the way for the Taliban. [loc-afghanistan, britannica-mullahomar]

Figures:

- Mohammad Najibullah (1947–1996) — Communist President of Afghanistan. The last leader of the Soviet-backed government, he clung to power for three years after the Soviet withdrawal before his regime collapsed in 1992, ushering in the mujahideen civil war.

## The Taliban, Intervention and Return — 1996 – present

The Taliban took Kabul in 1996, were toppled by a US-led intervention after the 11 September 2001 attacks, and returned to power as foreign forces withdrew in August 2021.

- 1996: **The Taliban capture Kabul** — The Taliban, led by Mullah Omar, seize Kabul and proclaim the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan, imposing a severe religious order and isolating the country internationally. [britannica-mullahomar, statedept-humanrights1996]
- March 2001: **Destruction of the Bamiyan Buddhas** — The Taliban dynamite the colossal sixth-century Buddhas of Bamiyan as idolatrous, destroying a unique monument of world heritage months before their own fall. [unesco-bamiyan, britannica-bamiyan]
- Oct–Dec 2001: **US-led intervention topples the Taliban** — After the 11 September attacks a US-led coalition and the Northern Alliance overthrow the Taliban; the Bonn Agreement of December 2001 installs Hamid Karzai's interim government. [statedept-usafghanrelations, britannica-mullahomar]
- 15 Aug 2021: **Taliban return to power** — Following the 2020 Doha agreement and the withdrawal of foreign forces, the Afghan government collapses and the Taliban retake Kabul on 15 August 2021, re-establishing the Islamic Emirate. [statedept-dohaagreement, statedept-usafghanrelations]

Figures:

- Mullah Mohammad Omar (c. 1960–2013) — Founder and leader of the Taliban. The reclusive founder of the Taliban, recognised in 1996 as 'commander of the faithful'. His refusal to surrender Osama bin Laden after the 11 September attacks precipitated the US-led intervention that toppled his government in 2001.
