Founding: The Anglo-Irish Treaty and the Irish Free State — 1921–1922. The modern Irish state traces its origin to the Anglo-Irish Treaty signed in London on 6 December 1921, which ended the War of Independence and established the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion within the British Empire. The Treaty also confirmed the partition of the island: six counties in the north-east remained within the United Kingdom as Northern Ireland. The settlement split the independence movement and led to a civil war in 1922-1923 between pro- and anti-Treaty forces. The Free State adopted a new constitution in 1937 renaming the state Éire/Ireland, and the Republic of Ireland Act, which came into force on 18 April 1949, formally declared the twenty-six-county state a republic and removed the last role of the British Crown. The aspiration to a united Ireland endured until it was reconciled with the principle of consent in the 1998 Good Friday Agreement.

Ireland's history runs from the Celtic Gaelic kingdoms and the coming of Christianity to a long entanglement with its larger neighbour and, finally, the winning of independence. Christianised in the fifth century and traditionally associated with St Patrick, the island became a celebrated centre of monastic learning while much of Europe was in turmoil. Viking raiders founded its first towns, including Dublin, before the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169 began centuries of English involvement that hardened into conquest, the plantations, Cromwellian land confiscation and the anti-Catholic Penal Laws. The Act of Union of 1801 bound Ireland to Great Britain just as the catastrophe of the Great Famine of 1845-1852 killed about a million people and drove a million more abroad. A nationalist revival, the 1916 Easter Rising and the War of Independence produced the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, which created the Irish Free State but partitioned the island, leaving Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom. The state declared itself a republic in 1949, joined the European Economic Community in 1973, and after the boom years of the Celtic Tiger and the 1998 Good Friday Agreement settled its long quarrel over the North by the principle of consent.

## Gaelic Ireland and the Monastic Golden Age — c. 400 – 795

A land of Celtic Gaelic kingdoms embraced Christianity and became one of early medieval Europe's brightest centres of learning and missionary zeal.

- 431: **Palladius sent to the Irish** — Pope Celestine I commissioned the bishop Palladius as the first bishop 'to the Irish believing in Christ', marking the formal arrival of organised Christianity on the island. [ebsco-christianity, wha-patrick]
- 5th century: **The mission of St Patrick** — The British-born missionary Patrick, traditionally active from the 430s, evangelised much of Ireland, secured toleration for Christians and fostered a native clergy and monasticism; his feast is 17 March. [wha-patrick, ebsco-christianity]
- 563: **Columba founds Iona** — The Irish monk Columba established the monastery of Iona off western Scotland, a launching point for the spread of Christianity and Irish learning into Scotland and Northumbria. [ebsco-christianity]
- 6th–8th c.: **The monastic golden age** — Monasteries such as Clonard, Clonmacnoise and Bangor became renowned centres of learning whose scribes copied Western literature and produced illuminated manuscripts, while Irish missionaries evangelised Europe. [ebsco-christianity]

Figures:

- St Patrick (5th century) — Missionary bishop, patron saint of Ireland. Carried to Ireland as a captive in his youth and later returning as a missionary, Patrick became the figure most associated with the conversion of the Irish. He fostered a native clergy and monasticism; his feast on 17 March is celebrated worldwide.
- Columba (Colum Cille) (c. 521 – 597) — Monk and missionary. An Irish noble turned monk, Columba founded the monastery of Iona in 563, which became a beacon of Christianity and learning for Scotland and northern England and a symbol of Ireland's missionary golden age.

## Vikings, Brian Boru and the Battle of Clontarf — 795 – 1169

Norse raiders turned settlers founded Ireland's first towns, including Dublin, before a Munster king united the island and broke their power at Clontarf.

- 795: **First Viking raids on Ireland** — Scandinavian raiders struck Rathlin Island in 795, beginning decades of assaults on the rich coastal monasteries recorded with alarm in the Irish annals. [wha-vikings, thecollector-vikings]
- 841: **Founding of Dublin as a longphort** — The Vikings built a fortified ship-camp (longphort) at Dublin in 841 and overwintered there, founding what would become Ireland's most important town and a hub of Norse trade. [wha-vikings, thecollector-vikings]
- c. 1002: **Brian Boru becomes High King** — Brian Boru of the Dál Cais of Munster made himself High King of Ireland around 1002, the most powerful ruler the island had known and a claimant to authority over all of Ireland. [wha-vikings]
- 23 Apr 1014: **Battle of Clontarf** — On Good Friday 1014 Brian Boru's army defeated the Norse of Dublin and the men of Leinster near Dublin, breaking Viking political power in Ireland; Brian was killed in the battle. [wha-vikings, thecollector-vikings]

Figures:

- Brian Boru (c. 941 – 1014) — High King of Ireland. King of the Dál Cais of Munster, Brian rose to become High King of Ireland around 1002. His victory over the Dublin Norse and Leinster at Clontarf in 1014, in which he died, made him a lasting symbol of Irish kingship.
- Sitric Silkenbeard (c. 970 – 1042) — Norse king of Dublin. The long-reigning Hiberno-Norse king of Dublin, Sitric allied with Leinster against Brian Boru and helped raise the coalition defeated at Clontarf in 1014, after which Dublin's independent power waned.

## Anglo-Norman Invasion, Plantation and Conquest — 1169 – 1691

A Leinster king's appeal for help drew in the Anglo-Normans, beginning centuries of English involvement that culminated in conquest, plantation and the rise of a Protestant ruling class.

- 1169: **Anglo-Norman landing in Ireland** — At the invitation of the exiled Leinster king Diarmait Mac Murchada, Anglo-Norman, Welsh and Flemish forces landed in Wexford in 1169, beginning centuries of English involvement in Ireland. [ebsco-normans, nwe-normans]
- 1171: **Henry II and the Lordship of Ireland** — Henry II landed at Waterford in 1171, the first English king in Ireland, and established the Lordship of Ireland; in 1177 he made his son John 'Lord of Ireland'. [nwe-normans, ebsco-normans]
- 1607–1609: **Flight of the Earls and the Plantation of Ulster** — After the Gaelic earls fled Ireland in 1607, King James I planted English and Scottish Protestant settlers across confiscated lands in Ulster from 1609, embedding a lasting religious and political division. [ebsco-christianity]
- 1649: **Cromwellian conquest** — Oliver Cromwell's parliamentary army stormed Drogheda and Wexford with notorious massacres; the conquest was followed by the 1652 Act for the Settlement of Ireland and the mass confiscation of Catholic land. [downsurvey-cromwell]
- 1690–1691: **Williamite victory and the Penal era** — The Protestant William III defeated the Catholic James II at the Boyne in 1690 and Limerick in 1691, after which the Protestant Ascendancy entrenched its rule through the anti-Catholic Penal Laws. [burns-penal]

Figures:

- Strongbow (Richard de Clare) (c. 1130 – 1176) — Anglo-Norman Earl of Pembroke. Leader of the Anglo-Norman expedition of 1170, Strongbow seized Dublin and Waterford and married Aoife, daughter of Diarmait Mac Murchada, becoming heir to Leinster and the symbol of the Norman arrival in Ireland.
- Oliver Cromwell (1599 – 1658) — Lord Protector, conqueror of Ireland. Commander of the parliamentary army that subdued Ireland from 1649, Cromwell became infamous for the massacres at Drogheda and Wexford and for the land confiscations that followed, making his name a byword for conquest in Irish memory.

## Union, Famine and the National Movement — 1691 – 1914

Bound to Britain by the Act of Union, devastated by the Great Famine and stirred by campaigns for the land and for Home Rule, Ireland forged a modern national movement.

- 1 Jan 1801: **Act of Union** — The Act of Union merged the Kingdom of Ireland with Great Britain into the United Kingdom on 1 January 1801, abolishing the Irish Parliament and placing Ireland under direct rule from Westminster. [udel-parnell, gpo-timeline]
- 1845–1852: **The Great Famine (An Gorta Mór)** — Potato blight destroyed Ireland's staple crop from 1845; about a million people died and a million more emigrated, and the population fell sharply even as food continued to be exported amid an inadequate British relief effort. [history-famine, irishmemorial-famine]
- 1879: **The Land League and the Land War** — The Irish National Land League, founded in 1879 with Charles Stewart Parnell as president, mobilised tenant farmers against landlordism, opening the 'Land War' over rents and ownership. [udel-parnell]
- 1886 & 1893: **The Home Rule bills** — Under Parnell's pressure the Liberal government introduced Home Rule bills in 1886 and 1893 to give Ireland self-government within the UK; both were defeated, but Home Rule became the central Irish demand. [udel-parnell]

Figures:

- Charles Stewart Parnell (1846 – 1891) — Leader of the Irish Parliamentary Party. An Anglo-Irish Protestant landowner, Parnell led the Land League and built the Irish Parliamentary Party into a disciplined force that made Home Rule the central question of British politics, until a divorce scandal split his party shortly before his death.
- Daniel O'Connell (1775 – 1847) — 'The Liberator', campaigner for emancipation. Through mass popular agitation O'Connell won Catholic Emancipation in 1829, opening Parliament and public office to Catholics, and later led the campaign to repeal the Act of Union, becoming the model of peaceful mass politics in Ireland.

## Rising, Independence and Partition — 1914 – 1949

The 1916 Easter Rising and the War of Independence won a treaty that created the Irish Free State, but also partitioned the island and split the nation in civil war.

- 24 Apr 1916: **The Easter Rising** — Republicans seized the General Post Office and other Dublin sites and proclaimed an Irish Republic; the rising was suppressed within a week, but the execution of its leaders turned opinion towards independence. [gpo-timeline]
- 1919–1921: **War of Independence** — After Sinn Féin's 1918 victory and the first Dáil in 1919, the IRA waged a guerrilla war against British forces until a truce on 11 July 1921 opened the way to negotiations. [gpo-timeline]
- 6 Dec 1921: **Anglo-Irish Treaty** — The Treaty signed in London created the Irish Free State as a dominion within the British Empire and confirmed the partition of Ireland, leaving six northern counties in the United Kingdom. [gpo-timeline]
- 1922–1923: **Civil War over the Treaty** — The Treaty split the movement: pro-Treaty forces under Michael Collins fought anti-Treaty republicans under Éamon de Valera in a civil war that the Free State won at heavy cost. [gpo-timeline]
- 18 Apr 1949: **Declaration of the Republic** — The Republic of Ireland Act, signed 21 December 1948 and in force from 18 April 1949, declared the twenty-six-county state a republic, removed the King's last role and left the Commonwealth. [irishcentral-republic]

Figures:

- Patrick Pearse (1879 – 1916) — Leader of the Easter Rising. Schoolteacher, barrister and Irish-language activist, Pearse was commander-in-chief of the 1916 rebels and read the Proclamation of the Republic outside the GPO. His execution by firing squad made him a martyr of the independence movement.
- Éamon de Valera (1882 – 1975) — Revolutionary and statesman. A surviving commander of the 1916 Rising, de Valera led the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, founded Fianna Fáil and dominated independent Ireland for decades, framing the 1937 Constitution and serving as Taoiseach and President.

## The Republic, Europe and the Northern Question — 1949 – present

A poor agricultural republic became a prosperous European state, the bitter conflict over Northern Ireland was settled by consent, and a curious dispute over an Atlantic rock endured.

- 1955: **UK annexes Rockall** — British Royal Marines landed on the uninhabited Atlantic islet of Rockall in 1955 and claimed it for the United Kingdom; it was declared part of Scotland by the Island of Rockall Act 1972. [thejournal-rockall, irishtimes-rockall]
- 1 Jan 1973: **Accession to the European Communities** — After a 1972 referendum carried by over 80 per cent, Ireland joined the European Economic Community on 1 January 1973, beginning a transformation from an agricultural economy to a modern European state. [ec-ireland-eu, cso-eu50, rte-eec]
- c. 1995–2007: **The Celtic Tiger** — From the mid-1990s, foreign direct investment and the single market fuelled rapid growth, the 'Celtic Tiger', turning one of western Europe's poorer countries into one of its most prosperous. [ec-ireland-eu]
- 10 Apr 1998: **Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement** — The 1998 Agreement settled the conflict over Northern Ireland on the principle of consent and created power-sharing institutions; Ireland amended Articles 2 and 3 of its Constitution to replace its territorial claim with a peaceful aspiration to unity. [peaceagreements-gfa, ni-assembly-gfa]
- 1988: **UK–Ireland continental shelf agreement** — Ireland and the United Kingdom agreed a delimitation of the continental shelf between them; Ireland maintained that Rockall, an uninhabitable rock, generates no continental shelf or exclusive economic zone. [thejournal-rockall, irishtimes-rockall]

Figures:

- John Hume (1937 – 2020) — Nationalist leader and Nobel laureate. Leader of the SDLP, Hume was a principal architect of the peace process whose insistence on non-violence and dialogue helped produce the Good Friday Agreement, for which he shared the 1998 Nobel Peace Prize with David Trimble.
- Mary Robinson (born 1944) — President of Ireland. Elected in 1990 as Ireland's first woman president, Robinson came to symbolise a more open, outward-looking republic during the years of European integration and the early peace process, later serving as UN High Commissioner for Human Rights.
