# The Chronicle of Central African Republic: From the megaliths of Bouar to a fragile peace at the heart of Africa

Founding: Independence from France — 13 August 1960. The territory of Ubangi-Shari became the fully independent Central African Republic on 13 August 1960, with David Dacko — a relative and successor of the nationalist founder Barthélemy Boganda — as its first president.

Landlocked at the continent's centre, the Central African Republic carries one of Africa's deepest archaeological records in the standing stones of Bouar and one of its harshest modern histories. Slave-raiding from the Sudanic states and traders such as al-Zubayr depopulated much of the Ubangi region in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; French conquest then bound it into a brutal concessionary-company regime within French Equatorial Africa. Independent since 13 August 1960, the country endured the imperial pretensions of Jean-Bédel Bokassa, repeated coups, and a sectarian civil war after the 2013 Séléka seizure of power, leaving it dependent on the UN mission MINUSCA and foreign actors for a precarious stability.

## Megaliths and Early Peoples — c. 3500 BCE – 17th century CE

Standing stones near Bouar and centuries of migration set the deepest record of the Ubangi watershed.

- c. 3500–2700 BCE: **The megaliths of Bouar are raised** — Farmers near Bouar in the west erected multi-ton standing stones (tazunu), among the most important prehistoric monuments in Central Africa and evidence of organised Neolithic communities. [britannica-car-history, kiddle-car-history, wiki-bouar]
- c. 1st millennium BCE: **Ironworking and migration** — Ubangian, Central Sudanic and Bantu-speaking peoples settled the watershed and ironworking spread, laying down the ethnic patchwork of Gbaya, Banda, Zande, Nzakara and others. [kiddle-car-history, britannica-car-history]

## Zande States and the Slave Trade — c. 1700 – 1894

Zande and Bandia kingdoms rose along the Ubangi as slave-raiding from the Sudanic north hollowed out the savanna.

- 18th century: **Zande and Bandia kingdoms expand** — Under the Avongara nobility the Zande spread across the south-east, while the Bandia clan founded Ubangi River states including the Nzakara kingdom and the sultanate of Bangassou. [wiki-azande, wiki-bangassou-nzakara, britannica-car-history]
- 1830: **Dar al-Kuti founded under Wadai** — The sultanate of Dar al-Kuti was established as a tributary of Dar Runga and ultimately of Wadai, becoming a staging ground for slave raids into the Banda and Nzakara lands to the south. [wiki-dar-al-kuti, britannica-car-history]
- 1890: **Al-Zubayr installs al-Sanusi at Dar al-Kuti** — The Sudanese warlord Rabih az-Zubayr (al-Zubayr) deposed Kobur and installed Muhammad al-Sanusi, who from his base at Ndélé intensified the razzias that depopulated much of the Ubangi region. [wiki-dar-al-kuti, wiki-rabih, britannica-car-history]

## Conquest and the Concession Regime — 1887 – 1920

France seized the Ubangi watershed and leased it to concessionary companies whose rubber and ivory exactions cost countless lives.

- 1889: **France founds Bangui** — French forces established a post at Bangui on the Ubangi River, the base from which they extended control over the territory that became the colony of Ubangi-Shari. [kiddle-car-history, britannica-car-history]
- 1899–1927: **Concessionary companies take over** — France leased huge tracts of Ubangi-Shari to private companies such as the Company of the Upper Ubangi Sultanates, whose militias exacted rubber and ivory through brutal forced labour. [wiki-french-equatorial-africa, wiki-upper-ubangi-company, yawboadu-car-economy]
- 12 January 1911: **Death of al-Sanusi of Dar al-Kuti** — French forces killed Muhammad al-Sanusi at Ndélé, ending the slave-raiding sultanate of Dar al-Kuti; his successor's resistance was suppressed by 1912. [wiki-dar-al-kuti]

Figures:

- Muhammad al-Sanusi (d. 1911) — Sultan of Dar al-Kuti. Installed at Dar al-Kuti by the warlord al-Zubayr and later accommodating French rule, he ran a slave-raiding state across the north until French officers killed him in 1911.

## French Equatorial Africa to Independence — 1910 – 1960

A colony within French Equatorial Africa, scarred by the Kongo-Wara revolt, found its national voice in Barthélemy Boganda.

- 1928–1931: **Kongo-Wara rebellion** — The Gbaya prophet Karinou led a mass revolt against forced labour and railway recruitment; the French killed him in December 1928 and crushed the uprising by 1931, with thousands dead. [wiki-kongo-wara, britannica-car-history]
- 28 September 1949: **Boganda founds MESAN** — Barthélemy Boganda, the territory's first deputy to the French National Assembly, founded the Movement for the Social Evolution of Black Africa, the engine of Central African nationalism. [britannica-boganda, newworld-boganda, wiki-mesan]
- 29 March 1959: **Death of Boganda** — Boganda, who had proclaimed the autonomous republic in December 1958 and championed a 'United States of Latin Africa', died in a plane crash months before independence; David Dacko succeeded him. [britannica-boganda, newworld-boganda, face2face-boganda]
- 13 August 1960: **Independence from France** — The Central African Republic became fully independent, with David Dacko as its first president and Bangui as its capital. [britannica-car-independence, wiki-dacko, factbook-car]
- 1910: **Ubangi-Shari joins French Equatorial Africa** — The dependency France set up in 1894 is folded into the federation governed from Brazzaville, a structure that lasted until independence. [bbc-caf]
- 1946: **An assembly, and a seat in Paris** — The territory gains its own assembly and representation in the French parliament; Barthélemy Boganda becomes the first Central African elected to it. [bbc-caf, britannica-boganda]
- 1 Dec 1958: **Self-government under Boganda** — Ubangi-Shari achieves self-government within French Equatorial Africa with Boganda as prime minister, months before his death in an air crash. [bbc-caf, britannica-boganda]

Figures:

- Barthélemy Boganda (1910-1959) — Priest, deputy and founder of MESAN. The first Central African elected to the French parliament and the architect of the country's independence, who wanted a single state across French Equatorial Africa and died in an air crash months before he could lead one.
- David Dacko (1930-2003) — First President of the Central African Republic. Boganda's cousin and successor, who led the country to independence in 1960, made it a one-party state, was overthrown by Bokassa in 1965 and restored to office by French troops in 1979.

## Bokassa and the Age of Coups — 1965 – 2003

Jean-Bédel Bokassa crowned himself emperor and was toppled by France, opening four decades of coups and instability.

- 1 January 1966: **Bokassa seizes power** — Army commander Jean-Bédel Bokassa overthrew President David Dacko in the Saint-Sylvestre coup, beginning more than a decade of personal rule. [wiki-bokassa, britannica-car-history]
- 4 December 1977: **Coronation of Emperor Bokassa I** — Having declared the Central African Empire in 1976, Bokassa crowned himself emperor in a lavish ceremony costing about US$20 million while the country remained among the world's poorest. [wiki-bokassa, britannica-bokassa]
- April 1979: **Killing of schoolchildren** — Protests against compulsory school uniforms ended in the massacre of scores of detained children and students in Bangui, an atrocity widely linked to Bokassa that hastened the end of his rule. [wiki-bokassa, britannica-bokassa]
- 20–21 September 1979: **France overthrows Bokassa** — A French military operation (Barracuda) deposed Bokassa and restored David Dacko; Bokassa fled into exile and was later tried and convicted. [wiki-bokassa, britannica-bokassa]
- 15 March 2003: **Bozizé seizes power** — After coups by André Kolingba (1981) and an elected interlude under Ange-Félix Patassé, army chief François Bozizé overthrew Patassé, continuing the cycle of armed takeovers. [kiddle-car-history, britannica-car-history]
- 1993: **Patassé ends twelve years of military rule** — Ange-Félix Patassé wins the presidency in elections that close the era opened by Bokassa and continued by his successors. [bbc-caf]

Figures:

- Jean-Bédel Bokassa (1921-1996) — President, then self-proclaimed Emperor. Army commander who seized power in 1965, declared himself president for life in 1972 and emperor in 1976, and was removed by French troops in 1979 after schoolchildren were killed in detention.

## Séléka, Anti-balaka and a Fragile Peace — 2012 – present

The 2013 Séléka coup triggered a sectarian civil war, leaving the country reliant on MINUSCA and foreign forces for survival.

- 24 March 2013: **Séléka captures Bangui** — The Séléka rebel coalition seized the capital and ousted President Bozizé; its leader Michel Djotodia declared himself president, the country's first Muslim head of state. [wiki-car-civil-war, britannica-car-history]
- December 2013: **Sectarian civil war erupts** — Reprisals between Séléka and the anti-balaka militias spiralled into sectarian massacres; attacks in Bangui in early December 2013 killed more than a thousand civilians. [wiki-car-civil-war, britannica-car-history]
- April–September 2014: **UN establishes MINUSCA** — After French and African Union deployments, the UN Security Council authorised the MINUSCA peacekeeping mission in April 2014; it assumed responsibility in September, alongside interim president Catherine Samba-Panza. [wiki-car-civil-war, un-minusca]
- 6 February 2019: **Khartoum peace agreement** — President Touadéra's government and fourteen armed groups signed a peace accord in Khartoum, but implementation was partial and fighting soon resumed. [wiki-car-civil-war]
- 2020–2021: **CPC offensive and Wagner** — A rebel coalition (the CPC) attacked around the December 2020 elections; the government, increasingly backed by Russian Wagner Group mercenaries deployed from 2018, pushed it back with MINUSCA's help. [wiki-car-civil-war, factbook-car]
