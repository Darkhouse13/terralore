# The Chronicle of Uganda: From the great lakeside kingdoms of the Buganda kabakas to a turbulent modern republic.

Founding: Independence from the United Kingdom — 9 October 1962. Uganda became an independent state on 9 October 1962, ending sixty-eight years of British rule that had begun with the proclamation of the Uganda Protectorate in 1894. Independence was negotiated through constitutional conferences in London, which preserved a special federal status for the kingdom of Buganda and the other kingdoms. Milton Obote, leader of the Uganda People's Congress, became the country's first prime minister and head of government; a year later, in October 1963, Uganda became a republic and the kabaka of Buganda, Sir Edward Mutesa II, was elected its first (largely ceremonial) president. The date 9 October remains Uganda's Independence Day.

Uganda lies at the heart of Africa's interlacustrine region, the well-watered country between the great lakes where Bantu farmers and Nilotic herders met and built some of the continent's most enduring kingdoms — above all Buganda, ruled by its kabaka, alongside Bunyoro-Kitara, Ankole and Toro. In the nineteenth century Swahili-Arab traders, then Christian missionaries, reached the court of Buganda, and religious rivalry led to the killing of the Uganda Martyrs in 1885–87. Britain declared the Uganda Protectorate in 1894 and through the 1900 Buganda Agreement governed by indirect rule, while the Uganda Railway, built largely by Indian labour, bound the country to the coast. Uganda became independent on 9 October 1962 with Milton Obote as prime minister and the kabaka Mutesa II as its first president. Independence gave way to upheaval: Obote's abolition of the kingdoms in 1966–67, Idi Amin's brutal dictatorship of 1971–79 and the expulsion of the Asian community, the war that ousted Amin, a second Obote period and the Bush War, and Yoweri Museveni's seizure of power in 1986. The north was then ravaged for two decades by the Lord's Resistance Army.

## Peoples and the Great Lakes Kingdoms — c. 1000 BCE – c. 1700 CE

Bantu farmers and Nilotic herders settled the interlacustrine region and built the kingdoms of Bunyoro-Kitara, Buganda, Ankole and Toro.

- c. 1st millennium BCE: **Bantu farmers settle the lakes region** — Iron-using Bantu-speaking agriculturalists spread into the interlacustrine region north and west of Lake Victoria, establishing the banana-farming societies that underlie the later kingdoms. [wiki-history-uganda, britannica-uganda-bunyoro-buganda]
- c. 11th–14th century: **Rise of Bunyoro-Kitara** — The kingdom of Bunyoro-Kitara, ruled by the omukama and linked in tradition to the Bachwezi, emerges as the dominant power of the interlacustrine region. [wiki-history-buganda, britannica-uganda-bunyoro-buganda]
- c. 14th–15th century: **Foundation of Buganda** — Buganda takes shape east of Bunyoro under its first kabakas; tradition credits its founding to Kintu or, in Bunyoro accounts, to Kato Kimera of the Babito dynasty. [wiki-history-buganda, buganda-history]
- c. 17th–18th century: **Buganda expands at Bunyoro's expense** — Under a line of aggressive kabakas, the compact kingdom of Buganda annexes territory from Bunyoro-Kitara, growing into the largest state in the interlacustrine region. [britannica-uganda-bunyoro-buganda, wiki-history-buganda]

## Traders, Missionaries and the Uganda Martyrs — c. 1844 – 1890

Swahili-Arab traders brought Islam and guns to the kabaka's court, Christian missionaries followed, and religious rivalry led to the killing of the Uganda Martyrs.

- c. 1844: **Swahili-Arab traders reach Buganda** — A caravan led by Ahmed bin Ibrahim reaches the court of Kabaka Suna II, opening Buganda to the Zanzibar-based ivory and slave trade and to Islam. [monitor-arab-traders, vilhanova-islam-uganda]
- 1877: **Anglican missionaries arrive** — Agents of the Church Missionary Society reach Buganda after Stanley publicises Mutesa I's apparent interest in Christianity, beginning Protestant evangelism at the kabaka's court. [wiki-history-uganda, vilhanova-islam-uganda]
- 1879: **Catholic White Fathers arrive** — Roman Catholic missionaries of the White Fathers reach Buganda, adding a third faith to the rivalry of Muslim, Protestant and Catholic factions at court. [wiki-history-uganda, vilhanova-islam-uganda]
- 3 June 1886: **Martyrdom of Charles Lwanga** — Kabaka Mwanga II has Charles Lwanga and a group of Christian pages burned to death at Namugongo, the most notorious episode in the killing of the Uganda Martyrs of 1885–87. [britannica-martyrs-uganda, wiki-uganda-martyrs]
- 1885–1887: **Killing of the Uganda Martyrs** — Mwanga II executes some forty-five Catholic and Anglican converts who refuse to renounce their faith; twenty-two Catholic victims were later canonised as saints. [britannica-martyrs-uganda, wiki-uganda-martyrs]

Figures:

- Mutesa I — Kabaka of Buganda (r. c. 1856–1884). The powerful kabaka who welcomed Swahili-Arab traders and the first Christian missionaries, balancing Muslim, Protestant and Catholic factions at his court while strengthening Buganda with imported guns.
- Charles Lwanga (1860–1886) — Master of the royal pages; Catholic martyr. Chief of the royal pages at Mwanga II's court, burned to death at Namugongo on 3 June 1886 for his Christian faith; canonised as a saint and patron of Africa's youth.

## The Uganda Protectorate — 1894 – 1962

Britain ruled through the kabaka and chiefs under the Buganda Agreement of 1900, drove the Uganda Railway inland, and relied on Indian labour and traders.

- 1894: **Uganda Protectorate declared** — Britain proclaims a protectorate over Buganda, soon extended across the wider territory, beginning sixty-eight years of colonial rule administered through the kingdoms. [wiki-uganda-protectorate, britannica-history-uganda]
- 1896–1901: **The Uganda Railway is built** — The railway is driven from Mombasa to Kisumu on Lake Victoria by some 32,000 indentured Indian labourers, binding Uganda to the coast and bringing a lasting South Asian presence. [wiki-uganda-railway, monitor-uganda-railway]
- 10 March 1900: **The Buganda Agreement** — Sir Harry Johnston and Buganda's regents sign the agreement recognising the kabaka and Lukiiko, creating the 'mailo' land system and entrenching Buganda's privileged status under indirect rule. [wiki-buganda-agreement, britannica-history-uganda]
- 1962: **Path to independence negotiated** — Constitutional conferences in London settle a federal arrangement preserving Buganda's special status, clearing the way for Ugandan self-government and independence. [britannica-history-uganda, statedept-uganda]

Figures:

- Sir Apolo Kagwa (c. 1864–1927) — Katikkiro (chief minister) of Buganda; regent. The leading Protestant chief and regent for the young kabaka, who signed the Buganda Agreement of 1900 and made Buganda's chiefly establishment central to British indirect rule.

## Independence and the First Obote Era — 1962 – 1971

Uganda became independent under Milton Obote with the kabaka as president, but in 1966 Obote crushed Buganda and abolished the kingdoms.

- 9 October 1962: **Uganda gains independence** — Uganda becomes independent from the United Kingdom with Milton Obote as prime minister; the constitution preserves a federal status for Buganda and the other kingdoms. [britannica-history-uganda, statedept-uganda, blackpast-obote]
- October 1963: **Mutesa II becomes first president** — Uganda becomes a republic and the kabaka of Buganda, Sir Edward Mutesa II, is elected its first president, a largely ceremonial post under the 1962 constitution. [wiki-mutesa-ii, statehouse-mutesa]
- 24 May 1966: **Battle of Mengo and the fall of Buganda** — Obote suspends the constitution and orders the army under Idi Amin to storm the kabaka's palace; hundreds die and Mutesa II flees into exile, dying in London in 1969. [wiki-mengo-crisis, britannica-history-uganda]
- 1967: **Kingdoms abolished** — A new republican constitution abolishes the kingdoms of Buganda, Bunyoro, Ankole and Toro and makes Obote executive president of a centralised, unitary Uganda. [wiki-mengo-crisis, britannica-history-uganda]
- 25 January 1971: **Amin's coup** — While Obote is abroad, army commander Idi Amin overthrows the government in a military coup, beginning eight years of dictatorship. [britannica-idi-amin, origins-amin-coup]

Figures:

- Milton Obote (1925–2005) — Prime minister (1962–66); president of Uganda. Leader of the Uganda People's Congress and Uganda's first prime minister at independence, who crushed Buganda in 1966 and abolished the kingdoms, before being overthrown by Idi Amin in 1971.
- Sir Edward Mutesa II (1924–1969) — Kabaka of Buganda; first president of Uganda. The thirty-fifth kabaka of Buganda and Uganda's first president, ousted when Obote stormed his palace in 1966; he died in exile in London in 1969.

## Idi Amin and the War that Ousted Him — 1971 – 1980

Idi Amin's dictatorship brought mass killing and the expulsion of the Asian community, until a war with Tanzania drove him from power.

- August 1972: **Expulsion of the Asian community** — Amin orders the roughly 80,000 South Asians in Uganda to leave within about ninety days, seizing their property and businesses and devastating the economy. [wiki-asian-expulsion, lords-library-asians]
- 1971–1979: **Mass killings under Amin** — Amin's regime murders soldiers, professionals, clergy and dissidents through bodies such as the State Research Bureau; estimates of the dead run up to about 300,000. [britannica-idi-amin, ebsco-amin-terror]
- 1977: **Murder of Archbishop Luwum** — The Anglican archbishop Janani Luwum, who had protested against state violence, is killed; his death becomes a symbol of the regime's brutality. [britannica-idi-amin, ebsco-amin-terror]
- 1978–1979: **Uganda–Tanzania War** — After Amin invades northern Tanzania, Julius Nyerere's forces and Ugandan exiles counter-attack and capture Kampala in April 1979, driving Amin into exile. [wiki-uganda-tanzania-war, britannica-idi-amin]
- December 1980: **Obote returns to power** — After interim governments under Lule and Binaisa, disputed elections return Milton Obote and the UPC to power; opponents allege the vote was rigged. [wiki-binaisa, britannica-obote]

Figures:

- Idi Amin (c. 1925–2003) — Military dictator of Uganda (1971–1979). Army commander who seized power in 1971 and ruled by terror, expelling the Asian community in 1972 and presiding over the killing of up to 300,000 people before a war with Tanzania ousted him.

## The Bush War, Museveni and the Northern Insurgency — 1981 – present

Yoweri Museveni's guerrilla war brought him to power in 1986 and a long presidency, while the Lord's Resistance Army ravaged the north for two decades.

- 1981–1986: **The Ugandan Bush War** — Museveni's National Resistance Army wages guerrilla war against Obote's government from the Luwero Triangle; the army's counter-insurgency kills and displaces many thousands of civilians. [wiki-bush-war, wiki-nra]
- 26 January 1986: **Museveni captures Kampala** — The National Resistance Army takes Kampala and Yoweri Museveni is sworn in as president, ending the post-Amin upheaval and beginning his long rule. [ebsco-museveni-kampala, britannica-museveni]
- 1987: **Rise of the Lord's Resistance Army** — Joseph Kony forms the Lord's Resistance Army in northern Uganda, launching an insurgency notorious for atrocities and the abduction of children. [hrw-kony-lra, wiki-lra-insurgency]
- 1993: **Cultural kingdoms restored** — Museveni's government restores the kingdoms in a cultural, non-political form; a new kabaka of Buganda, Ronald Mutebi II, is enthroned. [britannica-history-uganda, buganda-history]
- 2005: **Term limits removed** — Parliament removes presidential term limits from the constitution, and Uganda returns to multiparty politics; Museveni's tenure lengthens into one of Africa's longest. [britannica-museveni, loc-uganda-agelimit]
- c. 2006: **LRA driven from Uganda** — After nearly two decades that displaced almost two million people and saw tens of thousands of children abducted, the Lord's Resistance Army is pushed out of northern Uganda. [hrw-kony-lra, wiki-lra-insurgency]

Figures:

- Yoweri Museveni (born 1944) — President of Uganda (from 1986). Guerrilla leader of the National Resistance Army who seized Kampala in 1986 and went on to rule for more than three decades, restoring stability and the economy but removing constitutional limits on his power.
- Joseph Kony (born c. 1961) — Leader of the Lord's Resistance Army. Self-proclaimed spirit medium who led the Lord's Resistance Army from 1987 in a brutal insurgency in northern Uganda, later indicted by the International Criminal Court for crimes against humanity.
