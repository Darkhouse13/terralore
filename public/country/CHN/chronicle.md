Founding: Imperial unification under the First Emperor of Qin — 221 BCE. China's conventional founding is dated to 221 BCE, when the state of Qin completed its conquest of the rival Warring States and its king assumed the title Shi Huangdi, First Sovereign Emperor, creating the first unified Chinese empire and the bureaucratic template that shaped every later dynasty. The modern state, the People's Republic of China, was proclaimed by Mao Zedong from the Tiananmen gate in Beijing on 1 October 1949.

China is one of the world's oldest continuous civilizations, with a written record reaching back to the Bronze Age Shang dynasty around 1600 BCE. From the philosophical ferment of the Zhou and the imperial unification under the First Emperor of Qin in 221 BCE, China developed an enduring tradition of centralized bureaucratic government that outlasted dozens of dynasties. Golden ages under the Tang and Song produced printing, gunpowder, paper money, and a flourishing of art and poetry, while later the Mongol Yuan, Han-Chinese Ming, and Manchu Qing dynasties governed a vast multiethnic empire. The collapse of the empire in 1911-12 gave way to a republic, warlordism, devastating war with Japan, and civil war, ending with the proclamation of the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949. After the upheavals of the Mao era, market reforms launched in 1978 transformed China into a global economic power.

## Bronze Age Kingdoms and the Hundred Schools — c. 1600-256 BCE

Diviners cracked ox bones to read the will of heaven while sages debated how a kingdom should be ruled.

- c. 1600 BCE: **Shang dynasty rules the Yellow River valley** — The Shang, the first Chinese dynasty with historical records, governs from cities such as Anyang and produces ritual bronzes and the oracle-bone script, the earliest known Chinese writing. [britannica-china, si-zhou, metmuseum-han]
- c. 1200 BCE: **Oracle-bone divination records** — Shang diviners inscribe questions to ancestors and deities on ox bones and turtle shells; these inscriptions are the oldest unambiguous Chinese writing and a primary source for early history. [britannica-china]
- c. 1046 BCE: **Zhou conquest and the Mandate of Heaven** — King Wu of Zhou defeats the Shang at the Battle of Muye and founds the Zhou dynasty, justifying the overthrow with the Mandate of Heaven, a doctrine of morally conditional rule that endures for millennia. [si-zhou, worldhistory-zhou]
- 771 BCE: **Western Zhou collapses; Eastern Zhou begins** — Nomadic invasions force the Zhou court to flee east to Luoyang in 771 BCE, beginning the Eastern Zhou and a long decline of royal authority into the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods. [si-zhou, worldhistory-zhou]
- 551 BCE: **Lifetime of Confucius** — Confucius (551-479 BCE) teaches that social order rests on moral cultivation, ritual, and proper human relationships; his thought becomes the dominant ethical tradition of imperial China. [si-zhou, britannica-china]

Figures:

- Confucius (Kongzi) (551-479 BCE) — Philosopher and teacher. An itinerant teacher of the late Spring and Autumn period whose emphasis on virtue, filial piety, ritual propriety, and benevolent government became the ethical backbone of Chinese statecraft and education for more than two thousand years.

## Imperial Unification: Qin and Han — 221 BCE-220 CE

One emperor standardized a continent's script, roads, and weights; the next four centuries gave China its lasting name and ideal of government.

- 221 BCE: **Qin unifies China; the First Emperor is proclaimed** — Qin completes its conquest of the Warring States and its king takes the title Shi Huangdi, founding the first unified Chinese empire and the centralized bureaucratic system that defines imperial China. [britannica-qin-shi-huang, britannica-china]
- c. 214 BCE: **Frontier walls joined into the Great Wall** — Under the First Emperor, earlier northern fortifications are connected into a unified defense against steppe invasions, the origin of the Great Wall that later dynasties would expand. [unesco-great-wall, britannica-qin-shi-huang]
- 210 BCE: **Death of the First Emperor and the terracotta army** — Qin Shi Huang dies and is entombed near Xi'an in a colossal mausoleum guarded by roughly 8,000 terracotta warriors, rediscovered in 1974 and now a UNESCO World Heritage site. [unesco-qin-mausoleum, britannica-qin-shi-huang]
- 206 BCE: **Han dynasty consolidates the empire** — After the Qin collapse, the Han dynasty (206 BCE-220 CE) institutionalizes Confucian-trained bureaucracy and the civil service, giving China its enduring model of imperial government and the name of its majority people. [si-han, metmuseum-han, britannica-china]
- c. 138 BCE: **Silk Road trade routes open under Emperor Wu** — Emperor Wu's campaigns against the Xiongnu and missions into Central Asia open the overland Silk Road, linking China to India, Persia, and the Mediterranean in exchange of goods and ideas. [si-han, metmuseum-han]

Figures:

- Qin Shi Huang (259-210 BCE) — First Emperor of unified China. King of Qin who conquered the Warring States and proclaimed himself First Emperor in 221 BCE. He standardized script, coinage, and measures, built road and wall networks, and ordered the terracotta army for his tomb, founding the imperial system that lasted until 1912.
- Emperor Wu of Han (Han Wudi) (reigned 141-87 BCE) — Han emperor. Expansionist emperor who made Confucianism the state ideology, professionalized the bureaucracy, drove back the Xiongnu, and opened the Silk Road, roughly doubling the territory and prestige of the Han empire.

## Division, Reunification, and the Tang-Song Golden Age — 220-1279

After centuries of fracture, a cosmopolitan empire and a commercial revolution gave the world printing, paper money, and gunpowder.

- 220 CE: **Collapse of the Han and centuries of division** — The Han empire fragments into the Three Kingdoms and successive Northern and Southern Dynasties, a long era of disunion during which Buddhism takes deep root in China. [britannica-china]
- 589 CE: **Sui dynasty reunifies China** — The Sui (581-618) reunite north and south and build the Grand Canal, restoring a centralized empire after centuries of division and setting the stage for the Tang golden age. [britannica-china]
- 618 CE: **Tang dynasty and a cosmopolitan golden age** — The Tang dynasty (618-907) builds a vast, outward-looking empire centered on Chang'an, refining the examination system and producing a flowering of poetry; woodblock printing and gunpowder first appear. [si-tang, afe-columbia]
- 960 CE: **Song dynasty and a commercial revolution** — The Song dynasty (960-1279) presides over an economic and technological boom, introducing movable-type printing, government paper money, the navigational compass, and gunpowder weapons. [afe-columbia, britannica-china]

Figures:

- Emperor Taizong of Tang (Li Shimin) (reigned 626-649) — Tang emperor. A founding ruler of the Tang whose reign, the Zhenguan era, became the model of effective and benevolent government, consolidating the dynasty's power and inaugurating its cosmopolitan golden age.

## Empires of Conquest and Restoration: Yuan, Ming, Qing — 1271-1912

Mongol and Manchu conquerors and a restored Han dynasty governed the largest empire China had ever known.

- 1271 CE: **Kublai Khan founds the Mongol Yuan dynasty** — Kublai Khan declares the Yuan dynasty in 1271 and completes the conquest of the Song by 1279, ruling China from Dadu (Beijing) as the first foreign-led dynasty to govern all of China. [britannica-yuan, worldhistory-yuan]
- 1368 CE: **Ming dynasty restores Han rule** — Zhu Yuanzhang overthrows the Yuan and founds the Ming dynasty (1368-1644), which rebuilds the Great Wall in stone, constructs the Forbidden City, and revives Han Chinese imperial governance. [britannica-china, worldhistory-yuan]
- 1405 CE: **Zheng He's maritime voyages** — The Ming admiral Zheng He leads seven vast treasure-fleet expeditions across Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and the coast of Africa, projecting Chinese power before the dynasty turns inward. [britannica-china]
- 1644 CE: **Manchus establish the Qing dynasty** — The Manchus capture Beijing and found the Qing dynasty (1644-1912), China's last imperial dynasty, which expands the empire to its greatest extent under the Kangxi and Qianlong emperors. [britannica-china, worldhistory-yuan]
- 1839 CE: **First Opium War and the 'unequal treaties'** — War with Britain over the opium trade ends in Qing defeat and the Treaty of Nanjing (1842), ceding Hong Kong and opening treaty ports, the start of a century of foreign encroachment China calls its century of humiliation. [britannica-china]

Figures:

- Kublai Khan (1215-1294) — Founder of the Yuan dynasty. Grandson of Genghis Khan who completed the Mongol conquest of China, proclaimed the Yuan dynasty in 1271, and ruled a Eurasian empire from Dadu (Beijing), the first non-Han sovereign to rule all of China.
- Zheng He (c. 1371-1433) — Ming admiral and explorer. A Muslim eunuch admiral who commanded seven enormous Ming treasure-fleet voyages across the Indian Ocean in the early fifteenth century, reaching Arabia and East Africa decades before European ocean exploration.

## The Fall of the Empire: Republic, Warlords, and War — 1912-1949

Two thousand years of monarchy ended in 1912, but a fragile republic gave way to warlords, invasion, and civil war.

- 1911 CE: **Xinhai Revolution erupts** — The Wuchang Uprising of 10 October 1911 sparks the Xinhai Revolution against the Qing, rapidly spreading across the provinces and bringing down China's last imperial dynasty. [britannica-china]
- 1912 CE: **Republic of China founded; last emperor abdicates** — Sun Yat-sen becomes provisional president of the Republic of China on 1 January 1912, and on 12 February the child emperor Puyi abdicates, ending more than two millennia of imperial rule. [britannica-china]
- 1916 CE: **Warlord Era of political fragmentation** — The death of Yuan Shikai in 1916 plunges China into the Warlord Era, with regional militarists ruling much of the country until the Nationalists reassert partial unity by 1928. [britannica-china]
- 1937 CE: **Second Sino-Japanese War and the Nanjing Massacre** — Japan's full-scale invasion from July 1937 brings catastrophic violence, including the December 1937 Nanjing Massacre, in which Japanese forces murdered many tens of thousands of civilians and prisoners and committed mass rape. [britannica-sino-japanese, britannica-china]
- 1946 CE: **Chinese Civil War resumes; Communist victory** — After Japan's surrender, full-scale civil war between Nationalists and Communists resumes, ending in 1949 with Communist victory and the retreat of Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government to Taiwan. [britannica-china]

Figures:

- Sun Yat-sen (1866-1925) — Revolutionary leader and first provisional president of the Republic. The principal architect of the 1911 revolution and founder of the Nationalist Party, revered in both mainland China and Taiwan as the father of the Chinese republic for his role in ending imperial rule.

## The People's Republic: Revolution, Upheaval, and Reform — 1949-present

From the Tiananmen proclamation through famine and turmoil to market reform, China became the world's second-largest economy.

- 1949 CE: **People's Republic of China proclaimed** — Mao Zedong proclaims the People's Republic of China from the Tiananmen gate in Beijing on 1 October 1949, with Zhou Enlai as premier, after Communist victory in the civil war. [britannica-china, ebsco-prc-founding]
- 1958 CE: **Great Leap Forward and the great famine** — Mao's Great Leap Forward forces rapid collectivization and crude industrialization, causing a famine from 1959 to 1961 that killed an estimated 15 million to tens of millions of people. [britannica-great-leap, ebsco-great-leap]
- 1966 CE: **Cultural Revolution** — Mao launches the Cultural Revolution, a decade of political persecution, purges, and social upheaval that convulses Chinese society until his death in 1976. [britannica-china, nationalarchives-cultural-rev]
- 1978 CE: **Deng Xiaoping launches reform and opening** — Under Deng Xiaoping, China begins 'reform and opening' in 1978, decollectivizing agriculture and embracing markets and foreign investment, igniting decades of rapid economic growth. [britannica-china, cfr-mao-deng]
- 1989 CE: **Tiananmen Square crackdown** — The army violently suppresses pro-democracy demonstrations centered on Beijing's Tiananmen Square in June 1989, killing many protesters and reasserting tight Communist Party political control. [britannica-china]

Figures:

- Mao Zedong (1893-1976) — Founding leader of the People's Republic. Chairman of the Chinese Communist Party who led the Communists to victory in 1949 and ruled until 1976. He unified the country under party control but his campaigns, the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution, caused tens of millions of deaths and vast suffering.
- Deng Xiaoping (1904-1997) — Architect of China's economic reform. Paramount leader who launched the 'reform and opening' policies from 1978, transforming China's economy while preserving one-party rule. His era lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty but also saw the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown.
