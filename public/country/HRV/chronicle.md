# The Chronicle of Croatia: From Roman Dalmatia to the Adriatic republic, a nation forged between empires, partitioned and reborn through war.

Founding: The Coronation of Tomislav, first king of the Croats — c. 925. By tradition the Croatian kingdom dates to about 925, when Tomislav, who had united the Croats of Dalmatia and Pannonia and held off the Magyars and Bulgars, was recognised as king (kralj) in letters of Pope John X. The native kingdom built on the earlier duchy whose ruler Branimir had won papal recognition in 879. Croatia kept its own crowned rulers until 1102, when, the male line of native kings having died out, the realm entered a personal union with the kings of Hungary that would last, in changing forms, until 1918. The modern Republic of Croatia traces its sovereignty to the declaration of independence of 25 June 1991 and the war that followed, with international recognition coming in 1992.

Croatia's history runs along the meeting point of empires, faiths and seas. Its lands were the Roman provinces of Dalmatia and Pannonia before Slavic Croats settled them in the early Middle Ages, and a native Croatian kingdom emerged whose ruler Tomislav was, by tradition, crowned the first king around 925. In 1102 the Croatian crown passed by personal union to the kings of Hungary, an association that endured for eight centuries, while the Dalmatian coast fell to Venice and the merchant city of Dubrovnik (Ragusa) thrived as an independent republic. The Ottoman wars carved away much of the land and gave rise to the Habsburg Military Frontier; the nineteenth century brought a national revival that imagined a community of South Slavs. The collapse of Austria-Hungary in 1918 drew Croatia into the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, later Yugoslavia, an unhappy union broken by the Second World War and the murderous fascist Independent State of Croatia. After four decades within socialist Yugoslavia, Croatia declared independence in 1991 and fought a war to secure it, winning international recognition in 1992 and full control of its territory by 1998. It joined NATO in 2009 and the European Union in 2013, adopting the euro and entering the Schengen area in 2023.

## Roman Dalmatia, Pannonia and the Coming of the Croats — c. 9 CE – 879

Roman provinces on the Adriatic gave way to Slavic settlement, from which a Croatian duchy emerged and won recognition from Rome.

- c. 9 CE: **Dalmatia and Pannonia become Roman provinces** — After the crushing of the great Illyrian (Pannonian) revolt, Rome organised the eastern Adriatic and its hinterland into the provinces of Dalmatia and Pannonia, beginning centuries of Roman rule and urban life along the coast. [croatia-history-britannica]
- c. 305: **Diocletian's Palace at Split** — The Dalmatian-born emperor Diocletian built an immense fortified palace near Salona around the turn of the fourth century; the historic core of Split later grew within its walls, today the most complete surviving Roman palace. [split-unesco, croatia-history-britannica]
- 7th century: **Slavic settlement and the coming of the Croats** — Avar and Slavic invasions destroyed the Roman provincial order, including the capital Salona; the Croats settled the former provinces of Dalmatia and Pannonia and gradually organised duchies of their own. [croatia-history-britannica, croatia-eu-state]
- 879: **Papal recognition of Duke Branimir** — Pope John VIII acknowledged the rule of Duke Branimir over the Croatian realm, aligning the Croats with the Latin Church of Rome and marking the emergence of Croatia as a recognised state. [croatia-eu-state, croatia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Diocletian (c. 244 – 311) — Roman emperor. Born in Dalmatia, Diocletian reorganised the Roman Empire and, on his abdication in 305, retired to the colossal palace he had built near Salona, around which the city of Split later grew.
- Branimir (reigned c. 879 – 892) — Duke of Croatia. The duke under whom the Dalmatian Croatian realm won papal recognition in 879 and aligned itself with Rome, a turning point conventionally treated as the recognition of an independent Croatian state.

## The Medieval Kingdom and the Union with Hungary — 879 – 1102

A native kingdom of the Croats rose under Tomislav, then passed by personal union to the crown of Hungary, an association that would shape eight centuries.

- c. 925: **Tomislav recognised as king of the Croats** — Having united the Croats of Dalmatia and Pannonia and held off the Magyars and Bulgars, Tomislav was addressed as king in letters of Pope John X and is regarded by tradition as the first king of a unified Croatia. [tomislav-britannica, croatia-eu-state]
- 1075: **Coronation of Demetrius Zvonimir** — Zvonimir was crowned king of Croatia with the sanction of the papacy, the last of the great native rulers and a symbol of the medieval kingdom at its height. [croatia-history-britannica]
- 1102: **Personal union with Hungary** — King Coloman of Hungary was crowned king of Croatia and Dalmatia, joining the crowns in a personal union; Croatian tradition records the terms in the Pacta conventa, under which Croatia kept its Sabor and its ban. [croatia-eu-state, croatia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Tomislav (reigned c. 910 – 928) — First king of the Croats (by tradition). Ruler who united the Croatian lands of Dalmatia and Pannonia and, around 925, was recognised as king in papal correspondence, traditionally honoured as the first king of a unified Croatian kingdom.
- Demetrius Zvonimir (reigned 1075 – 1089) — King of Croatia. Crowned with papal blessing in 1075, Zvonimir was the last of the powerful native kings; the failure of the royal line after his death opened the way to the union with Hungary.

## Venetian Dalmatia, the Republic of Ragusa and the Ottoman Wars — 1102 – 1797

While the coast fell to Venice and the merchant republic of Dubrovnik flourished, the Ottoman advance carved away the land and gave rise to the Habsburg Military Frontier.

- 1420: **Venice secures Dalmatia** — After long rivalry Venice took control of most of the Dalmatian coast, which it would govern for nearly four centuries until 1797, shaping the art, architecture and administration of the coastal cities. [croatia-history-britannica]
- 15th–16th c.: **The Republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik)** — Dubrovnik governed itself as an aristocratic merchant republic, paying tribute to the Ottoman sultan after 1458 while rivalling Venice in trade; its walled old city is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [dubrovnik-unesco, croatia-history-britannica]
- Venetian period: **The Dalmatian towns under Venice** — Coastal cities such as Trogir flourished under Venetian rule, acquiring fine Romanesque, Renaissance and Baroque buildings; the historic island town of Trogir is preserved as a UNESCO World Heritage Site of the Venetian period. [trogir-unesco, croatia-history-britannica]
- 1527: **Habsburgs elected to the Croatian throne** — Facing Ottoman conquest after the disaster at Mohacs in 1526, the Croatian nobility elected the Habsburg Ferdinand I as king, binding Croatia's defence to the Austrian monarchy. [croatia-history-britannica]
- 16th–18th c.: **The Military Frontier** — To hold the line against the Ottomans the Habsburgs created the Military Frontier, a militarised border zone settled by free soldier-peasants, including many Serb and Vlach refugees, who served in exchange for land and privileges. [croatia-history-britannica]
- 1667: **The Great Dubrovnik earthquake** — A devastating earthquake struck Dubrovnik in 1667, destroying much of the city, killing thousands and ending the long prosperity of the Ragusan republic. [dubrovnik-unesco]

Figures:

- Nikola Subic Zrinski (1508 – 1566) — Croatian ban and military commander. Ban of Croatia who died defending the fortress of Szigetvar against the army of Suleiman the Magnificent in 1566, becoming a celebrated symbol of Croatian resistance to the Ottoman advance.
- Marko Marulic (1450 – 1524) — Humanist and poet of Split. A Renaissance humanist of Split whose epic 'Judita' (1501) is regarded as the first work of art written in the Croatian language, earning him the title of father of Croatian literature.

## The National Revival and the End of Austria-Hungary — 1797 – 1918

A nineteenth-century movement of language and culture reawakened Croatian nationhood, even as the Croats remained divided among the lands of the Habsburg monarchy.

- 1809–1813: **Napoleon's Illyrian Provinces** — Napoleon gathered much of the coast and hinterland into the short-lived Illyrian Provinces, an episode that helped stir a sense of shared South Slav identity among the Croats. [croatia-history-britannica]
- 1830s–1840s: **The Illyrian movement and national revival** — Led by Ljudevit Gaj, intellectuals and clergy promoted a standard Croatian literary language and South Slav cultural unity, laying the foundations of the modern Croatian nation. [croatia-revival-britannica, croatia-history-britannica]
- 1847: **Croatian made the official language** — The Croatian Sabor adopted Croatian in place of Latin as the official language, a milestone of the national revival and a response to the imposition of Hungarian. [croatia-revival-britannica]
- 1868: **The Croatian-Hungarian Settlement (Nagodba)** — Within the Dual Monarchy created in 1867, the Nagodba granted Croatia-Slavonia limited autonomy under its own ban and Sabor, though real authority remained with Budapest. [croatia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Ljudevit Gaj (1809 – 1872) — Linguist and leader of the Illyrian movement. The central figure of the Croatian national revival, Gaj standardised the Croatian literary language and orthography and founded newspapers that carried the Illyrian idea of South Slav cultural unity.
- Josip Jelacic (1801 – 1859) — Ban of Croatia and military commander. Ban of Croatia from 1848, Jelacic led Croatian forces during the revolutions of 1848 and became a national symbol; his statue stands in the central square of Zagreb that bears his name.

## Yugoslavia, the Ustase Catastrophe and Socialist Croatia — 1918 – 1990

Drawn into a Yugoslav union after 1918, Croatia endured the murderous Ustase state in the Second World War before four decades within socialist Yugoslavia.

- 1 Dec 1918: **Entry into the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes** — After the collapse of Austria-Hungary, the Croatian lands joined Serbia and Montenegro in the new South Slav kingdom proclaimed on 1 December 1918, later renamed Yugoslavia. [kingdom-scs-britannica, croatia-history-britannica]
- 1941: **The Independent State of Croatia (NDH)** — Following the Axis invasion, Germany and Italy set up the Independent State of Croatia, a puppet state run by the fascist Ustase under Ante Pavelic, embracing Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina. [ndh-britannica, ustasa-britannica]
- 1941–1945: **The Jasenovac camp** — The Ustase regime operated its largest camp complex at Jasenovac, where it murdered an estimated 77,000 to 99,000 people, among them tens of thousands of Serbs, Jews and Roma, between 1941 and 1945. [jasenovac-ushmm, ustasa-britannica]
- 1945: **Croatia in socialist Yugoslavia** — The Partisan victory under Tito made Croatia one of the six republics of a federal socialist Yugoslavia, ending the Ustase state and beginning four decades of communist rule. [croatia-wwii-britannica, croatia-history-britannica]
- 1971: **The Croatian Spring** — Reformers and students in the 'Croatian Spring' demanded greater economic and constitutional autonomy; Tito crushed the movement and purged its leaders, though the 1974 constitution later widened republican rights. [croatia-history-britannica]

Figures:

- Josip Broz Tito (1892 – 1980) — Partisan leader and President of Yugoslavia. Born in Croatia, Tito led the communist Partisan resistance to victory in the Second World War and ruled socialist Yugoslavia until his death in 1980, holding its rival nationalities within a federal state.
- Ante Pavelic (1889 – 1959) — Leader (Poglavnik) of the Ustase state. Head of the fascist Ustase movement and dictator of the Independent State of Croatia from 1941 to 1945, responsible for a regime of terror and mass murder against Serbs, Jews, Roma and political opponents.

## Independence, the War and the Modern Republic — 1990 – present

Croatia broke from Yugoslavia and fought a war to secure its borders, then rebuilt a sovereign European state amid lingering disputes with its neighbours.

- 25 Jun 1991: **Declaration of independence** — The Croatian Sabor declared independence from Yugoslavia on 25 June 1991; rebel Serbs backed by the Yugoslav army seized nearly a third of the country and proclaimed the Republic of Serbian Krajina, igniting war. [tudjman-britannica, icty-conflicts]
- 18 Nov 1991: **The fall of Vukovar** — After a siege of some three months, Vukovar fell to the Yugoslav army and Serb forces on 18 November 1991; two days later 194 people taken from the hospital were killed at the Ovcara farm, a crime later prosecuted at the ICTY. [vukovar-irmct, icty-conflicts]
- 1992: **International recognition** — Croatia won international recognition as a sovereign state in 1992; the United States recognised it on 7 April 1992, and the country was admitted to the United Nations. [us-recognition-gov, icty-conflicts]
- Aug 1995: **Operation Storm** — In August 1995 the Croatian army launched Operation Storm, which within days overran the Republic of Serbian Krajina and ended its existence, accompanied by the flight of tens of thousands of Serbs; generals later tried at the ICTY were acquitted on appeal in 2012. [icty-conflicts, gotovina-icty]
- 29 Jun 2017: **Piran Bay arbitration award** — The Permanent Court of Arbitration issued its Final Award on the Croatia-Slovenia border in the Gulf of Piran; Croatia, which had withdrawn from the process in 2015, declared it did not consider itself bound by the ruling. [piran-arbitration-gov]
- 1979: **Plitvice Lakes inscribed by UNESCO** — Plitvice Lakes, Croatia's oldest national park, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979 for the ongoing formation of tufa barriers that dam its waters into a chain of lakes and waterfalls, the country's first World Heritage site. [plitvice-unesco]
- 1 Jul 2013: **Accession to the European Union** — Croatia joined the European Union on 1 July 2013 as its 28th member, after a 2012 referendum, having joined NATO in 2009; it adopted the euro and entered the Schengen area on 1 January 2023. [croatia-eu-mvep, croatia-eu-commission, croatia-euro-ecb]

Figures:

- Franjo Tudjman (1922 – 1999) — First President of independent Croatia. Leader of the Croatian Democratic Union and president from 1990, Tudjman led Croatia to independence and through the war that secured it, a dominant and divisive figure in the founding of the modern state.
- Ante Gotovina (born 1955) — Croatian army general. Commander in Operation Storm in 1995, Gotovina was indicted and convicted by the ICTY but acquitted on appeal in November 2012; he is regarded by many Croats as a national hero.
