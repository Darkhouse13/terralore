# The Chronicle of Guinea: From the heartland of the Mande to the bauxite republic

History of Guinea: From the heartland of the Mande to the bauxite republic. 6 eras and 25 sourced events, every claim traceable to a named reference.

Canonical: https://terralore.co/country/GIN/chronicle
Updated: 2026-06-13

Founding: Independence from France — 2 October 1958. After Guinea alone among France's African colonies voted 'Non' in the referendum of 28 September 1958, rejecting membership of de Gaulle's French Community, France abruptly withdrew its aid and personnel and Guinea proclaimed itself a sovereign republic on 2 October 1958, with Ahmed Sékou Touré as its first president.

On the upper Niger in eastern Guinea lay the cradle of the Mande peoples and of the medieval Mali Empire, and the country's highlands and savannas fell in turn within the reach of the great Sahelian states. In the eighteenth century Fulani reformers built the Islamic Imamate of Futa Jallon, and in the nineteenth Samori Touré forged the Wassoulou Empire and waged a long armed resistance to French conquest until his capture in 1898. As French Guinea within French West Africa, the territory stood apart in 1958 when, alone among France's African colonies, it voted 'Non' to Charles de Gaulle's proposed Community and became independent on 2 October 1958 under Ahmed Sékou Touré. Decades of authoritarian one-party rule, the Camp Boiro repression, military coups in 1984, 2008 and 2021, and the 2009 stadium massacre have marked its independent history, even as Guinea holds some of the world's largest bauxite reserves.

## The Mande Heartland and the Sahelian Empires — c. 13th–16th century CE

On the upper Niger lies the cradle of the Mande peoples and of the Mali Empire, drawn in turn into the reach of the great Sahelian states.

- c. 1235: **Foundation of the Mali Empire on the upper Niger** — Sundiata Keita unites the Malinké chiefdoms of the Manding heartland and founds the Mali Empire, with its capital at Niani on the upper Niger. [britannica-guinea, ebsco-mali, worldhist-songhai]
- 1324: **Mansa Musa's pilgrimage makes Mali's gold legendary** — Under Mansa Musa, the Mali Empire — embracing the Mande lands of the upper Niger — reaches its height and becomes renowned abroad for the wealth of its rulers. [ebsco-mali, britannica-guinea]
- 1493: **Songhai ascendancy in the western Sudan** — Askia Muhammad seizes power and expands the Songhai Empire, which dominates much of the Niger valley and the western Sudan into the sixteenth century. [worldhist-songhai, ebsco-songhai]
- 1591: **Fall of Songhai to a Moroccan army** — A Moroccan expeditionary force defeats the Songhai at Tondibi, ending the era of the great Sahelian empires that had embraced the Guinea region. [worldhist-songhai, ebsco-songhai]

## The Imamate of Futa Jallon — 1725 – 19th century

In the highlands of central Guinea, Fulani reformers build one of West Africa's first Islamic theocratic states.

- c. 1725: **Fulani jihad founds the Imamate of Futa Jallon** — Fulani pastoralists and Muslim traders rise against the non-Muslim chiefdoms of the Futa Jallon highlands, founding an Islamic theocratic state governed by sharia. [wiki-futajallon, wiki-karamokho, britannica-guinea]
- c. 1751: **Ibrahima Sori succeeds Karamokho Alfa** — On the death of the first leader Karamokho Alfa, the military commander Ibrahima Sori takes power and consolidates the imamate as a regional force. [wiki-futajallon, wiki-karamokho]
- 1896: **French annexation of Futa Jallon** — After decades of nominal autonomy, the imamate is brought under French colonial control as European expansion absorbs the Guinea interior. [wiki-futajallon, wiki-frenchguinea]

Figures:

- Karamokho Alfa (d. 1751) — First almami of Futa Jallon. Fulani religious leader who led the jihad founding the Imamate of Futa Jallon and was recognised as its first ruler and Commander of the Faithful.

## Samori Touré and the Resistance to France — 1878 – 1898

Samori Touré forges the Wassoulou Empire and wages one of Africa's longest armed resistances to French conquest.

- 1878: **Samori proclaims the Wassoulou Empire** — Samori Touré declares himself faama of the Wassoulou Empire, the state he has built in the savannas of present-day Upper Guinea and beyond. [wiki-samori, britannica-guinea]
- 1881: **Capture of Kankan** — Samori takes control of the major Dyula trading centre of Kankan on the upper Milo, a hub of the kola-nut trade. [britannica-guinea, wiki-samori]
- July 1884: **Islam proclaimed state religion; Samori takes the title almami** — Samori formally adopts Islam as the religion of his empire and assumes the title of almami, fusing political and religious authority. [wiki-samori]
- 1886–1887: **Treaties with France** — Samori signs agreements with France, including the Treaty of Bissandougou in 1887, as he manoeuvres between negotiation and war against colonial expansion. [wiki-samori]
- 29 September 1898: **Capture of Samori Touré** — After nearly two decades of resistance and a long fighting retreat eastward, Samori is captured by French forces at Guélémou and later exiled to Gabon, where he dies in 1900. [wiki-samori, britannica-guinea]

Figures:

- Samori Touré (c. 1830–1900) — Founder of the Wassoulou Empire; resistance leader. Muslim military leader who built the Wassoulou Empire in Upper Guinea and waged one of Africa's longest armed resistances to French colonial conquest before his capture in 1898.

## French Guinea — 1891 – 1958

The conquered territory becomes a colony of French West Africa, with its capital at Conakry.

- 1884: **Founding of Conakry** — The French establish Conakry on the Atlantic coast; it becomes the capital of the colony of French Guinea. [wiki-frenchguinea, britannica-conakry]
- 1891–1894: **Colony of French Guinea established** — The coastal territory of Rivières du Sud is reorganised into the colony of French Guinea, its borders extended over the conquered interior. [wiki-frenchguinea]
- 1895: **Incorporation into French West Africa** — French Guinea becomes a constituent colony of the federation of French West Africa, administered from Dakar. [wiki-frenchguinea]
- c. 1947: **Rise of the Parti Démocratique de Guinée** — Postwar political mobilisation gives rise to the PDG, which under Ahmed Sékou Touré becomes the leading nationalist party in the colony. [britannica-guinea, ebsco-independence]

## The 'Non' of 1958 and the Sékou Touré Years — 1958 – 1984

Guinea alone votes 'Non' to de Gaulle's Community and wins independence, then endures decades of authoritarian one-party rule.

- 28 September 1958: **Guinea votes 'Non'** — In the constitutional referendum, Guinea overwhelmingly rejects de Gaulle's French Community — the only French African colony to do so. [ebsco-independence, wiki-frenchguinea, britannica-guinea]
- 2 October 1958: **Independence proclaimed** — Days after France abruptly withdraws its aid and personnel, Guinea proclaims itself a sovereign republic with Ahmed Sékou Touré as president. [ebsco-independence, britannica-guinea]
- 1960–1984: **Camp Boiro and the one-party state** — Under a one-party socialist regime, the Camp Boiro detention centre becomes the centre of a repression in which thousands of detainees are executed or die from torture and starvation. [wiki-campboiro, justiceinfo-boiro]
- 26 March 1984: **Death of Sékou Touré** — President Sékou Touré dies while receiving medical treatment abroad, ending more than a quarter-century of one-party rule. [wiki-1984coup, britannica-guinea]

Figures:

- Ahmed Sékou Touré (1922–1984) — First President of Guinea. Trade unionist and PDG leader who led Guinea's 'No' to de Gaulle in 1958 and its independence, then ruled as authoritarian head of a one-party socialist state until his death in 1984.

## Coups, Civilian Rule and Bauxite — 1984 – present

Military rule, a brief civilian opening and renewed coups unfold over a country holding some of the world's largest bauxite reserves.

- 3 April 1984: **Lansana Conté seizes power** — A bloodless military coup led by Colonel Lansana Conté takes control after Sékou Touré's death; political prisoners at Camp Boiro are released. [wiki-1984coup, britannica-guinea]
- December 2008: **Camara's coup after Conté's death** — On the death of long-ruling President Lansana Conté, Captain Moussa Dadis Camara leads a military junta to power. [britannica-guinea, globalsecurity-massacre]
- 28 September 2009: **Conakry stadium massacre** — Security forces fire on a peaceful opposition rally in a Conakry stadium, killing at least 157 people and committing widespread sexual violence. [hrw-massacre, globalsecurity-massacre]
- 2010: **Alpha Condé elected; return to civilian rule** — Veteran opposition leader Alpha Condé wins Guinea's first competitive presidential election, beginning a civilian government; he is re-elected in 2015 and 2020. [wiki-conde, britannica-guinea]
- 5 September 2021: **Doumbouya's coup ousts Condé** — Special-forces commander Mamady Doumbouya overthrows President Alpha Condé, dissolves the constitution and installs himself as transitional leader. [wiki-2021coup, wiki-doumbouya]

Figures:

- Alpha Condé (b. 1938) — President of Guinea (2010–2021). Long-time opposition leader who became Guinea's first competitively elected president in 2010; re-elected in 2015 and 2020, he was overthrown in the 2021 coup.
