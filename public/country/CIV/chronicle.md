Founding: Independence from France — 7 August 1960. On 7 August 1960 Côte d'Ivoire became a fully independent republic, ending more than six decades of French colonial rule and a brief period as an autonomous republic within the French Community. Its first president was Félix Houphouët-Boigny, a wealthy Baoulé planter and physician who had risen through French colonial politics, founded the African Agricultural Union (Syndicat Agricole Africain), and helped abolish forced labour in the French Empire in 1946. Unlike many of his contemporaries, Houphouët-Boigny had favoured a continued close association with France, and he kept Abidjan firmly within the French orbit after independence. The new state was already the most prosperous in French West Africa, its economy built on cocoa and coffee, and it entered nationhood with the relative stability that would underpin the so-called 'Ivorian miracle' of the following two decades. Houphouët-Boigny would rule, unopposed for most of that time, until his death in 1993.

Côte d'Ivoire takes its European name from the ivory once shipped from its coast. Long before that trade, its northern savannas were drawn into the trans-Saharan commerce of the Ghana and Mali empires, and Mandé merchants known as the Dyula built the trading city of Kong into the heart of an empire founded about 1710 that grew rich on gold and kola nuts. In the mid-eighteenth century the Baoulé arrived from the east, led by tradition under Queen Abla Pokou, who broke from the Asante; other Akan refugees founded the Agni kingdoms, while Senufo and Mandé peoples held the north. France made the territory a colony in 1893, crushing the resistance of Samori Touré by 1898, and bound it into French West Africa as a forced-labour economy producing cocoa and coffee. The planter Félix Houphouët-Boigny led the campaign that abolished forced labour in 1946 and brought the country to independence on 7 August 1960. Decades of one-party stability and rapid growth — the 'Ivorian miracle' — gave way after his death in 1993 to disputes over the doctrine of 'Ivoirité', a civil war from 2002, and a violent post-election crisis in 2010–2011, before a fragile recovery under Alassane Ouattara.

## The northern savannas, the Dyula, and the Empire of Kong — c. 1300 – 1750

Drawn into the trans-Saharan trade of the great Sahelian empires, the northern savannas became home to Senufo farmers and to Mandé merchants — the Dyula — whose town of Kong grew into the seat of an empire trading gold and kola.

- 11th–14th centuries: **The savannas join the trans-Saharan trade** — As the Ghana and Mali empires expanded southward, the northern peoples of the region — Senufo and Mandé — were drawn into trans-Saharan commerce, exchanging forest kola nuts for salt, cloth, and gold. [britannica-civ-history, wiki-civ-history, state-bgn-civ]
- 14th century onward: **Dyula merchants found northern trading towns** — Mandé-speaking Muslim merchants, the Dyula, established trading and Islamic centres such as Kong, Bondoukou, Bouna, and Begho, linking the rainforest to the markets of the Sahel. [wiki-civ-history, wiki-kong-empire, britannica-korhogo]
- c. 1710: **Seku Watara founds the Empire of Kong** — Seku Watara united the Dyula of Kong into a state, often called the Wattara Empire, that grew rich controlling the gold and kola trade and reached into present-day Burkina Faso, Mali, and Ghana. [wiki-kong-empire, wiki-civ-history]
- 1737: **Kong expands northward** — Under Seku Watara and his kin the empire of Kong extended its reach, conquering toward Bobo-Dioulasso and cementing its dominance over the regional trade routes before central authority weakened after his death about 1745. [wiki-kong-empire]

## The Akan migrations: Baoulé and Agni kingdoms — c. 1690 – 1840

In the mid-eighteenth century waves of Akan peoples fled the rising Asante Empire to the east, founding the Baoulé kingdom under the legendary Queen Abla Pokou and the Agni kingdoms of Indénié and Sanwi.

- c. 1690: **Akan states form on the eastern frontier** — As the Asante Empire rose in present-day Ghana, Akan groups such as the Abron founded states near the eastern edge of modern Côte d'Ivoire, beginning a long era of Akan migration into the region. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]
- c. 1750: **The Baoulé migrate under Queen Abla Pokou** — By tradition, Queen Abla Pokou broke from the Asante after a succession struggle and led her people west across the Comoé River, founding the Baoulé kingdom around Sakasso — the largest people of modern Côte d'Ivoire. [wiki-queen-pokou, wiki-baoule, wiki-civ-history]
- mid-18th century: **The Agni kingdoms of Indénié and Sanwi** — Other Akan groups fleeing the Asante established the Agni (Anyi) kingdoms of Indénié and Sanwi in the south-east, near the lagoons and the Gold Coast, extending Akan institutions across the forest zone. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]

Figures:

- Abla Pokou (reigned c. 1750 – c. 1760) — Queen and founder of the Baoulé. By tradition a kinswoman of the Asante royal house, Pokou led her followers west into modern Côte d'Ivoire after a succession dispute. The legend that she sacrificed her only son to cross the Comoé River — crying 'Ba-ouli', 'the child is dead' — gives the Baoulé their name.

## The ivory coast, the French, and the conquest — 1483 – 1898

European traders worked the coast for ivory and other goods from the fifteenth century; in the nineteenth, France turned trade into protectorates and a colony, declared in 1893 and secured by the defeat of Samori Touré in 1898.

- 1483: **Earliest recorded French voyage to the coast** — French mariners reached the West African coast; over the following centuries European traders worked the shore for ivory, gold, and other goods, giving the Ivory Coast its lasting name, though its surf-bound coast limited permanent contact. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]
- 1843–1844: **France signs treaties on the lagoon coast** — The naval officer Bouët-Willaumez concluded treaties with rulers around Grand-Bassam and Assinie, securing the first durable French trading footholds on the coast of present-day Côte d'Ivoire. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]
- 10 March 1893: **France constitutes the colony of Côte d'Ivoire** — After protectorate treaties negotiated by Binger and Treich-Laplène, France formally established the colony of Côte d'Ivoire on 10 March 1893, naming Louis-Gustave Binger its first governor. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]
- 29 September 1898: **Capture of Samori Touré ends northern resistance** — The Mandé empire-builder Samori Touré, who had resisted the French for years and sacked Kong, was captured at Guélémou on 29 September 1898 and exiled to Gabon, breaking organised resistance in the savanna. [blackpast-samori, britannica-samory, wiki-civ-history]

Figures:

- Samori Touré (c. 1830 – 1900) — Mandé empire-builder and resistance leader. From 1878 Samori forged a powerful Muslim empire across present-day Guinea, Mali, and northern Côte d'Ivoire, and resisted French conquest for more than a decade with scorched-earth warfare. Captured in 1898 and exiled to Gabon, he became an enduring symbol of African resistance to colonialism.

## Colony, forced labour, and the road to independence — 1893 – 1960

Within French West Africa, Côte d'Ivoire became a cash-crop colony built on forced labour and head taxes — until the planter Félix Houphouët-Boigny led the campaign that abolished forced labour in 1946 and brought the colony to independence in 1960.

- 1904: **Côte d'Ivoire joins French West Africa** — The colony became a constituent territory of the Federation of French West Africa, governed from Dakar, and was developed as a source of cocoa, coffee, and timber for the metropole. [wiki-civ-history, state-bgn-civ]
- from 1900: **Head tax and forced labour** — France imposed a head tax in 1900 and a forced-labour regime that compelled African men to work without pay on infrastructure and plantations, drawing recruits from the lands later organised as Upper Volta and provoking repeated revolts. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-civ-history]
- 1944: **Houphouët-Boigny founds the African Agricultural Union** — Félix Houphouët-Boigny organised African cocoa and coffee planters into the Syndicat Agricole Africain to win them the rights enjoyed by European growers — the start of his rise to political leadership. [britannica-civ-history, wiki-houphouet, encafricana-houphouet]
- 1946: **Forced labour abolished** — On a bill closely associated with Houphouët-Boigny, the French parliament abolished forced labour throughout the empire in 1946; the same year he helped found the inter-territorial RDA and its Ivorian party, the PDCI. [britannica-civ-history, wiki-houphouet, encafricana-houphouet]
- 7 August 1960: **Independence under Houphouët-Boigny** — After choosing autonomy within the French Community in a 1958 referendum, Côte d'Ivoire became a fully independent republic on 7 August 1960, with Félix Houphouët-Boigny as its first president. [sahistory-civ-independence, britannica-civ-history, wiki-civ-history]

## The Ivorian miracle and the long Houphouët-Boigny era — 1960 – 1993

For three decades Houphouët-Boigny ruled a one-party state whose cocoa-and-coffee boom made it one of Africa's most prosperous nations — a stability crowned, and strained, by grand projects such as the vast basilica at Yamoussoukro.

- 1960s–1970s: **The 'Ivorian miracle'** — Liberal, export-led growth built on cocoa and coffee and close ties to France made Côte d'Ivoire one of the most prosperous states in sub-Saharan Africa, with Abidjan a thriving commercial hub. [britannica-civ-history, wiki-civ-1960-99, state-bgn-civ]
- early 1980s: **Commodity slump ends the boom** — A collapse in world cocoa and coffee prices ended the era of rapid growth, leaving heavy debt and rising discontent that would strain the one-party system. [wiki-civ-1960-99, britannica-civ-history]
- 1983: **Capital moved to Yamoussoukro** — Houphouët-Boigny designated his birthplace, Yamoussoukro, the official political capital, though Abidjan remained the economic and administrative centre. [wiki-civ-history, britannica-basilica]
- 10 September 1990: **Basilica of Our Lady of Peace consecrated** — The vast basilica at Yamoussoukro, modelled on St Peter's in Rome and recognised as the largest church in the world, was consecrated by Pope John Paul II amid controversy over its enormous cost. [britannica-basilica, blackpast-basilica]
- October 1990: **First multiparty election** — Under domestic and international pressure Houphouët-Boigny legalised opposition parties and won the country's first multiparty presidential election against the historian Laurent Gbagbo. [wiki-civ-1960-99, britannica-civ-history]
- 7 December 1993: **Death of Houphouët-Boigny** — The country's founding president died in office after more than three decades in power, leaving a celebrated record of stability and growth but no clearly prepared successor. [wiki-houphouet, britannica-houphouet, qiraat-houphouet]

Figures:

- Félix Houphouët-Boigny (c. 1905 – 1993) — First President of Côte d'Ivoire. A Baoulé planter and physician who organised African growers, helped abolish forced labour in 1946, and led the country to independence in 1960. He ruled for over three decades, presiding over the 'Ivorian miracle' and building the basilica at Yamoussoukro, while keeping close ties to France.

## Ivoirité, civil war, and recovery — 1993 – present

The succession to Houphouët-Boigny unleashed the divisive politics of 'Ivoirité', a 1999 coup, civil war from 2002, and a violent post-election crisis in 2010–2011 — before a fragile recovery under Alassane Ouattara.

- 1990s: **The politics of 'Ivoirité'** — President Bédié promoted 'Ivoirité', a doctrine of authentic Ivorian belonging used to exclude rivals such as Alassane Ouattara and to question the citizenship of northerners and immigrants, deepening a north–south rift. [wiki-civ-history, everycrs-civ, eisa-civ-crisis]
- 24 December 1999: **The first coup d'état** — General Robert Guéï overthrew President Henri Konan Bédié in the country's first military coup, ending the PDCI's unbroken hold on power since independence. [wiki-civ-history, everycrs-civ]
- 19 September 2002: **Civil war divides the country** — An army mutiny grew into a rebellion that split Côte d'Ivoire between a government-held south and a rebel-held north under the Forces Nouvelles, drawing in French and UN peacekeepers. [wiki-civ-history, everycrs-civ, wiki-2010-11-crisis]
- 2007: **Ouagadougou Political Agreement** — A peace accord brokered through regional mediation brought rebel leader Guillaume Soro into a unity government and set a path toward reunification and elections, easing the first civil war. [everycrs-civ, wiki-civ-history]
- 2010–2011: **Post-election crisis** — After the disputed November 2010 runoff, Gbagbo refused to cede to Ouattara; the standoff became open conflict that killed about 3,000 people before pro-Ouattara forces, backed by France and the UN, captured Gbagbo on 11 April 2011. [wiki-2010-11-crisis, everycrs-civ, eisa-civ-crisis]
- since 2011: **Recovery under Ouattara** — Alassane Ouattara took office and presided over a return to rapid growth; he was re-elected in 2015 and won a contested third term in 2020 that opponents argued was unconstitutional. [wiki-civ-history, everycrs-civ]

Figures:

- Laurent Gbagbo (born 1945) — President of Côte d'Ivoire, 2000–2011. A historian and long-time opposition leader who became president in 2000. His refusal to concede the 2010 election to Ouattara triggered the post-election crisis; captured in 2011, he was later tried and acquitted at the International Criminal Court.
- Alassane Ouattara (born 1942) — President of Côte d'Ivoire since 2011. A northern Muslim economist and former prime minister long excluded from power by the politics of 'Ivoirité'. Recognised as winner of the 2010 election, he took office after Gbagbo's capture in 2011 and oversaw economic recovery, winning further terms in 2015 and 2020.
