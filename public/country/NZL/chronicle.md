Founding: The Treaty of Waitangi — 6 February 1840. New Zealand's founding moment is the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi (Te Tiriti o Waitangi) on 6 February 1840 at Waitangi in the Bay of Islands, where the British Crown's representative Captain William Hobson and a group of Māori rangatira (chiefs) signed an agreement that sheets carried around the country would eventually bring more than 500 signatories. The Treaty established a basis for British authority in New Zealand while guaranteeing Māori the possession of their lands and properties. Crucially, its English and Māori texts are not exact translations of one another: where the English text has Māori cede 'sovereignty', the Māori text speaks of granting kāwanatanga (governorship) while retaining tino rangatiratanga (full chieftainship). That difference, and the meaning of the Treaty as a whole, has been contested from 1840 to the present and lies at the heart of New Zealand's constitutional life.

New Zealand — Aotearoa — was among the last large landmasses on Earth to be settled, reached by Polynesian voyagers around 1300 CE whose descendants developed the distinct Māori world of iwi and hapū. European contact came with Abel Tasman's 1642 sighting and James Cook's accurate charting in 1769–70, followed by missionaries, traders, and the muskets that transformed inter-tribal warfare. On 6 February 1840 the British Crown and Māori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi, the nation's founding document, whose English and Māori texts differ in meaning and whose interpretation has been contested ever since. Through the New Zealand Wars and land confiscation, the world-first grant of women's suffrage in 1893, Dominion status, the welfare state, and a post-1970s Māori renaissance, New Zealand became a prosperous bicultural democracy still working through the Treaty's unfinished promises.

## The Polynesian voyage and the making of the Māori world — c. 1300 – 1642 CE

Around 1300 CE, Polynesian voyagers in great ocean-going canoes reached Aotearoa, the last large temperate landmass to be settled, and over the following centuries developed the distinct Māori world of iwi, hapū, and migration traditions.

- c. 1300 CE: **Polynesian voyagers settle Aotearoa** — The ancestors of the Māori reached New Zealand from East Polynesia in great voyaging canoes around 1300 CE, making it one of the last large landmasses on Earth to be permanently settled. [nzh-voyaging, teara-arrival, brit-nz]
- 14th–15th centuries: **The Māori world takes shape** — Settlers adapted to a temperate land and developed the distinct Māori culture of iwi and hapū, organised by whakapapa and rangatira and expressed through carving, weaving, and the working of pounamu. [teara-arrival, brit-nz]
- c. 1300–1550: **Settlement of the Chatham Islands** — Māori reached the remote Chatham Islands (Rēkohu) over 750 km east of the mainland, where the settlers became the distinct Moriori people with their own traditions. [nzh-voyaging, teara-arrival]

## European contact, missionaries, and the musket wars — 1642 – 1840

Abel Tasman's fleeting 1642 sighting and James Cook's accurate charting in 1769 opened Aotearoa to traders and missionaries, while the spread of muskets unleashed a generation of devastating inter-tribal warfare.

- December 1642: **Abel Tasman sights New Zealand** — The Dutch navigator Abel Tasman became the first European to sight New Zealand, charting a fragment of the west coast but leaving without landing after a deadly clash with Māori in Golden Bay. [nzh-european, teara-european, brit-nz]
- 6 October 1769: **James Cook charts the islands** — James Cook sighted New Zealand and over 175 days circumnavigated and accurately charted both main islands aboard HMB Endeavour, fixing the country's outline for European maps. [nzh-european, teara-european, brit-nz]
- 25 December 1814: **First Christian mission** — Samuel Marsden conducted New Zealand's first Christian service at Rangihoua in the Bay of Islands, beginning the missionary presence that spread Christianity, literacy, and new ideas among Māori. [nzh-marsden, teara-marsden]
- c. 1818–1840: **The musket wars** — The spread of firearms unleashed a generation of devastating inter-tribal warfare, with leaders such as Te Rauparaha driving migrations and conquests that killed tens of thousands and reshaped the tribal map. [teara-musket, nzh-terauparaha]

Figures:

- James Cook (1728–1779) — Royal Navy lieutenant and explorer. Charted both main islands of New Zealand with great accuracy during his first Pacific voyage in 1769–70, fixing the country's outline for European maps.
- Te Rauparaha (c. 1768–1849) — Rangatira of Ngāti Toa. A formidable war leader of the musket-wars era who led Ngāti Toa's migration south to Kāpiti and conquests across Cook Strait, earning the epithet 'the Napoleon of the South'.

## The Treaty of Waitangi, the New Zealand Wars, and confiscation — 1840 – 1872

The Treaty of Waitangi of 1840 founded British New Zealand on an agreement whose two texts differed in meaning; within two decades, disputes over land and authority erupted into the New Zealand Wars and large-scale confiscation of Māori land.

- 6 February 1840: **Treaty of Waitangi signed** — The Crown and Māori rangatira signed the Treaty of Waitangi, founding British New Zealand; its differing English and Māori texts — over sovereignty versus governorship — left a meaning contested to this day. [nzh-treaty-differences, teara-tiriti, brit-nz]
- 1860–1872: **The New Zealand Wars** — Disputes over land and sovereignty erupted into war in Taranaki in 1860 and spread across the North Island, pitting imperial and colonial forces against iwi defending their land and autonomy until about 1872. [brit-nz-wars, teara-nz-wars]
- 1863: **Land confiscation legalised (raupatu)** — The New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 gave a legal cloak to the confiscation of about 1.2 million hectares of Māori land, chiefly in the Waikato and Taranaki, often taking land from iwi who had not fought the Crown. [nzh-raupatu, teara-nz-wars]

Figures:

- William Hobson (1792–1842) — First Governor of New Zealand. As the Crown's representative he drafted and secured the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840 and became New Zealand's first governor, establishing British authority over the new colony.

## Colony, women's suffrage, and Dominion — 1872 – 1914

As the settler colony prospered and built parliamentary self-government, New Zealand made history in 1893 as the first self-governing nation to grant women the vote, and in 1907 it became a Dominion within the British Empire.

- 1882: **First refrigerated meat shipment** — The first cargo of frozen meat sailed from New Zealand to Britain, launching the refrigerated export trade in meat and dairy that would underpin the country's economy for a century. [brit-nz]
- 19 September 1893: **Women win the vote** — New Zealand became the first self-governing nation in the world to grant women the parliamentary vote, the culmination of a petition campaign led by Kate Sheppard and the temperance movement. [nzh-suffrage, brit-sheppard]
- 26 September 1907: **New Zealand becomes a Dominion** — New Zealand's status was raised from colony to Dominion within the British Empire, a symbolic advance toward nationhood that left its close ties to Britain largely intact. [nzh-statute, brit-nz]

Figures:

- Kate Sheppard (1847–1934) — Suffrage campaigner. Led the campaign that made New Zealand the first self-governing nation to grant women the vote in 1893, and became founding president of the National Council of Women.

## Nationhood, the world wars, and the welfare state — 1914 – 1970

Two world wars deepened New Zealand's sense of nationhood even as it moved toward full legal independence, while the Labour government of 1935 built one of the world's most comprehensive welfare states.

- 25 April 1915: **Gallipoli and the Anzac legend** — New Zealand and Australian troops landed at Gallipoli, a costly campaign that, despite ending in withdrawal, became a foundational national story commemorated each year on Anzac Day. [brit-nz]
- 1938: **The welfare state** — The first Labour government's Social Security Act 1938 created a comprehensive system of pensions, benefits, and public health, building a 'cradle to grave' welfare state admired around the world. [teara-social-security, brit-nz]
- 25 November 1947: **Statute of Westminster adopted** — New Zealand adopted the Statute of Westminster, ending the British Parliament's power to legislate for it and confirming the country's full legal independence — the last Dominion to do so. [nzh-statute, brit-nz]

Figures:

- Apirana Ngata (1874–1950) — Māori leader and Member of Parliament. The first Māori to complete a New Zealand university degree; as MP for Eastern Māori (1905–43) he championed Māori land development and a renaissance of Māori arts and culture.

## The Māori renaissance and bicultural New Zealand — 1970 – present

From the 1970s a Māori cultural and legal renaissance reshaped the nation: the Waitangi Tribunal, Treaty settlements, and the revival of te reo Māori turned New Zealand toward an explicitly bicultural identity by the present day.

- 1975: **Waitangi Tribunal established** — The Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 created the Waitangi Tribunal to investigate Crown breaches of the Treaty's principles; in 1985 its mandate was extended back to 1840, enabling redress for historic wrongs. [teara-tribunal, nzh-treaty-differences]
- 1987: **Te reo Māori made official** — The Māori Language Act 1987 recognised te reo Māori as an official language and created a commission to promote it, a landmark in the post-1970s Māori cultural renaissance. [teara-tereo, teara-tribunal]
- 1995: **Waikato-Tainui settlement and Crown apology** — The Crown signed a Deed of Settlement with Waikato-Tainui including a formal royal apology for the wars and confiscations of the 1860s, a model for later Treaty settlements such as Ngāi Tahu in 1998. [teara-settlements, nzh-raupatu]
- 1990 / 1993: **UNESCO World Heritage recognition** — Tongariro National Park — inscribed for its dual natural and Māori cultural significance — and Te Wahipounamu in the south were recognised by UNESCO, honouring both landscape and Māori heritage. [unesco-tongariro, unesco-tewahipounamu]
