## Entangled histories (6 events)

Events in which either nation's sourced chronicle names the other — including under earlier names of the same state. Each is the record exactly as its own chronicle carries it, with that chronicle's sources.

- 1219: **Conquest of the Khwarazmian empire** — Mongol armies invade and destroy the powerful Khwarazmian empire of Central Asia, opening the way to Persia and the Russian steppe and demonstrating the reach of the new Mongol military. (from the Mongolia chronicle; sources: Genghis Khan; Mongol empire)
- 1237-1240: **Mongol invasion and the fall of Kiev** — Batu Khan's Mongol army sacks the Rus' principalities, destroying Ryazan and Vladimir and storming Kiev in December 1240; the Russian lands become tributaries of the Golden Horde. (from the Russia chronicle; sources: Russia; Batu Khan)
- 1480: **The Great Stand on the Ugra ends the Mongol yoke** — Ivan III, having stopped paying tribute, faces down the Great Horde at the Ugra River; the bloodless standoff is traditionally regarded as the end of Mongol overlordship and the rise of a sovereign Muscovite state. (from the Russia chronicle; sources: Russia)
- 1921: **People's Revolution captures the capital** — Damdin Sükhbaatar's People's Army, with the Soviet Red Army, drives out the White Russian forces of Baron Ungern-Sternberg and takes Niislel Khüree in July 1921, installing a people's government. (from the Mongolia chronicle; sources: Damdiny Sühbaatar; Mongolia: Independence and revolution)
- 1924: **Mongolian People's Republic proclaimed** — After the death of the Bogd Khan in May 1924, the monarchy lapses and the Mongolian People's Republic is proclaimed in November, the world's second socialist state and a satellite of the Soviet Union. (from the Mongolia chronicle; sources: Mongolia: Independence and revolution; Bogd Gegeen Khan)
- 1937: **The Great Repression** — Under Choibalsan and Soviet NKVD advisers, the state carries out mass purges, killing tens of thousands of people, executing thousands of lamas, and destroying hundreds of Buddhist monasteries. (from the Mongolia chronicle; sources: Mongolia: Counterrevolution and Japan; Mongolia: A Country Study)
